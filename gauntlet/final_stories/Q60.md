# Q60 - tell me about a time you encountered a technical bottleneck in a prototype

**LP:** Dive Deep (primary); Bias for Action; Deliver Results   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the non-rigid alignment bottleneck in the first working comparison prototype (STORY 2, canonical alignment telling; bottleneck/unblock lens, distinct from Q20's deep-dive telling, Q29's WASM blocker, Q48's training blocker; STORY 12/13 excluded).

## Core Answer

The first working version of my PCB-versus-layout comparison prototype hit a bottleneck that made everything else in it irrelevant: alignment - lining the photo of the board up with its digital design drawing so both sit in the same place, at the same scale and angle. A PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device; the layout is its CAD design file. On my own clean test images it lined a board photo up with the CAD layout well enough to compare. On the first real factory photos it didn't.

Why that was a bottleneck and not just a bug: the comparison runs on a 10-pixel mismatch threshold - a pixel is one dot on screen, and the tool calls a spot a mismatch when photo and drawing disagree by more than 10 of them. If photo and layout sit more than a few pixels apart, every mismatch it flags is noise: false alarms caused by bad alignment, not real differences. The tool existed to compare positions, and the positions didn't line up - anything else I polished was fake progress.

I could have started tweaking the detector - the part that circles the differences. Instead I asked which layer the error lived in, and it lived in two. The first was my transform model - the mathematical rule for shifting, rotating, and bending one image onto the other. I'd started with an affine transform - it keeps parallel lines parallel, so it can't reproduce the look of a photo taken at an angle. Photograph a board at an angle and parallel edges converge, so no affine can undo that. I switched to a homography fit with RANSAC - a transform that does model camera angle, fitted by voting out bad feature matches. The global error went away, but real boards still averaged 15-20 pixels of misalignment - matching points still sat that many screen dots apart on average - which told me there was a second layer.

That one was physical. Boards pass through a reflow oven - the oven that melts the solder attaching the parts - and the heat bows them unevenly, the center differently from the edges. A global transform, one shift-and-tilt rule for the whole picture, structurally can't fix a local bow, so the prototype needed a stage it didn't have. I compared non-rigid methods - ones that let different parts of the board bend differently - and picked thin-plate splines on top of the homography: the bend that flexes the image the way a thin plate bends, deterministic, no physical constants with no clear meaning for a board. Control points - the spots the bend is pinned to - came from template-matching known features, locating on the photo the spots the design drawing already marks: corners, fiducials (small reference dots placed on the board on purpose), pads (where a part's legs solder down) - and I rejected weak matches, because one bad point drags the whole warp the wrong way.

Then I made it measurable instead of trusting my eyes: a small ground-truth test on real board photos - photos whose correct positions I checked by hand, so accuracy had a real yardstick. Misalignment dropped from 15-20 pixels to 2-3 on that hand-checked set, and comparison accuracy on moderately warped boards - the share of boards the tool judged correctly - went from roughly 85% to over 98%, the tool's documented before/after rather than a number I instrumented from scratch. The prototype became a product.

The lesson I keep: find which layer the error lives in before changing anything. And start simpler - I spent time on full SURF feature matching, the standard automated hunt for distinctive points like corners and marks across a whole image, before discovering board-outline detection - tracing just the outer edge of the board - covered most boards.

## Memory Anchors

- Problem: First working comparison prototype aligned only my clean test images; real factory photos left 15-20 px misalignment against a 10-pixel comparison threshold - every downstream flag was noise, the deliverable was stalled.
- Customer: Manufacturing engineers; the PCB-vs-layout comparison was built for the factory floor, where warped boards and angled photos are the norm.
- Insight: The bottleneck had two layers - affine structurally can't represent perspective (camera model), and no global transform can fix non-uniform reflow warping (physics). Symptom said "alignment is bad"; the layer said what to change.
- Action (mine): Localized before touching anything; replaced affine with a RANSAC homography myself; chose TPS over elastic/B-spline on fewest-unsupported-knobs grounds; template-matched control points with weak-match rejection; built a small ground-truth test instead of trusting overlays.
- Result: 15-20 px -> 2-3 px; comparison accuracy ~85% -> >98% on moderate warping; prototype became the shippable core of the suite.
- Learning: Find the error's layer before changing anything; start with the simplest thing that works - board-outline contours beat full SURF for most boards.

## Follow-Up Bank

1. What exactly was the bottleneck? - Not a crash: a 15-20 px average misalignment on real photos against a 10-px mismatch threshold (in plain terms: matching points sat 15-20 screen dots apart on average, but the tool only tolerates 10 before it cries wolf on everything; the average came from real factory photos, not an estimate). No comparison output could be trusted until alignment landed within about three pixels. The tool's whole premise - comparing positions - was blocked. [SAFE]
2. How did you localize it instead of trying transforms one by one? - Isolated which layer the error lived in. Affine can't represent converging parallels (parallel edges meeting in the distance when photographed at an angle) -> homography. That removed the global error but left 15-20 px, which ruled out the transform model and pointed at something local: reflow-oven bowing, non-uniform, beyond any global transform. Each step's residual error chose the next move. [SAFE]
3. Why TPS and not elastic deformation or B-splines? - Elastic needs physical constants that have no clear meaning for a composite board (it treats the board as one material and asks "what material?" - a circuit board is many); B-splines add a control-grid resolution - another knob (how fine the bending grid is). TPS minimizes bending energy (it works out the bend that flexes the image with the least total effort), is deterministic given control points (same photo in, same output out every time), and OpenCV's implementation is mature (OpenCV = the standard free image-processing library). Fewest unsupported knobs won. [SAFE - wording locked; don't name specific material parameters, that traces to the excluded telling]
4. How was the fix validated? - My own harness: 20 board photos with markers at known positions (a test set I built, with reference points whose right answers were known by construction); average residual offset 2.1 px, standard deviation 0.8 - in plain terms, after the fix matching points still sat about 2 screen dots apart on average, and sd 0.8 is the spread around that average: most photos fell within roughly a pixel of it. Plus the engineers overlaid aligned photos on layouts - that caught one case where the number looked fine but the warp was subtly wrong. Small-n, self-built, directional - not production telemetry (small-n = only 20 photos, so it shows the direction, not a certified rate). [PARTIAL - offsets measured by me; label as my harness]
5. Where does the 85% -> >98% come from? - The documented before/after for the comparison on moderately warped boards (accuracy = the share of board comparisons the tool got right; "moderate" = bent enough to push but within the range the method handles). Honest split: the per-image offsets are my measurement; the accuracy swing is the tool's documented outcome, for the moderate-warping regime, not something I instrumented from scratch. [PARTIAL - honesty label on probing]
6. What still fails? - Severe warping beyond roughly 5 mm: control points can't capture that much deformation (the pinned spots the bend follows physically can't track a bow that big); that needs a more physically based model, and it's rare in production. Past about 45 deg the feature matching itself breaks (tilt the camera that far off straight-on and the software can't find the landmarks to align on); the pipeline handled up to ~30 deg off-perpendicular (tilted from straight-on) and ~3 mm of visible warp. I name the boundary rather than imply universality. [SAFE]
7. Why not a learned registration model? - A network predicting a displacement field (an AI that outputs a map of how far every point in the image must move) needs misaligned/aligned pairs with ground-truth correspondences (example photos where the correct point-to-point mapping is known by hand); I had none, and collecting them was its own project. TPS is classical - zero training data. Real alternative at data scale; would revisit. [SAFE]
8. What was tempting to get wrong? - Treating it as a detector problem (the detector being the part that circles the differences). The failure read like detection noise - red marks that looked like over-eager flagging; tuning thresholds or the detector would have looked like progress while the error stayed in alignment. Patience to localize first was the unblock. [SAFE]
9. Any disagreement while unblocking? - No person-vs-person dispute; I built the suite solo. The friction was internal: my measurement gate against shipping an alignment that "mostly worked." I won't inflate that into a stakeholder fight. [PARTIAL - none documented]
10. What would you do differently? - Start simpler and add complexity only when it buys accuracy - full SURF (automated distinctive-point search across the whole image) came before the board-outline insight (tracing just the board's outer edge). And I'd have questioned earlier whether a warp model was the end state: severely warped boards still defeat it. [SAFE]
11. If the interviewer probes TPS math? - I used and tuned OpenCV's implementation and can explain its behavior (bending-energy minimization = the bend that flexes the image with the least total effort; control-point sensitivity = why one wrong pin drags the whole warp; why homography runs first); I won't fake deriving the formulation.
