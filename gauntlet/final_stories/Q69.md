# Q69 - tell me about a time when you had to understand a complex problem with detail, who did you talk to to find valuable information, how was it used to help solve the problem?

## Core Answer

The complex problem was making board-to-board comparison actually usable on a manufacturing floor, and the detail I couldn't get from the algorithm was in how the engineers worked.

The job: check a photographed new board (a PCB - the green circuit board inside every electronic device) against a known-good one. In practice they did it by hand - on a Zoom screen-share, toggling between the two images and circling anything suspicious for the other person. They told me that took thirty to sixty minutes a board. I tested a raw pixel difference - comparing the two photos point by point, like spot-the-difference with no tolerance - against the way they actually photograph: different lighting, slight angles, varying distance - and it cried wolf about fifteen percent of the time, about 15 flagged spots in every 100 where nothing was actually wrong, almost all of it lighting and noise. That baseline is from my own small self-check, not a lab study. And the output I was heading toward was a single difference score - one number for the whole board.

So I went to the people doing the job. Before writing the real code, I sat in on three of their screen-sharing sessions. That's where the valuable information was. The detail that mattered most wasn't that they found differences - it was what they did with them: they circled regions, by hand, for each other. They were communicating *where to look*, not a verdict. A score tells you something's wrong; a circle tells you what to open next. That was a human-workflow fact, not an image fact.

I used it in two places. The comparison had to become trustworthy first, so I replaced the single pixel diff with four complementary signals - raw difference, local-contrast normalization (evening out light and shadow across small patches of the image) so lighting stops registering as a defect, a contrast stretch for near-identical parts (amplifying tiny differences until they show up), and brightness-immune edge maps (comparing only the outlines of components, so a dark or bright photo doesn't matter) - votes combined into a weighted score (each signal votes "same" or "different," and the more reliable votes count for more), threshold (the cutoff for what counts as a difference) left on a slider because they know the board and I didn't. The output replicated their process: drag-and-drop, one click, color-coded regions (colored highlights showing exactly where to look) replacing the Zoom circling - delivered as one self-contained file that opens in a browser on their machines, like double-clicking a document, no install, no IT ticket.

False alarms went from about fifteen percent to under three, and comparison time from thirty-to-sixty minutes to under two. In plain numbers: where about 15 in every 100 flags on good boards used to be nothing, now fewer than 3 in 100 are; and checking one board takes under 2 minutes instead of half an hour to an hour. The accuracy figures come from my own tests - the starting ~15% from a small self-check, the under-3% from a 100-board test set I built by hand with known, hand-checked faults; the minutes are what the engineers reported, not stopwatch-measured. The result I count most is adoption: the color-coded diff (the difference picture) became the reference they argued over.

What I learned: I was about to treat this as an image-processing problem when the binding constraint - the thing actually holding the whole job back - was the verification workflow. Three sessions found the key behavior - but I went in looking for camera conditions and nearly missed the output design. Next time I write down what I'm watching for before I watch.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. Who exactly did you talk to? - "The manufacturing engineers who did the board verification. Honest answer on 'talked to': the valuable information came from watching three of their screen-sharing sessions, not from interviewing them. The conversation came after - them using the tool and arguing over the diff was the feedback loop." [SAFE - observation is the documented channel; claim stated, not padded]
2. Why couldn't you work it out from the images or the algorithm alone? - "The images told me two boards differed. They didn't tell me which differences the engineers cared about or how they wanted to consume the answer. The hand-circling behavior was the answer, and it only exists while they're working." [SAFE]
3. So how did their input change the design, concretely? - "Two places. Output: I'd been heading toward a single difference score; watching them showed me they needed highlighted *regions*, so the diff color-codes where and one-click replaces their toggle dance. Robustness: their real photos - light, angle, distance - are why one pixel diff was never going in, and why I went to complementary signals instead." [SAFE]
4. How was the false-alarm drop measured? - "Baseline: I ran the single raw-diff version over real photographed boards and counted flags with no defect behind them - a small personal harness, ~15% is the order of magnitude that killed the design, not a lab figure. Final: a 100-board ground-truth set I built myself with planted wrong, missing, and extra components; swept the weights and threshold; under 3% false alarms at true catches above 95, best threshold F1 0.94. In plain words: a 'ground-truth set' is 100 board photos where I placed the faults myself and wrote down exactly where they were; 'false alarms' are flags on things that are actually fine (fewer than 3 in 100); 'true catches above 95' means it found more than 95 of every 100 real faults; 'F1 0.94' is one accuracy score on a 0-to-1 scale that balances missing a real fault against crying wolf. Same numbers I use in my exceeded-expectations story - one ledger, no variants." [PARTIAL - self-built, small N; baseline and final on different data, disclose it]
5. Why four signals instead of one better one? - "Each covers a category the others miss: gross mismatch, lighting-robust difference, subtle appearance difference, structural difference immune to brightness. I tested a fifth - frequency-domain via FFT, a technique that compares the images' fine texture patterns rather than individual pixels - added compute, no accuracy, cut it." [SAFE]
6. Why leave the threshold on a slider? - "The right operating point moves with board and lighting, and the engineer knows both and I didn't. A fixed threshold - one hard-wired cutoff - bakes my guess into every board. Simple over clever." [SAFE]
7. How solid is 30-60 -> under 2 minutes? - "Before-time is the engineers' own reported figure for the manual check; after-time is their reported usage plus what I watched in later sessions. Personal observation and self-report - nothing was instrumented. Plainly put: one hand check took half an hour to an hour; with the tool they said, and I watched, it take under two minutes. Both sides are people's word, not a timer." [PARTIAL - reported, not measured]
8. How do you know it was adopted, not polite use? - "Behavior, not surveys: the color-coded diff - the picture with the differences highlighted in color - replaced their Zoom circling and became the artifact they discussed board problems over. Delivery helped - one browser file, no install, no IT ticket, so they could start same-day. Across the suite they told me it was about 15 engineers at 2 sites saving roughly 20 hours a week - engineer-reported, not instrumented; and that's all three tools in the suite, not this one alone." [PARTIAL - adoption figure is reported recollection]
9. What went wrong in my thinking? - "My own default frame. I walked in treating it as an image-processing problem and was ready to ship a verdict score. The workflow said that was useless. The fix was admitting the constraint lived in the process, not the math." [SAFE]
10. Any disagreement on this tool? - "None documented in this telling. The only pressure was my own wrong assumption. I won't borrow a conflict from another part of the project." [GAP - stated plainly]
11. Did it run offline on the locked-down floor machines? - "Yes - the factory build is one self-contained HTML file with the browser CV libraries embedded - the computer-vision code that does the comparing, bundled so it runs inside a normal browser with no internet - opens in seconds, no server. I verified it by running the same known boards through the server build and the offline file and comparing outputs." [SAFE - unified standalone stance; keep size/time soft]
12. Hardest part? - "Watching instead of building. Three sessions felt like lost time when I was confident about the algorithm; the annotation behavior they cost me an afternoon with - the one detail no file could have shown me." [SAFE]
13. Scale / do differently? - "One team, two sites, one tool. Differently: structure the observation - a written watch-list before the sessions. Three hours of unstructured watching found the key behavior; a study of the whole verification process would have caught other needs sooner." [SAFE]
