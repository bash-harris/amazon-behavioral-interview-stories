# Q58 - a time when you had to make a difficult decision to meet a tight deadline

**LP:** Bias for Action (primary); Deliver Results; Are Right, A Lot.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the five-day prototype scope decision (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 8). Same event as Q19, different lens: Q19 is the deadline and what to cut; this one is the *choice between options* and the reversibility criterion used to make it. Q74 is a different decision (standalone-vs-server, STORY 4).

## Core Answer

The date in this story was my own. The hard decision was a bet I had to price before its information existed: a PCB comparison tool had burned six months of scoping attempts without an agreed definition, and I'd stopped believing meetings would produce one. (A PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device; the tool photographed a finished board, compared the photo to the board's design drawing, and flagged where the two differed. "Scoping" = pinning down exactly what the tool should do. "Six months" = roughly how long that stretch of failed attempts lasted, from my own memory of the project history - not an instrumented figure.) So I gave myself five days to a demo - which meant choosing up front, on my own guess alone, which build to stake them on. (Five days = the deadline I set myself, and also what the prototype actually took, per my own work record - not instrumented.)

Option one was keep collecting requirements. Six months of evidence said that doesn't work. Option two was the version I'd be comfortable showing: alignment, illumination, export - the trustworthy package. (Alignment = lining the photo up with the design drawing so the comparison is valid; illumination = working when the lighting changes; export = getting the results out into a file others can use.) That took about two weeks - an at-the-time estimate of build time, never actually run, because I didn't choose it. Option three was the smallest thing that answered one question, *can it see a difference at all?*, and demo it on day five.

I chose option three. My rule when a decision has to be made before the information exists: pick whichever option makes the cheaper mistake. The two-week build wasn't a two-week bet - if it guessed wrong about what they needed, I'd burned two weeks and landed back in the stalemate I was standing in. The prototype cost five days, worst case. It was the reversible call, and reversible calls are the ones you're allowed to make fast (reversible = I can take it back cheaply if I'm wrong - I lose only the days spent, not the project).

What made it hard wasn't the date, I made that up. It was that option three was a tool I was embarrassed to show. No alignment, no illumination handling, no export - it worked if you photographed the board the way I had, and real lines don't. (A "line" = a factory production line, where boards move and nobody poses the photo for you.) Demoing it was inviting someone to call it worthless. That was the priced downside: the bet capped at five days and an embarrassment, and its one claim - it could see a difference - was provable live in the room. So I presented the demo as a question, not an answer. Its only job was to get them to tell me what was missing.

The lead shared it with the team right away, and the answers came back fast and specific - lighting, alignment, export. I fixed them in order of impact, and two weeks from day one the tool was production-ready. (Production-ready = a milestone meaning the engineers used it in real work - not a measured score of how often the tool is correct.) The five days bought exactly the information the two-week build would have had to guess at.

What I'd do differently: shoot a couple of real board photos before the demo. The team spent part of the first hour (of the demo; from my recollection, not logged) rediscovering limits I already knew.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: PCB comparison tool parked six months in the backlog (backlog = the to-do list of features not yet started); scoping deadlocked - nobody could define what it should do.
- Customer: the manufacturing engineers; the engineering lead as first demo audience.
- Insight: the binding constraint (the real thing blocking progress) was definition, not capability - and a decision that must precede its own information should be made reversible, not right.
- Action (mine): set a five-day demo; priced the three options by cost-of-mistake; committed to the smallest end-to-end slice (a working strip from one end to the other, not a mockup: upload, pixel compare - checking the two images against each other spot by spot for differences - visible result); deferred alignment, illumination, export as "conditions," not correctness; framed the demo as a question to the team.
- Result: demoed day five; team's specific gaps (lighting, alignment, export) became the roadmap in impact order; production-ready two weeks from day one (5 + 9 = five days building plus nine more fixing, per my own work record, not instrumented).
- Learning: buy information with the cheapest reversible artifact (artifact = the actual thing you build and show); and bring real input data to the demo so feedback starts at next steps.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. Was the deadline imposed? - No. I set it, and I say that first. The real pressure was a six-month stalemate; I compressed on purpose so the decision happened while it was still cheap. [SAFE]
2. Why option three over the two-week build? - The two-week build was a guess dressed as a plan: it bet nine extra days on a definition nobody had given me. The prototype was a bet I could take back in five. Cheapest mistake wins. [SAFE]
3. What exactly was the criterion for keeping vs. cutting? - Whether a feature changed *whether the concept works* or only *whether the answer is trustworthy*. Upload, pixel comparison, visible result answer the first. Alignment, illumination, export are the second - the deferrable half. [SAFE]
4. Demoing something you knew fails on a real line - isn't that a bad decision? - The cut was "works under line conditions," not "works correctly." It was right under the conditions it claimed, and I presented it as a question, so the fragility was the topic, not a surprise. [SAFE]
5. What were the problems the team found? - Three, inside the first hour of the team seeing it (the three-problem count from my recollection, not logged): pixel comparison broke under lighting changes, no alignment so photos had to match exactly, no export. They became the roadmap: alignment first - nothing worked without it - illumination second, export last. [SAFE]
6. How reliable are the time figures? - My own work record, not instrumented: five days to prototype, nine more to production-ready. "Production-ready" is a milestone - the engineers used it - not a measured quality rate (a measured quality rate would be a percentage score of how often the tool is right), and the comparison-engine (the part that matches the board photo against the design drawing and flags differences) accuracy numbers belong to that fix's own story; I don't restate them here. [PARTIAL]
7. Was the decision right, or lucky? - The outcome was specific and directional (directional = the answers told me what to do next, not just that people liked it): the three gaps found in the first hour were the exact list the deferred half produced, that list became the fix order, and two weeks later it was in use. A lucky demo produces praise or rejection, not a roadmap. [SAFE]
8. Hardest part? - The embarrassment. Option three was a tool I didn't want to show, and the hard part wasn't building it in five days, it was choosing it over the version that felt safe on day one. [SAFE]
9. Who disagreed? - Nobody in person, and I won't inflate it. The opposition was a standing backlog verdict - "too complex to scope" - and I didn't argue with it. I scheduled a demo instead. [PARTIAL - stated plainly]
10. What if the team had called it worthless? - Then the five days were still the cheapest possible way to find that out, because export was one of the cuts - there was no fallback artifact to salvage anyway. The prior route was engineers comparing board images by eye; I was managing the risk of building the wrong features, not the risk of adoption. [SAFE]
11. Bigger picture / what did it lead to? - I built the suite solo, so the call was mine alone. The prototype's pixel compare later grew into the aligned comparison engine (the finished, properly working version of that photo-vs-design matcher), and I broke the monolith (one big block of code) into modules (separate pieces that can be changed on their own) right after those two weeks so the next feature wasn't a rewrite. I keep sizes out of it - the records carry conflicting line counts (line counts = how many lines of code the program has), so "modularized it" is all I say. [GAP -> generalize]
12. Do differently? - Collect a couple of real board photos before the demo, so the team's first hour went to next steps instead of rediscovering lighting and angle limits I already knew about. [SAFE]
