# Q56 - a time where you gave someone wrong information

## Core Answer

[GAP: nothing in my record shows me personally misinforming a colleague, so I won't build a fake scene. This is the same hole as Q25 - same gap, same LP - so I treat them as one prep answer. What follows is a labeled framework, not a story.]

The closest I've actually lived is my software handing the manufacturing engineers - the people on the factory floor who build and check the boards - wrong information, and the pattern below is what I did there and would do face to face.

The big one was the BOM reconciler's pin-count check. A BOM (bill of materials) is the parts list for a circuit board; the reconciler is my tool that cross-checks that list against the board's design drawing. Its pin-count rule matched BOM pins (the connector legs a part's spec lists) against layout pads (the solder points on the drawing) exactly, and any difference became a flagged mismatch. On boards mixing component types it pushed a false claim to engineers roughly 12% of the time - in plain terms, about 12 flags in every 100 said a part was wrong when it wasn't: a part looked misplaced when it was only a different package variant (the same electrical part made in a different physical shape, which legitimately has a different leg count). That 12% was my own hand count off early flagged reports, not a number a system measured. I owned that as my rule, not the data's fault. I worked through 20 flagged boards and found the asymmetry: more pads than pins was almost always a legitimate variant, fewer pads was the real error. I rewrote the rule around that split, adding a component-type check and a tunable ratio (a threshold I could dial), and false alarms fell below 2% - same hand-counted method, just fewer wrong flags. I left the residual miss-risk - the leftover chance a real error slips through unflagged - visible rather than dropping the check to keep reports looking clean.

Two smaller documented cases. The debug view I built (a technical screen showing exactly what the software read) exposed the parser's raw output - the parser being the part of the tool that reads the uploaded files - so when it misread files with unusual naming, twice in the first week, the engineer saw the exact point where the data went off track, and the fix was fast. And the GPT-4o analysis - an online AI service that looks at board photos and describes what it sees, right about 85% of the time by my rough count (my own estimate, no formal test behind it) - was always presented as a suggestion: an "AI Analysis" label, a confidence number, a "verify with your expertise" prompt, and a right/wrong button. Never the final word.

So, framework for misinforming a person directly:

1. Tell them myself the moment I know, before they build on it.
2. Swap in the right fact and spell out what the wrong one already cost.
3. Own the cause - no scapegoating the tool, the data, or them for asking.
4. Show how the slip happened so they can calibrate the rest of what I say.
5. Change the process behind it and confirm the correction landed.
6. Ask them to call me on it next time.

The record covers how I clean up wrong information from my systems. A personal scene isn't in it, and otherwise.
