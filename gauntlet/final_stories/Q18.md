# Q18 - tell me about a time when you got negative feedback from a higher up

**LP:** Earn Trust (primary), Learn and Be Curious   **Story:** the five-day prototype and the one-hour team review (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md STORY 8); process lessons from STORY 10 and STORY 11

## Core Answer

My lead put my rough five-day prototype - a deliberately unfinished first version, made in five days just to draw reactions - in front of the manufacturing team, and within an hour they came back with three critical issues. That's the honest shape of the strongest negative feedback I got from above - the lead escalated it (passed it up for the team to review), the team delivered the critique, and no manager sat me down one-on-one. I won't inflate it past that.

Context in one line: engineers had wanted an automated PCB comparison tool for six months. (A PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device; the tool was meant to automatically spot differences between two images of a board, and the "six months" is how long the request had sat, per the team's account.) It kept stalling because it was "too complex" to scope - too complicated to pin down exactly what to build. So instead of scoping, I built. Five days: one monolithic HTML file, inline JavaScript, no build system, no modules, no tests. (Plain words: a single web-page file that a browser opens, with all the program code written inside it - nothing split into separate parts, no tools that assemble the code automatically, and none of the automatic self-checks that catch coding mistakes.) It loaded two images, ran a basic pixel comparison - matching the images dot by dot on each dot's numeric brightness value - showed a result. It worked under clean conditions and was rough everywhere else - and I'd written its limitations down before the demo.

On day five I demoed to my lead. He shared it with the team right away. An hour later, the three. One: the pixel comparison broke under illumination changes - light levels - the same good board, photographed under different light, came back full of diffs, meaning the tool reported differences all over it. Two: no alignment at all - the tool couldn't line the two images up so the same points matched - unless the board was photographed at the exact same angle, the diff was meaningless. Three: no export - engineers couldn't save a result - no way to write it out to a file for use elsewhere; the tool only existed on screen.

I didn't defend it. I'd documented the limitations going in, so the feedback confirmed known gaps instead of exposing hidden ones. I told the team all three were real, and asked which order mattered for their work.

I ranked by impact and complaint frequency: alignment first - without it nothing else could work; illumination normalization second - fixing out the lighting differences so the same board doesn't look different - the most frequent complaint; export third - valuable but not a blocker. Nine days after the review the tool was production-ready - in use by the team for their actual work - two weeks total, five prototype plus nine of repair. That one hour taught me more about what to build than six months of scoping had. (Every number here - six months, five days, one hour, nine days, two weeks - counts calendar time spent on this work, from one source: my own telling of the story; these are self-reported durations, not figures a system logged.)

Two changes stuck. I started building observability in from day one instead of bolting it on after trust erodes - observability means the tool shows its own inner workings: what it decided and why. The BOM tool's debug view had been an afterthought (BOM = bill of materials, the parts list for a board; the debug view = a screen showing what the tool looked at and why it called what it called). And collecting failure cases, finding the pattern, fixing the root cause became my standard loop - failure cases being examples where the tool got the answer wrong, and the root cause being the underlying reason, not the surface symptom. I ran it on the Good Board tool's illumination, rotation, and scale problems - the board photographed under different light, at different angles, or at different zoom levels. The lesson wasn't "build better prototypes." I'd optimized for speed of feedback and underinvested in realism. The review cost me an hour of embarrassment and bought a roadmap that was actually right.

3. How did you stay non-defensive? - I'd documented the prototype's limitations before the demo, so nothing the team hit was a surprise. Shipping a known limitation is different from shipping a bug, and the prototype's whole purpose was to learn what to build - defending it would have meant defending the wrong thing. [SAFE]
4. How did you pick the priority order? - Impact and frequency of complaint; my call, not the team's. Alignment gates every credible comparison on real re-shot photos (plain words: until the two images are lined up so the same point on each matches, any reported difference is unreliable). Illumination was the most frequent complaint; export is valuable but doesn't block inspection. [SAFE]
5. Is the timeline solid? - Two weeks total: 5 days prototype + 9 days refinement. One telling, one source, and the review happened on day 5 - so "production-ready nine days after the review" is the same fact stated carefully. In plain terms: these numbers count working days on the build, from my own account of the story - self-reported scheduling, not timestamps a system recorded. [SAFE]
6. Why does light break a pixel comparison? - Pixel diffing matches on raw values; global brightness shifts those values, so an unaligned board fires diffs everywhere. Plain words: a "pixel" is one dot of an image; the tool compares each dot's numeric brightness value, and turning the overall lighting up or down changes every one of those values at once - so the whole picture reads as different. That's the mechanism behind the documented illumination failure, stated as illustration - not an extra event. [PARTIAL - illustrative restatement]
7. What alternatives did you reject? - Going back to requirements gathering - asking users in detail what they need before building - it had already stalled six months - and defending scope ("it's just a prototype"), which dodges the feedback the prototype existed to collect. Both were rejected in favor of accepting the three hits and ordering them. [SAFE]
8. What went wrong / hardest part? - Handing over five days of work and watching an hour of review list three blockers - things that stop the tool from being usable. The embarrassment was the real cost. The alternative - polishing privately for months - was the failure that had already happened. [SAFE]
9. If a manager had said the whole approach was wrong? - Framework, not memory: ask for the specific failing case, not a verdict; reproduce it in the user's environment on the user's inputs; test the alternative against the cases that broke mine; commit to whichever survives the evidence. The record shows three specific issues, not a wholesale rejection. [GAP-labeled - framework]
10. What changed about how you take feedback now? - I ask for the failing case instead of the verdict, and reproduce it on the user's setup. And I make feedback cheap to give: the debug view exists so a user can show me which rule fired instead of just saying the answer looks wrong (the debug view = a screen inside the tool showing what it checked and why it decided what it decided; "which rule fired" = which of its checks triggered). [SAFE]
11. What would you do differently? - Build observability from day one instead of after trust erodes - the BOM tool's debug view was an afterthought, and from the start it would have caught edge cases - unusual situations outside the normal use - earlier. And run the collect-failures, analyze-patterns, fix-root-causes loop from the start - which I later did on the Good Board tool's illumination, rotation, and scale edge cases. [SAFE]
12. Who did the work? - Me. The PCB suite was a solo build - I was the only engineer. The prototype, the review response, and the two-week fix were all mine. No "we" in this story. [SAFE]
