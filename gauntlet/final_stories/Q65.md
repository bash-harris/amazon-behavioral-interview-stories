## Core Answer

The ask on this project came with a number attached. The defect classifier - the trained AI that reads a photo of a board component and names what's wrong with it - had to clear 90% on data it had never seen: photos deliberately held back from its training, and the number simply counts how many of those it got right. The manufacturing engineers were going to read its verdicts on the line, and the error that costs money is the bad board that gets through.

My first trained model looked like it smashed the ask: 99% accuracy - right on the very photos it had studied while training. That number was worthless. On data it hadn't seen, the same model sat at 72 - it was memorizing, not learning. The easy outs were right there: quote the 99, or call the 72 a first iteration. I refused both.

What made the result exceptional wasn't a model trick - it was making the number mean something before chasing it. I rebuilt the split at component level - the "split" is which photos the model studies and which it's tested on, and I put every photo of the same physical part on one side only, so test photos can't hide inside the study material and flatter the score. I normalized for floor lighting, because photos come from under fluorescent tubes, LED panels, and equipment shadows, and a classifier will happily learn the lamp instead of the part. And I judged per-class recall, not one headline accuracy - for each kind of defect, how many of the real ones the model actually caught, checked category by category - because most components are good, so accuracy flatters a model that just says "good" every time.

Then the training itself. The model has two parts: a pretrained base - an off-the-shelf image recognizer that had already learned edges, shapes and textures from millions of ordinary photos - and a new "head," the output layer I trained to name our specific defects. So: stabilize the new head with the pretrained base frozen - locked so it can't change - and only then thaw the top layers at a gentler rate, unlocking the upper few layers for much smaller adjustments. Thaw too wide or too fast and the model overwrites the exact features you borrowed. That got to 94% on a few hundred photos it had never seen, with the gap between the two numbers - score on studied photos versus score on unseen ones - down to a couple of points - and no defect class below the bar, meaning every single defect type on its own cleared 90%.

The part that actually exceeded expectations is the second layer. The ask was one classifier that passes. The result was a standard - honest split, honest metric, retrainable when new defect types showed up - and the recipe carried into every training run after. What I learned the hard way: a number without a named test set isn't a result. The 99 wasn't; the 94 was.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: Classifier shipped bar was >90% on held-out data (held-out = photos kept back from training and never seen; trust on the line; bad-board escapes are the costly error). First run: 99% training but 72% validation (its score on studied photos vs on the unseen ones) - memorizing, and the headline could have passed for real.
- Customer: Manufacturing engineers reading the tool's per-component verdicts; the tools were already in use; suite built solo.
- Insight: Fix what the number *measures* before trying to raise it - component-level split blocks leakage (test photos hiding in the training set), lighting normalization stops "learning the lamp," per-class recall is the honest metric under imbalance (most parts are good, so rare defects hide inside a big overall percentage).
- Action (mine): Re-split 70/15/15 stratified by component (70% study, 15% adjust-while-training, 15% untouched final exam; every defect type present in the same proportions in each part); CLAHE preprocessing (a contrast-flattening filter that evens out lighting differences in the photo); up-weighted defect classes (~5:1) and judged on precision-recall (of the defects it claimed, how many were real / of the real ones, how many it caught); two-phase progressive unfreezing (head first on frozen base, then top layers at a lower rate); logged runs instead of trusting memory; stopped tuning where more thawing only overfit (memorized the study photos).
- Result: 72% -> ~94% validation on the ~300-image holdout (15% of ~2,000) - the share of the held-back photos it classified correctly, from one documented final run, not averaged; train/val gap ~3 points (distance between studied-photo and unseen-photo scores); per-class recall >90% every defect category (each defect type clears the bar on its own); retrainable in ~30 min on one GPU (a single graphics card).
- Learning: A number without a named test set isn't a result - refuse the flattering headline, and refuse to ship the "first iteration" excuse either.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. What exactly was the expectation, and who set it? - "The ship criteria were written down: validation accuracy above 90% - the score on photos never used in training - training accuracy not way above it, inference under 100 ms - the computer judging one photo in under a tenth of a second - and retrainable as new defect types appear. The 90 wasn't academic - engineers were reading its verdicts on the line." [SAFE]
2. Why wasn't the 99% real? - "Training accuracy is memory; validation is generalization - its score on the photos it studied versus the photos it never saw. A huge gap between them is the diagnosis itself - the model memorized. I quote 99 only as the trap I didn't fall into." [SAFE]
3. What was actually wrong, mechanically? - "Catastrophic forgetting: my randomly initialized head - the brand-new output layer, which starts knowing nothing - fired big noisy gradients early on, meaning its first, crude corrections were violent enough to overwrite the pretrained features I'd borrowed from the image recognizer. Phase one trains the head with the base frozen; phase two thaws the last few dozen layers at a tenth the rate - each adjustment about ten times smaller." [SAFE]
4. How was the 94% measured? - "On a validation holdout of roughly 300 images - a stratified 70/15/15 split of about 2,000 labeled component photos - 70% to learn from, 15% to adjust against during training, 15% kept untouched as the final exam, with every defect type represented in the same proportions in each part - split by component so the same part never appears twice. Single final run, no repeated seeds - that's the honest limit: one run of one model, not an average across attempts." [PARTIAL - documented set, one run]
5. Wasn't 91% already over the bar? Why keep going? - "The regularization pass landed around 91 - that's the round where I added anti-memorization safeguards - technically passing. But one bad split from that margin vanishes, and the imbalance fix (defect classes weighted ~5:1, judging recall instead of accuracy - measuring how many of the real defects it caught rather than the overall share it got right) is what bought the margin to ~94. Past the bar with no cushion isn't a floor tool." [SAFE - 91% intermediate is documented]
6. Isn't there a '500-image test set' in your notes? - "Yes, and I flag it myself: an older note records the same 94 on a '500-image held-out test,' which doesn't reconcile with 15% of ~2,000. I lead with the ~300 validation holdout - the ~300 photos kept back from training - and don't blend the two records." [GAP - disclose, don't merge]
7. Why not just use a bigger model? - "I'd researched the sizes: the largest variant beat the one I used by under two points for about three times the training cost, and transformers - a newer family of image models that learns from scratch and needs far more example photos - wanted data I didn't have. At ~2,000 images, a bigger model memorizes faster, not better." [SAFE]
8. Was it fast enough to use? - "About 45 ms per image on CPU - roughly one-twentieth of a second on an ordinary processor - against the under-100 ms requirement - a single-image benchmark, hardware unrecorded, and the shipped path ran in-browser via TensorFlow.js - the model converted to run inside the web browser itself, no server - so it's a benchmark, not a floor." [PARTIAL]
9. What's the 'standard' concretely, and how do you know it stuck? - "Retraining took about 30 minutes on one GPU - a single graphics card - so a newly discovered defect type was a data-plus-retrain task, not a project. The two-phase approach with the audited split and recall-first metrics - judging per-defect-type catch rates before the headline number - carried into every later run I did; the repo's checked-in training script is one of those later, hardened variants - for a different binary task, yes-or-no rather than naming among defect types, so I never fuse its numbers with this run." [PARTIAL]
10. What else went wrong? - "Two assumptions died. Unfreezing more layers always helps - the 50-layer config overfit, memorizing instead of learning, and falsified it. And CLAHE handles all lighting - the contrast-flattening filter - some extremes survived it; controlled lighting wasn't an option on the floor." [SAFE]
11. Hardest part? - "Resisting the good-looking number. The 99 made the run feel successful in the moment; admitting the deliverable was 72-and-broken was the whole story." [SAFE]
12. Do differently? - "Cosine-annealed learning-rate schedule instead of a fixed phase-two rate - the adjustment size smoothly eased down over the phase instead of sitting constant; component-level split from day one; more labels before any training - more human-checked example photos." [SAFE]
13. How is this different from your comparison-engine story? - "Different component, different numbers. The engine is four-signal board-vs-layout comparison - false alarms ~15% to under 3, F1 0.94 on a 100-board ground truth - F1 being one accuracy number that balances not-missing-a-real-defect against not-crying-wolf, and ground truth meaning hand-confirmed real defect locations. This is the trained classifier - 72 to ~94 on a ~300-image holdout (the photos kept back from training). Same suite, separate ledgers." [SAFE]
