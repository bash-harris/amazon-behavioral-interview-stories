## Core Answer

I'll be straight with you: that story isn't in my record. I built the PCB inspection suite solo - PCB means printed circuit board, the green board inside nearly every electronic device, and the suite checks photos of finished boards against their design files. Sole builder, sole architect, no reports, no junior teammate. I've never delegated a critical task to a junior, and I won't invent one in this interview. Hire and Develop the Best is a manager leadership principle (Amazon calls its core values "leadership principles," and this is the one about growing the people below you), and at my level it's marked out of scope - SDE-1/L4 is the entry-level engineering rung. So what I can honestly give you is the underlying discipline I'd use, labeled as a framework, drawn from things I actually did.

Move one: hand over a slice with a checkable output - a piece of work you can grade right-or-wrong on evidence, not on opinion. I don't grade work by "looks right." I verified alignment (whether a photo of the board lines up exactly with the design image it's checked against) against twenty ground-truth board images - "ground truth" means samples whose correct answer was established by hand - average offset 2.1 pixels, a pixel being one tiny dot of the image and the offset the distance the two pictures disagree, standard deviation 0.8, which measures how tightly the twenty individual results cluster around that average (low = consistent, not scattered). And I pushed fifteen BOM files through both builds of the standalone tool - a BOM is the bill of materials, the parts list for a board; the standalone tool is the single-file version that runs offline on factory machines with no server and no installs; "both builds" means the server-based version and the offline one - and required the reports to match exactly: same inputs, identical outputs. A junior's task would get the same treatment: a measurable checkpoint early enough that a failure is still recoverable, not a status report at the deadline.

Move two: when it fails, own the outcome and develop the person. My record has me naming my own misses - I assumed every factory machine ran a modern browser and shipped the IE11 compatibility check after being wrong (IE11 = Internet Explorer 11, an old, slow-to-update browser). If a junior's failure reached a customer, I'd take the delay as mine in front of them, then make it their rep - their chance to earn it back: reproduce the failure, propose the fix, review it against the evidence. That's the human version of the debug view I built - a display showing exactly how the tool reached each conclusion - so the manufacturing engineers could verify the tool's reasoning without asking me.

The honest close: I have no lived example, so at this level I'd call it N/A - and I'd ask what "good" looks like before L5, the senior engineering level one step above mine.
