# Q86 - a time where you interacted with a customer with negative feedback directly

**LP:** Customer Obsession (primary); Earn Trust; Learn and Be Curious   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - STORY 2 warped-board false alarms and the in-person overlay correction; spine = the direct interaction and how I handled the feedback in the room (distinct from Q45's difficulty spine and Q78's feedback-loops spine)

## Core Answer

The clearest piece of negative feedback I got came straight from the manufacturing engineers who used my PCB-vs-layout comparison tool - told to my face, and they were right. (A PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device; the "layout" is its design drawing.) The tool was flagging mismatches on warped boards that were actually fine. Warped means the board bent or curled out of flat. A false alarm is when the tool cries wolf - it says there's a problem where there isn't one - and that's the worst feedback a QA tool can get, because every false alarm sends someone over to hand-check a part that was never wrong.

The context: the tool compares a photo of a real board against its layout and calls out missing or shifted components. It flags a mismatch at about 10 pixels - a pixel is one of the tiny dots a digital photo is made of, so the tool calls a defect when the photo and the drawing sit 10 dots apart over a part. That setting is a fixed choice I made, not a measurement, and it means the alignment - how exactly the photo lands on top of the drawing, part over part - has to hold to a couple of pixels or everything after it is noise. My alignment was one global perspective correction (a single uniform tilt-and-stretch applied to the whole picture equally - one adjustment for everything), and by my own metric it looked good.

I could have pulled up the numbers at them. Instead I asked them to show me. They put the aligned photo over the layout like a tracing - an overlay check - and checked the pads (the small metal spots where components solder on), and right there some pads plainly didn't line up - on boards where my average metric said fine. My number and their eyes disagreed, and their eyes were right. The average was hiding a local problem: boards warped in the reflow oven - the oven that melts the solder joints, whose heat bows the board - and the warping wasn't uniform, so the middle bowed differently than the edges. One global transform can't straighten a bow in the middle of a board.

How I handled the moment mattered more than the fix. I didn't defend the metric and I didn't leave the desk. I had them mark the failing boards, I reproduced each one myself on the same inputs, and I treated their overlay as data I didn't have rather than an opinion to argue with. Then I went back to the physics and added a second, non-rigid stage - a thin-plate spline, a rubber-sheet-style correction that bends the picture by different amounts in different places, anchored at control points I placed at the corners, the fiducials (small engraved reference dots boards carry for machine positioning), and the pads - to correct the local deformation.

Far-edge misalignment - how far the photo sat off from the drawing at the corner farthest from the middle, counted in photo dots - dropped from 15 to 20 pixels to 2 or 3, comparison accuracy on warped boards went from about 85% to over 98% (the share of boards the tool judged correctly - real defects caught and good boards left alone, out of every hundred checks), and the false alarms stopped. Both numbers are measured before/after comparisons from my alignment testing, not guesses. And I kept their overlay check in how I validated, because it caught what the average couldn't.

What I took from it: when a customer says my result is wrong, my number is the hypothesis and their observation is the data. You handle negative feedback by finding the failing case with them, not by winning the argument.

## Follow-Up Bank

1. Was that really direct negative feedback from a customer, or a QA finding? - Two documented pieces, and I'll be precise. The engineers reported false alarms on warped boards - user feedback. Then the overlay check was direct and corrective, in the room, with them driving it. In plain words, the overlay check is laying the aligned photo over the drawing like a tracing sheet and looking to see whether the parts actually sit on their spots. I don't have a verbatim quote, so I won't invent one. [SAFE - premise labeled, never inflated]
2. How did you keep from getting defensive? - I separated the kinds of evidence. My aggregate said fine; their overlay showed specific pads off. I asked them to show me the failing boards and reproduced them, instead of arguing the average. The case decides. [SAFE]
3. What if they'd been wrong - a bad photo, not a bad tool? - Reproducing it would have shown that, and I'd have said so. That's why I reproduced rather than either conceding or arguing blindly. Here the reproduction matched what they saw, so the tool was wrong. [SAFE]
4. What specifically did you change? - A non-rigid thin-plate-spline stage after the homography (the first-stage global correction - one uniform tilt-and-stretch for the whole picture), control points at corners, fiducials, and pads, with template-similarity gating on control-point matches - meaning each anchor is checked against what it's supposed to be sitting on before it's trusted, because one bad point bends the whole warp. [SAFE]
5. Why TPS over elastic deformation or B-splines? - It minimizes bending energy - it picks the smoothest possible bend through the anchors - is deterministic given the control points (same inputs, same output, every time), needs no physical constants that don't mean anything for a board (real material properties like stiffness, which a photo doesn't have), and the math is mature in OpenCV (the standard free image-processing library). Chosen on those grounds, not by default. [SAFE]
6. How measured - how did you know it actually fixed it? - Quantitative: a 20-image controlled test with ground-truth markers - twenty board photos I set up on purpose against known correct positions, so the right answers were fixed in advance - average offset 2.1 pixels (how far the photo landed from where it should have been, on average) with a standard deviation of 0.8 (typical spread around that average - most boards within about a pixel of it). Qualitative: I kept the engineers' overlay spot-check alongside it. Both checks, because the first one alone missed what the second caught. [SAFE - canonical alignment numbers]
7. How reliable are the headline numbers? - In plain terms: the photo's far edge used to sit 15-20 dots off the drawing, now 2-3; and on bent boards the tool judged roughly 85 of every hundred correctly before, better than 98 after. 15-20 px -> 2-3 px and ~85% -> >98% are the bank's one canonical alignment telling (STORY 2); I don't blend the alternate 5-10 px or timing figures from other tellings of the same pipeline. [SAFE]
8. Where does it still fail? - It handles photos up to about 30 degrees off-perpendicular (camera angled up to about 30 degrees away from straight overhead) and roughly 3 mm of warp (how far the board's surface bows away from perfectly flat); above about 5 mm of deformation the control points can't capture it. Rare on the floor - I name the boundary instead of implying the fix is universal. [SAFE]
9. What was the hardest part of the interaction itself? - Letting go of a metric that looked good in front of the people using the tool. The physics fix was work; admitting an average can hide a wrong board changed how I validate. [PARTIAL - my reflection, not a source line]
10. Any disagreement in the room? - Not with the users. The resistance I had to work through was my own prior assumption that a passing aggregate meant the alignment was right. [PARTIAL - no documented user conflict]
11. What's the judgment/trade-off? - Defend the number or find the case; and a simple global transform vs. adding a non-rigid stage that costs compute and complexity. I kept the global stage and added local correction only because their failing boards demanded it, and kept a human check even after the numbers passed. [SAFE]
12. Did the false alarms come before or after the fix? - The source records them in the post-fix result line ("previously triggered false alarms"); I'm telling the pre-fix problem that motivated the work. It stays "they were getting false alarms" - never a named meeting or date. [PARTIAL - ordering implied by the fix, not separately quoted]

Across the full suite it was about 15 engineers across 2 sites; they told me it saved roughly 20 hours a week - engineer-reported, not instrumented (they told me what they believed they were saving; I never measured it). [PARTIAL - reported]
14. What would you do differently? - Bring the customer into validation from the start. I validated against a number before I validated against their eyes, and their eyes found the gap first. [SAFE]
