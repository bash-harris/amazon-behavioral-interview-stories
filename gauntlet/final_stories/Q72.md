# Q72 - tell me about a time you pushed for something

**LP:** Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit (primary - data-backed advocacy); Customer Obsession (primary); Ownership (primary).   **Story:** BGSW PCB suite - the pin-count rule change and the transparency push in BOM reconciliation (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 10, debug view expanded in STORY 11). Same event as Q4/Q17/Q52, distinct spine: the advocacy and the push-through to trust - not Q4's wrong-assumption failure, not Q52's layered investigation, not Q17's commit framing.

## Core Answer

The things I pushed for were two: a change to how my BOM reconciliation tool decided a part matched - and then, once the rule had changed, for the engineers to be able to see inside it. ("BOM" is short for "bill of materials" - the shopping list of every part that goes onto a circuit board. "Reconciliation" means checking that list against the board's layout drawing and flagging every place the two disagree.)

The tool compared pin count in the parts list against pad count in the layout, literally. In plain words: a chip's pins are its little metal legs, and a pad is the solder dot on the board where one leg attaches - the original rule demanded the two counts be exactly equal. On real boards most flags were legitimate package variants: the footprint - the pad pattern drawn for a part - is often shared by a whole family of parts, with extra "no-connect" pads left over for the family's other members, so a perfectly good part can have fewer legs than dots. The engineers' answer was to hand-verify every pin-count flag - open each one and judge it by eye - which defeats the point of automating it. That workaround was the real problem. A tool nobody trusts is a tool nobody uses. So I pushed to change the rule.

I didn't push with an opinion - I built the case. I pulled twenty BOMs with pin-count mismatches and sorted every case: twenty parts lists the pin check had flagged as disagreeing - the ones I had to hand - with every flagged part in them classified by hand, one by one. The split was clean - a footprint larger than the part (more dots than legs, so spare dots left over) was almost always a legitimate variant; a smaller one (fewer dots than legs, with legs that have nowhere to go) was a genuine error, because the part physically can't fit.

What I pushed for specifically: keep pin count in the comparison, because it's what catches a wrong part, but treat it as directional, not equal - the direction of the disagreement is what matters (more dots than legs is normal; fewer is a real fault), not exact agreement. That left a hole where a wrong part in a larger footprint could sail through, so I pushed again: a component-type check with a configurable threshold, defaulted from that same data. Plainly put: also verify that the kind of part matches what the pad pattern expects, and allow the dots-versus-legs counts to differ only up to a set limit - the threshold, a cutoff you can tune - whose starting value came from the same twenty files.

The part I'm most deliberate about is the second push. A fixed rule doesn't earn trust by itself. I pushed for a debug view: a screen that shows the tool's working - the raw entry (the part's line exactly as the parts list reads), the parsed reference after each step (what the tool thought that line meant after each translation), the rule applied (which check made the call), and an override for the engineer (a button for the human to disagree with the tool). It surfaced edge cases my heuristic - my rough rule of thumb - got wrong in the first week: unusual naming conventions, parts written under label styles the tool hadn't learned to read. Those were mine to fix, not the engineers' to route around.

The result: on the files I had, false alarms - the tool crying wolf - on mixed-component boards (boards populated with several variants of parts sharing one pad pattern, exactly where the old rule stumbled) fell from about twelve percent to under two. What it counts: of the tool's flags on those files, about 12 in every 100 were wrong before the change and fewer than 2 in every 100 after. Where it came from: my own hand-sorting of those twenty flagged files, re-checked after the fix against a regression suite - a fixed pile of known files re-run so nothing that matched before flipped. How solid: self-judged, not audited by anyone else - a direction of travel on my files, not a measured factory-wide rate. The engineers stopped re-verifying every flag.

Honest boundary: I pushed against the tool's rule and the requirement to kill false alarms without blinding the tool - not against a person. There's no rival colleague in this record and I won't invent one. What I can stand behind: I built the case with data, proposed the change, owned the residual risk, and saw it through to trust instead of stopping at "this is broken."

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: literal pin-count-equals-pad-count rule (a part's legs had to match the board's solder dots exactly) flagged legitimate package variants; manufacturing engineers hand-verified every pin-count mismatch - the workaround was the call to action
- Customer: the engineers living with the false alarms (wrong red flags); report credibility was the stake; their trust was the actual target of the second push
- Insight: "pin count is noisy" is a preference; the directional asymmetry (larger footprint = variant, smaller = physically impossible) is a finding - and a fixed rule still needs to be see-through to be trusted
- Action (mine): pulled and categorized 20 failing BOMs; pushed to keep pin count but make it directional (larger -> review, smaller -> definite mismatch); pushed again for component-type check with configurable 2:1 threshold (at most about twice as many pads as pins) defaulted from the data; built debug view (raw entry, parsed reference, rule applied, override)
- Result: ~12% -> under 2% false alarms (crying-wolf flags) on mixed-component boards - self-categorized sample + 15-BOM regression re-run (a fixed pile of 15 known files re-checked so nothing that worked before broke), not a validated production rate; engineers stopped hand-verifying every flag; week one surfaced edge cases (unusual naming conventions), fixed as mine
- Learning: pushing for something = build the case with data, propose the concrete change, own the residual risk, and follow through to transparency - flagging the problem is the first third, not the whole job

## Follow-Up Bank
1. What exactly did you push for, and who were you pushing against? - Two pushes: change the measurement rule (directional, not equality), and make the tool transparent (debug view with override). Counterparty honestly: the record shows a push against the tool's existing rule and the requirement to fix false alarms without breaking genuine detection. No named person opposed the change; I won't invent a rival. The stakeholders were the manufacturing engineers whose workaround was the evidence. [SAFE - honesty is the point]
2. How did you build the case before saying anything? - Pulled 20 BOMs with pin-count mismatches, categorized every case into three buckets. Saying "pin count is unreliable" is a preference; showing larger-footprint cases are almost always legitimate variation is a finding people can act on. [SAFE]
3. Why not just delete pin count from the comparison? - It's the signal that catches the physically impossible: a 16-pin part on an 8-pad footprint (sixteen legs over only eight solder dots - the part simply cannot fit). Deleting it would trade false alarms for missed wrong parts. The push was to make the signal discriminating, not to silence it. [SAFE]
4. How did you pick the threshold? - From the 20 files: legitimate larger-footprint variations mostly ran 2:1 or under - at most about twice as many pads as pins. Thin base, so it's a configurable default, not a verdict; borderline ratios (files sitting right near the cutoff) go to review and the engineer who knows the board decides. Residual risk: a heuristic (rough rule of thumb) can still miss a mismatch that satisfies the type-and-ratio rules (right kind of part, pad-to-pin ratio within the cutoff) - stated openly, mitigated by the debug view. [SAFE]
5. How was 12% -> under 2% measured? - Honestly: my own categorization of the 20 failing BOMs plus a 15-BOM regression suite (a fixed pile of 15 known parts lists re-run to confirm nothing that worked before broke) re-run after the fix. In plain terms the two percentages count wrong red flags: about 12 in every 100 pin-count flags cried wolf before the change, fewer than 2 in every 100 after, on the 20 problem files I had. No held-out set (no fresh cases held back from development to score against fairly), no labeled ground truth (no pile where a person has confirmed each case's right-or-wrong answer) - a direction of travel on the files I had, not a production rate. Path to rigor: label ~200 components as ground truth (have a person confirm each as right or wrong), pick the operating point (the chosen cutoff) off a precision-recall curve (a chart trading "how many of my flags are wrong" against "how many real mismatches I'd miss" at every possible cutoff). [PARTIAL - self-categorized sample]
6. How did you know the fix didn't break working boards? - Full 15-BOM regression (the fixed 15-file test pile re-run whole), 5 with pin-count issues: the 5 came out correct, the other 10 unchanged, no previously-matching component flipped. A fix that breaks the good cases isn't a fix. Safety net on files I had, not certification on unseen boards. [PARTIAL]
7. Why push for the debug view - wasn't the rule fix enough? - No, and that's the part I'm most deliberate about. The engineers didn't just need the rule changed; they needed to see why any call was made. Raw entry, parsed reference after each step, layout reference found, rule applied, override available - in plain words: what the tool read, what it decided it meant at each step, which pad pattern it matched in the layout, which rule fired, and a manual veto. That's what turned "the computer said so" into something they'd rely on for production verification. [SAFE]
8. Did the debug view find anything wrong after you shipped it? - Yes - within the first week it surfaced edge cases my heuristic got wrong, both unusual component naming conventions (parts written under label styles the tool hadn't seen). I treated those as the tool's (and my) bugs to fix, not the engineers' problem to route around. That response is part of why the trust stuck. [SAFE]
9. Same event as your failure story / your challenge story - which one is it? - It's one event with more than one true face. Told as a failure, it's my wrong assumption about pin count; as a challenge, the layered fix; here, the advocacy - building the case, proposing the rule, pushing transparency through to adoption. The facts and numbers are identical across all three; only the lens changes. I won't pretend these are different episodes. [SAFE - consistency is the defense]
10. Where's the line between pushing and not letting go? - Push once, with evidence, on the finding and the customer risk; once the rule is set with the residual risk documented and an override in the user's hands, the judgment belongs to the engineer - nothing left to relitigate. The test I use: do I have new data, or just a preference? [SAFE - method line; the disagree-and-commit story itself lives in Q17/Q6]
11. Show me the code. - Don't: the story doc's file mapping for this fix (bom_parser.js / debug endpoint - a hidden web address inside the tool that exposes its inner workings) doesn't match the repo snapshot. Present at story-documentation level only. Same caveat as Q4/Q52. [GAP]
12. What would you do differently? - Collect diverse sample BOMs from the floor before writing any rule, instead of discovering edge cases one production file at a time; treat a matching signal as a hypothesis until it's held across varied samples - and know the better signal is reference designator plus footprint name (the part's own label on the board, plus the name of its pad pattern). Build the debug view from day one; it belonged there before the pushback, not after. [SAFE]
