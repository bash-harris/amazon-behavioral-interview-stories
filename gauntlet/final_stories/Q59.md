## Core Answer

I don't have a documented moment where someone handed me one role and I deliberately did a different job. I was the sole builder and architect of the PCB inspection suite - PCBs are the green circuit boards inside electronic devices, and my suite checks photos of finished boards for assembly mistakes - and since I built and designed it alone, the charter was already wide. I won't relabel assigned work as "beyond my role."

So here's the answer I can actually defend. I take responsibility at the outcome level, not the ticket level (a ticket = one task formally assigned to me). A role is a list of tasks; responsibility is whatever sits between those tasks and the user getting real value. Where that chain had a hole and nobody owned it, I treated it as mine. Three holes, briefly.

The model needed labels - example photos with the right answers marked on them, so an AI can learn from them and be scored - and there were none. I built a keyboard-driven labeling tool (a small helper app where you mark answers using the keyboard) instead of treating "no dataset" as a blocker - about 100 minutes for 2,000 images, versus an estimated ten hours doing it in a spreadsheet. In plain terms: the 2,000 is the number of board photos that needed answers marked; the ~100 minutes is my self-reported timing and the ten hours is my rough guess at the slow way - neither was a measured study. The tool itself was on my task list; refusing to let the missing data stall the model was the move.

The engine - the checking program itself - worked; the engineers still didn't trust it. Nobody's ticket said "make them believe the verdict," so I built a debug view: a screen that shows *why* the tool reached each decision, not just the decision. In their first week they caught two naming edge cases I'd gotten wrong - two odd cases where a verdict came out wrong about a part's name - through the tool, not by hand.

The third one I own hardest. After shipping, I audited my own numbers and found my detection thresholds had never been calibrated. A threshold is the cutoff that decides when a difference in a photo counts as a defect, and "calibrated" means set from measured evidence rather than by feel. I'd tuned them by eye until the false positives - false alarms, where the tool flags something that's actually fine - stopped annoying me, which quietly trades away recall - how many real defects actually get caught - and in PCB QA a missed defect ships. Nobody assigned that fix. I've written it down as the next thing I own: label a few hundred components (mark their right answers), plot precision against recall, and pick the operating point from what a miss actually costs. Precision is how often an alarm is a real problem; the operating point is just where on that trade-off between misses and false alarms you choose to run.

That's my honest answer: I owned the data, the trust, and the measurement, because the outcome needed them - not because a task list named them. What I won't claim is that any of it was formally outside my role, because on the record it wasn't.
