# Q61 - a time when you had a conflict with a team-mate or someone

**LP:** Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit (primary), Earn Trust (touch) - the peer-conflict half of this question is an acknowledged GAP, not a story   **Story:** the `R1||R2` variant-semantics disagreement with the manufacturing engineer (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 1), labeled stakeholder-not-teammate, plus an explicitly labeled peer framework

## Core Answer

I'll be straight about the shape of this: I built the PCB suite solo. PCB means printed circuit board - the small green board inside nearly every electronic device - and my suite was the set of tools that checks finished boards against their design files. I was the only engineer on it, so there was no teammate to clash with, and I'm not going to invent one. "Have Backbone" is an Amazon leadership principle - holding your ground and respectfully challenging a decision rather than going along - and it's the thinnest principle in my record. What I actually have is one documented disagreement - with a manufacturing engineer, so a stakeholder, someone whose work depends on the tool, not a peer - and a method I'd bring to a teammate conflict, which is a labeled framework, not an event.

The real one was about data semantics: what the characters in a board's files actually mean. My parser - the code that reads the parts and design files - treated references like `R1||R2` as malformed, as if they were written wrong, and dropped them quietly: the entries vanished from the check without any warning. The manufacturing engineer said those weren't errors - one physical position can accept either part, depending on the board variant, meaning different versions of the same board. My first move was to push back, on principle: I don't normalize data I don't understand - I won't rewrite it just to make it look tidy - because a guess inside a verification tool becomes a silent false match: the tool approving something that doesn't actually match. He didn't argue harder; we opened the actual board files together. He was right and my parser was losing real references - genuine part entries were disappearing from the verification. I rebuilt the splitter - the code that takes a combined label like that and breaks it into its separate part options - to handle both symbols people used to write "or" with, and I retired my original handling completely - no private fallback, no hidden back door keeping my old behavior alive, and no relitigating after.

That's the part I'd want judged as backbone in both directions: I held the principle long enough to get the reasoning instead of nodding along, then changed the code when the domain evidence - his knowledge of how the boards are actually made - beat my assumption instead of defending it. The customer - an engineer trying to verify boards correctly - outranked my parser looking tidy.

For a genuine peer conflict, the method, prepared not lived: name the shared goal before the disagreement. Bring a failing case, not a preference - a concrete example that breaks, not an opinion; "I think that's wrong" isn't a disagreement. Separate reversible calls from values calls - a reversible call is a decision you can change later at low cost, a values call is a line you can't walk back. On a reversible call, disagree once, directly, privately, then commit fully; on a safety, compliance, or trust line, escalate with documentation rather than fake agreement. And commit visibly - implement the decision as if it were mine.

What I won't pretend the framework covers: the repair after a heated argument - knowing whether the other person felt heard. I've done the technical-disagreement mechanics; I have less practice on that part, and I'd rather say so than pad the story.
