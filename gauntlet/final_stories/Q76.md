## Core Answer

**(a) The conflict.** The honest label first: my record has no peer conflict, and I'm not going to stage one. "Peer conflict" means a real disagreement with a coworker. I built the inspection suite alone - nobody sat beside me to clash with. (The suite checks finished circuit boards - the green boards inside electronic devices - against their design files.) What I do have is two real disagreements. One was a correction: my parser - the code that reads the parts list - dropped references like `R1||R2`, a board-file way of writing "either of these two parts fits in this one spot," and it treated that as malformed, or badly written. A manufacturing engineer - the person who runs the production line - explained that one physical position takes either part, depending on board variant. He knew the floor; I didn't; I rewrote the splitter. The other was aimed at a rule, not a person: literal pin-count matching - checking that a part's pin count exactly equals the design's, pin = the metal leg a component plugs in with, and a "footprint" = the pattern of pads on the board it mounts to - cried wolf on legitimate footprints, flagging good parts as mismatches. A BOM is the board's parts list. So I sorted twenty real BOMs - actual parts lists from production, a count from my own working record, not an instrumented study - into three buckets and rebuilt the measurement on that. My method for a future peer conflict is the same instinct, labeled as prepared, not lived: say it once, face to face, data and correctness risk on the table; ask what would change their mind; once the call lands, own it - no hallway relitigating. The scene itself is the gap.

**(b) The comfort zone.** This part is real, and the stretch wasn't a new library - it was letting go of my own tool. The engineers used the reconciliation reports but didn't trust them: "reconciliation" means cross-checking the parts list against what the board files say should be there, and the verdict - "this part matches" or "it doesn't" - showed up without its reasoning, so anything that mattered got re-checked by hand - the exact work I'd automated away. The comfortable fix was to quietly tune accuracy and announce it when better. Instead I exposed the reasoning and handed over the authority: a debug view - switched on by typing `?debug=true` onto the tool's web address, which shows the step-by-step reasoning behind each verdict - showing, per component, the raw BOM text, the parse after every cleanup step, the layout entry it matched, and the rule that fired - plus an override, so the engineer's call beat mine. That's the uncomfortable part: putting my heuristics - my code's best-guess rules - on the floor, in front of the people whose job depends on catching me wrong. They did - two naming edge cases in the first week, meaning two parts whose names my rules mishandled; the engineers reported them through the new view, both mine, both fixed. Then the hand-checking faded. The lesson: transparency belongs in the first commit - the first saved version of the code - not after the trust is lost.

**(c) The recent project.** Briefly, because the other two parts carry this question. Newest is personal: Tab Scroller, a Chrome extension - a small add-on installed in the Chrome browser - for running tabs in plain English on a cloud or local model (you type what you want done to your open browser tabs in everyday words, and AI carries it out; the AI runs either as an internet service or on your own computer). Deepest is still the three-tool PCB suite for the factory floor. I tell both in full elsewhere - name either one and I'll go where you point.
