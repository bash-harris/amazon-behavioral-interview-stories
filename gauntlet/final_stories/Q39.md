# Q39 - tell me about a time when you had a conflict with a team member, tell me about a time when you stepped out of your comfort zone, tell me about a project you recently worked on and what the...

**LP:** (a) Have Backbone; Earn Trust - GAP-bridge; (b) Learn and Be Curious; (c) Deliver Results; Invent and Simplify   **Story:** (a) no peer conflict documented - labeled bridge per Q10/Q22; (b) the five-day prototype demo (STORY 8 axis, not Q21/Q71's); (c) PCB suite at work + Tab Scroller at home, inverted from Q94's framing

## Core Answer

**(a) The conflict.** Straight answer first: I don't have a documented conflict with a teammate I can stand behind. I built the inspection suite solo - no teammate across the table in the record - and I won't invent one. The closest true things: a manufacturing engineer corrected my parser (the code that reads a board's parts list and breaks it into pieces) - I'd dropped `R1||R2` variant references as invalid (a part written `R1||R2` means one spot that can hold either of two interchangeable components; that's a valid way to write a parts list); he was right about the floor, so I rewrote the splitter (the piece that splits a combined label like that into its parts) to match either reference. And I pushed back on a measurement rule, not a person: literal pin-count matching (comparing the number of electrical connection points a part lists against the design) flagged normal package variants (the same component sold in different physical housings), so I sorted twenty production BOMs (bill-of-materials files - a board's parts list) into three buckets and replaced the rule with that. My framework for a real peer conflict is the same reflex: raise it once, directly, with data; argue the finding, not the person; ask what evidence would change the outcome; then commit fully and not relitigate sideways. The lived event itself is a gap, and I treat it as homework.

**(b) The comfort zone.** The uncomfortable part wasn't technical - it was being seen doing it. I'm fine alone with a hard problem; putting unfinished work in front of people is not my default. My first browser inspection tool sat in a backlog six months as "too complex to scope" (too big to even define the work). Instead of arguing scope, I spent five days on a working prototype and demoed it half-finished. The team found three real problems in the first hour - more than weeks of polishing alone would have caught. Two weeks later it was production-ready. The scariest single technical moment was coding a warping algorithm (a formula that bends one image so it lines up with a photo taken at an angle) straight out of a 1989 paper because no library (ready-made code someone else had already written) existed - but the habit that actually changed was demoing unfinished work, early and on purpose.

**(c) The recent project.** Most recent at work: the PCB inspection suite (a PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device) - three browser-based tools I built solo in eight weeks: BOM reconciliation (checking the parts list against what's actually placed on the board), board-photo-versus-design comparison, and a vision model (an AI program that looks at images) reading mismatch reports. Locked-down factory machines, no installs, no network - so it shipped as one offline HTML file (a single web page you just open in a browser), no server, no IT approval. About 15 engineers across 2 sites used it; they told me it saved roughly 20 hours a week - reported, not measured. Most recent overall is personal: Tab Scroller, a Chrome extension (an add-on for the Chrome browser) where you control tabs in plain English on a cloud or local model (an AI that runs on someone else's servers over the internet, or on your own computer). Happy to go deep on either.
