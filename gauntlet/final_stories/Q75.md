# Q75 - tell me about a time you made a mistake in a data pipeline / a data quality issue and how you resolved it

**LP:** Dive Deep (primary - traced wrong outputs to the input distribution, not the model); Earn Trust (no blame on the cameras or the photographers); Deliver Results (the fix lived in the pipeline, where every tool benefits)   **Story:** the defect classifier that learned factory lighting instead of component features - a data-quality mistake in my preprocessing pipeline, fixed with CLAHE normalization (behavioral/AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md:345-347); distinct from Q7 (the class-imbalance failure, a different pipeline mistake) and from the classifier training/holdout tellings (Q11/Q44/Q65 own those numbers)

## Core Answer

The mistake was in the picture pipeline, not in the model. I trained a defect classifier - the piece of my inspection suite that looks at a photo of a board component and names what's wrong with it - and its errors on factory photos had a signature: the same defect read correctly under one lamp and wrongly under another. [PARTIAL - the pattern is my account; no per-lighting scorecard exists in the record]

So I went after the data instead of the model. The photos came from different benches under different light - fluorescent tubes, LED panels, shadows from the inspection equipment. My preprocessing step had been the simplest thing that could work: resize the crop, feed it to the model. That left every batch carrying a brightness fingerprint correlated with where and when the pictures were taken. A model maximizing its score on that data takes the cheapest available signal, and it did exactly that: some of what it learned as "defective" was really "photographed under the LED bay." The labels were fine; the inputs carried a tell.

The fix was a preprocessing step called CLAHE - Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization. In plain words, instead of one global brightness tweak that blows out regions that were already bright, it divides the image into small tiles, boosts local contrast tile by tile, and caps the boost so it can't amplify noise. I applied it to every input at training and inference - the same normalization both sides of the pipeline, or the fix is a lie. After that, the lighting-difference pattern stopped showing up the way it had started - my observation in normal use, not a measured before/after, and I say it that way. [GAP - no lighting-broken-down accuracy number exists]

The judgment worth owning is what I didn't do: I resisted the two attractive moves - retraining harder and collecting more data. When the input distribution carries a nuisance signal, more examples from the same benches just deepens the rut. Normalization removes the tell directly, and it cost one pipeline step instead of a labeling project.

The rule I kept from my own mistake: data quality isn't just correct labels - it's the boring physical conditions, light and angle and focus, too. A model will always take the shortcut that works; my job in the pipeline is to make sure there are no shortcuts hiding in the inputs.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: my classifier's errors tracked lighting, not defects - raw photos from fluorescent / LED / shadowed benches let the model learn "how it was lit" as a feature
- Customer: the engineers relying on inspection verdicts; a light-dependent classifier quietly flips them between benches
- Insight: the labels were correct; the input distribution carried a confound - the pipeline normalized geometry but not illumination
- Action (mine): diagnosed via the error signature; added CLAHE tile-wise contrast normalization (capped to avoid noise amplification) as a mandatory preprocessing step on every input at training and inference
- Result: the same-defect/different-lamp pattern stopped reproducing in use (behavioral observation, not a percentage [GAP]); the fix sits in preprocessing, upstream of the model
- Learning: before retraining or collecting more data, ask what nuisance signal the inputs are offering the model; remove the tell, don't out-label it

Standard technique; my use is both sides of the pipeline. [SAFE]
