# Q42 - tell me about a time where you received critical feedback, how did you implement it?

**LP:** Earn Trust (primary), Learn and Be Curious - emphasis on implementation and verification, distinct from Q18 (receiving non-defensively) and Q62 (one durable process change)   **Story:** the five-day prototype and the one-hour team review (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md STORY 8); fix details from STORY 2/3/5/6 (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md)

## Core Answer

My lead put a five-day prototype of a board-comparison tool - a tool that checks a photo of a finished circuit board against its design drawing - in front of the manufacturing team. "Five days" is how long I spent building it, from my own account of the work, not a timed study. Within an hour they came back with three critical problems - that's the demo meeting's length and the number of issues they raised, again my own recollection. This is about the changes and how each got checked.

Context in one line: engineers had wanted an automated PCB comparison tool for months - a PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device; it kept stalling as too complex to scope. So I built instead: one monolithic HTML file - a single web-page file with all its logic written directly inside it, so it runs in a browser with no server and no installs - with a basic pixel comparison: the two images matched dot against dot, marking every place the dots differ. It worked under clean conditions and was rough everywhere else. The three hits were real - pixel comparison broke under lighting changes (a shadow or a reflection showed up as a difference), no alignment (the photo and the drawing weren't lined up on top of each other), so boards had to be photographed at the exact same angle, and no export (results couldn't be saved or emailed as files), so results couldn't leave the screen.

I accepted all three, but implemented them in my order, not the order they were listed. Alignment first - without it no comparison on a real photo can be trusted. Illumination second - most frequent complaint. Export third - valuable but not blocking inspection.

What actually changed, fix by fix. Alignment: a two-stage correction - a homography to correct global perspective (homography is the math that squares up a photo taken at an angle into a head-on view; global perspective means the overall camera tilt), then a Thin Plate Spline warp - which bends the image the way a flexible sheet bends - for the local board flex a single global correction can't reach. Average misalignment: 15-20 pixels to 2-3. A pixel is one dot on screen, and this counts how far a feature on the aligned photo still misses its true spot on the drawing; it's measured on a controlled test of 20 board photos I ran myself - real measurements, small targeted sample. Illumination: I applied CLAHE - contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization; in plain words, it automatically evens out the brightness and contrast across each area of an image, like making two photos taken under different lamps look alike - to both images before the comparison, so lighting stopped registering as a difference. Both fixes went into shared preprocessing modules - a common first-pass step built once and reused - so every tool in the suite inherited them. Export: PNG and PDF output - the two standard file formats (image and document) engineers could save and send.

Then how I knew they landed - shipping isn't landing. I measured the alignment against ground-truth markers on a set of board photos - small targets placed at known positions, like survey benchmarks, so the true answer is known by construction. Engineers overlaid the aligned photo on the layout and eyeballed the pads - the little metal dots where components solder to the board - which catches a good number hiding a subtly wrong warp. I kept a fixed regression set of known boards and files - a stored list of familiar cases with their correct results, re-run after every change so one fix can't quietly break another. And the behavioral signal: engineers reported it now caught mismatches on warped boards that used to false-alarm - their word, not instrumented counts. Production-ready in two weeks - the 5 prototype days plus 9 fix days, from my own account of the schedule.

What stuck: a debug view on a later tool came too late, so now I build observability - a built-in window showing what the tool is doing and why - from day one. And on the next tool's problems I collected failing cases and fixed patterns, not symptoms. What I implemented wasn't an apology - it was three fixes and a check on each.

## Memory Anchors

- Problem: rough 5-day prototype -> three critical issues in a one-hour team review; the question is implementation + proof, not reception
- Customer: manufacturing engineers taking real factory photos; false alarms on warped boards were the live cost
- Insight: accept all three, but sequence by dependency, not by listing order - alignment gates every credible comparison
- Action (mine): homography + TPS alignment (angle-squaring math + flexible-sheet bend correction); CLAHE (brightness/contrast leveling) in shared preprocessing; PNG/PDF export; per-fix verification (20-image ground-truth test against known-position markers, engineer pad overlay, fixed regression set)
- Result: misalignment 15-20 px -> 2-3 px (measured 2.1 px avg / 0.8 sd - sd = standard deviation, the typical spread around that average - on 20 images, my own controlled test); warped-board false alarms now reported caught; production-ready in two weeks (5 + 9)
- Learning: shipping isn't landing - every fix needs its own measured check; observability from day one; fix patterns, not symptoms

## Follow-Up Bank

1. How did you pick the order? - Impact and complaint frequency. Alignment gates everything - without it no comparison on a re-shot photo is trustworthy. Illumination was the most frequent complaint. Export is valuable but doesn't block inspection. The team set the problems; I owned the sequencing. [SAFE]
2. What alternatives did you reject? - Fixing them in the listed order (export first would have polished a tool whose comparisons still couldn't be trusted), and retreating to more scoping - six months of that had already failed. [SAFE]
3. What did the alignment fix actually take? - Two stages of math. Homography with RANSAC - RANSAC is a fitting method that ignores outlier bad matches - corrects global perspective: tilt, off-perpendicular shots (a photo not aimed straight down at the board). But boards flex non-uniformly in the reflow oven - the oven that solders parts onto the board, whose heat warps them - so I added a Thin Plate Spline warp fitted through control points at board corners, fiducials (small reference marks machines use to locate the board), and component pads (the metal dots parts solder to), rejecting matches below a template-similarity threshold - a cut-off for how closely a candidate match must look like the expected picture to count. Handles up to ~30 deg off-perpendicular and ~3 mm of warp - the most camera tilt and physical bend the corrections held up against on the controlled test images. [SAFE]
4. What did CLAHE change, concretely? - It normalizes local contrast in both images before comparison - it brightens and darkens each area of both pictures separately so they look lit the same way, like equalizing two photos taken under different lamps. The diff had been keying on shadows and light level; after it, the comparison saw meaningful differences - like a wrong-value component with slightly different markings. It went into shared preprocessing, so all three tools got it, not just this one. [SAFE]
5. How do you know 2.1 pixels is good? - It's a controlled check: 20 board images with ground-truth markers (targets at known positions, so the right answer is known by design), average offset 2.1 px - how far a spot on the aligned photo still misses its true location, one pixel being one dot on screen - sd 0.8 - the typical spread around that average; most results land within roughly 1-2 px of 2.1 - my own harness, not CI (CI = the team's automated test pipeline). The operational backing is that aligned-board comparison accuracy moved from ~85% to >98% - the share of checks where the comparison calls "same" vs "different" correctly, before vs after alignment - and the engineers' eyeball overlay caught cases where the metric looked fine but the warp was subtly wrong. Small, targeted sample - I'd say that before anyone pressed it. [PARTIAL - sample size labeled]
6. What exactly was the regression set? - The same known boards and BOM files (BOM = bill of materials, the board's parts list), run through the tool after each change and compared against stored outputs. A fixed personal harness, deliberately small and boring - good enough to catch one fix silently breaking another. [SAFE - self-built, labeled]
7. Did you disagree with any of the feedback? - Not on substance; all three were real. My only pushback was on ordering - the list treated export as co-equal, and I moved it last because it didn't block inspection. [SAFE]
8. Wasn't this just the team's list? - They set the problems; I owned the solutions and the proof. Anyone can accept a critique - the job is converting it into changes that survive contact with real boards: method choice, sequencing, the 2.1-pixel validation, the regression habit. [SAFE]
9. Hardest part? - The alignment math. One transform was demonstrably not enough; the two-stage fit with control-point matching and the similarity threshold is where the hours went. [SAFE]
10. How did you measure the fixes overall? - Per fix: alignment got a quantitative check (20-image ground-truth, 2.1 px / 0.8 sd - average miss distance and its spread, on a small controlled set I ran) plus the engineer overlay; illumination and export got the regression set plus the engineers' report of behavior change - warped boards that used to false-alarm now read correctly. No single dashboard; three checks matched to three changes. [PARTIAL - behavioral reports are engineer-reported, not instrumented]
11. Larger scale? - The fixes lived in shared modules from day one, so the other two tools inherited alignment and CLAHE without their own fix rounds - three tools, one preprocessing core. That's also why nine days covered everything. [SAFE]
12. What would you do differently? - Build observability in from day one instead of bolting a debug view on later, and on the next tool's illumination, rotation, and scale problems I ran the collect-failing-cases-then-fix-the-pattern loop from the start instead of symptom by symptom. [SAFE]
13. Was the critique from your lead personally? - Same honest channel note as my other tellings of this event: I demoed to my lead, he shared it with the team immediately, the team produced the three issues within the hour. I won't put a personal one-on-one critique on the record that isn't there. What I'm walking through here is the implementation, not the reception. [SAFE - premise labeled]
