# Q20 - a time when you needed to deep dive to solve a problem

**LP:** Dive Deep (primary); touches Are Right, A Lot and Insist on the Highest Standards.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the image-alignment pipeline (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 2, canonical alignment telling; STORY 12/13 excluded).

## Core Answer

The problem looked small: alignment was off, so the PCB-versus-layout comparison tool started crying false alarms. (A PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device; the "layout" is its design drawing; the tool lines the photo up over the drawing and flags every spot where the two disagree. A "false alarm" is the tool crying wolf - flagging a board that's actually fine because the two images aren't lined up. "Alignment" just means getting photo and drawing to land point-on-point.) The tool flags a mismatch at a ten-pixel photo-vs-layout disagreement - a pixel is one dot of the image, so ten dots apart fires a flag - if alignment drifts more than a few pixels, every result downstream is noise. The symptom was "alignment is bad." The deep dive was finding out *why*.

My first wrong assumption was the textbook one: match features, fit a transform - start with affine. (A "transform" is a math recipe for moving one image onto the other; "affine" is the simplest family of recipes.) Six numbers: rotation, scaling, translation, shear - turn, resize, shift, slant. It worked on flat boards shot straight-on and failed on real photos. So I asked - not "how do I fit it better?" but "why can't affine fit this at all?" The answer was in the camera model - the geometry of how a tilted photo flattens a real scene. Affine preserves parallel lines. Photograph a board at an angle and its parallel edges converge toward a vanishing point, like a straight road's edges meeting at the horizon. Perspective breaks parallelism, so no affine matrix can undo it. Wrong tool, not bad tuning. I switched to a homography fit with RANSAC - a perspective model that votes out outlier feature matches, so a few bad correspondences can't corrupt the matrix. (A homography is the warp recipe that includes camera tilt, letting parallel lines converge the way they do in a photo. RANSAC is a voting trick: rebuild the warp from a handful of matched points, many times over, and keep whichever version the most other points agree with - so wrong matches get voted out.)

That fixed the global case, but real photos still averaged fifteen to twenty pixels of misalignment - still 15-20 dots off, far past the ten-pixel flag rule. Second wrong assumption, mine: that any single global transform - one recipe applied identically to the whole image - was the end state. The physical detail that broke it - boards pass through a reflow oven (the oven that solders the parts down), and the heat bows them unevenly, the center differently from the edges. A global transform structurally can't fix a local bow. So I added a non-rigid stage - a warp that can bend different regions by different amounts. I compared elastic deformation, B-splines, and thin-plate splines (three bendable-warp families: one simulates real material bending, one is steered by an adjustable grid of points, one bends like a least-effort thin sheet), and picked TPS: it minimizes bending energy (the warp bends as little as its anchor points force - the smoothest fit possible), is deterministic given the control points (the anchor points where the warp is pinned; same anchors in, exactly the same warp out), needs no material constants that have no clear meaning for a board, and OpenCV's implementation is mature (OpenCV = the standard free image-processing toolbox). Control points came from template-matching known features - corners, fiducials, pads - (template matching = slide a small known picture across the photo and take where it overlaps best; fiducials = the reference dots the manufacturer prints on the board for machines to line up on; pads = the little solder dots where a component's legs attach) rejecting weak matches, because one bad point drags the whole warp.

Then I measured instead of eyeballing. My controlled test: twenty board images with markers at known positions - align each, measure the offsets afterward. (An exam I built for my own tool: I already knew each marker's true spot, so after alignment I could count exactly how many dots it landed off.) Average 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8 - typical error about two dots, and the 0.8 spread means the errors clustered tightly: consistent, not one lucky run. The engineers overlaid the aligned photo on the layout and spot-checked pads - that caught cases where a good number hid a subtly wrong warp.

Far-edge misalignment came down from fifteen to twenty pixels to two or three - out at the board's edges, where the bowing bites hardest, the photo now landed within 2-3 dots of the drawing. And the false alarms stopped: with the images truly lined up, the tool quit flagging misalignment as if it were a defect. The lesson: "alignment is bad" was never one problem - it was two, each needing its own root cause.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: Comparison tool firing false alarms (flags on fine boards caused by sloppy photo-vs-drawing lining-up); symptom "alignment is bad"; the 10-pixel mismatch threshold (a 10-image-dot disagreement fires a flag) means alignment must land within a few pixels or every downstream result is noise.
- Customer: Manufacturing engineers using the PCB-vs-layout comparison on the factory floor.
- Insight: Two dropped assumptions, both on evidence - affine (the simplest warp math) structurally can't represent perspective (camera tilt: it preserves parallel lines), and a global transform (one stretch-and-shift for the whole image) structurally can't fix non-uniform reflow warping (uneven heat-bending from the soldering oven - physics, not tuning).
- Action (mine): Replaced affine with a RANSAC homography (tilt-aware warp with outlier voting) myself; added the TPS stage (least-bend flexible warp) after it; template-matched control points (corners, fiducials, pads) with similarity-threshold rejection (drop any match scoring below a minimum overlap); built the 20-image ground-truth test (markers whose true positions are the known answer key); kept engineers' overlay spot-checks as the safety net.
- Result: 15-20 px -> 2-3 px (far-edge photo-vs-drawing error before vs after, in image dots); 2.1 px avg / sd 0.8 on the 20-image test (my own measured exam; sd = spread around the average, so errors were tightly clustered); comparison accuracy ~85% -> >98% on moderate warping (how often the tool's right-or-wrong verdicts come out correct, before vs after - a documented outcome, not something I instrumented); pipeline handles up to ~30 deg off-perpendicular (camera tilt from straight overhead) and ~3 mm warp (how tall the board's bend is).
- Learning: Root-cause the symptom before reaching for a better fit; start with the simplest matching that works - board-outline contours (tracing just the board's outer edge) beat full SURF (a heavyweight landmark-matching algorithm) for most boards; name the boundary - beyond ~5 mm warp, TPS still fails.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. Affine versus perspective, mathematically? - In plain words first: affine can turn, resize, shift and slant an image but always keeps parallel lines parallel; perspective additionally lets them converge, which is exactly what a tilted photo does. Affine maps (x,y) to (ax+by+e, cx+dy+f): linear plus translation, six parameters, preserves parallel lines. Perspective divides by (gx+hy+1): eight parameters, parallel lines can converge to a vanishing point - exactly what an angled photo does. [SAFE]
2. How does RANSAC work here, and why four points? - Randomly draw 4 point pairs, fit a homography, count how many other pairs agree within a pixel tolerance - the agreeing ones are the "inliers," points voting for this candidate warp - repeat thousands of times, keep the model with the most inliers. The homography has 8 unknowns and each pair gives 2 constraints, so 4 is the minimum - 3 is underdetermined (too few clues for a unique answer). I ran OpenCV's default 2000 iterations - 2000 random attempts; theory says ~370 suffice at a 50% outlier rate, i.e. if half the matches are bad - the extra cost on the order of 50 ms, a twentieth of a second, bought robustness insurance. [SAFE]
3. Why TPS over elastic deformation or B-splines? - In plain terms: elastic deformation simulates bending like a real material, B-splines steer a smooth surface with a grid of adjustable points, TPS bends like a least-effort thin sheet. Elastic needs physical constants I don't have and that have no clear meaning for a warped composite board. B-splines add a control-grid resolution - another knob. TPS minimizes bending energy - it bends as little as the anchors force - is deterministic given the control points (same anchors in, exactly the same warp out), and OpenCV's implementation is mature. [SAFE - wording locked; naming specific material parameters traces to the excluded telling]
4. How many control points, and what if one is badly matched? - A 2D warp needs at least 4 - four anchors is the bare minimum to pin down a flat stretch; with only 4 there's no redundancy to average out noise (no spare points for mistakes to cancel against), so in practice I typically found 15-30 spread across the board. A wrong point pulls the field - the bend-map - toward the wrong position - hence template matching on known features and rejecting matches below a similarity threshold, a minimum score for how closely the small picture must overlap the photo. [SAFE]
5. Why not a learned registration model? - A network predicting a displacement field (a map telling every pixel where to move) needs misaligned/aligned pairs with ground-truth correspondences - an answer key saying which spot in one image matches which in the other; I had no such data, and collecting it was its own project. TPS is classical - zero training data. With thousands of labeled pairs a learned model might win under extreme warp; real alternative, would revisit. [SAFE]
6. How were the numbers measured, and how much do you trust them? - The offsets are my own harness - a small test rig I built: 20 board images, markers at known positions, pixel offsets measured after alignment - average 2.1 px, sd 0.8 (the sd, standard deviation, is the spread around the average: errors clustered tightly around two dots). Small-n - only 20 samples - self-built, single-run: directional, not production telemetry (not data from real daily use). That's exactly why the engineers' overlay spot-checks stayed in - they caught a good-number-hides-a-subtly-wrong-warp case. The ~85% -> >98% comparison accuracy - how often the tool's verdicts come out right, both catching real problems and not crying wolf - is the documented before/after for the moderate-warping regime, the everyday-bend range this story covers; the per-image offsets are what I instrumented. [PARTIAL - offsets measured, accuracy documented outcome]
7. Where did the 2-3 pixel target come from? - Forced by the spec - the tool's own flag rule - not a wish: the comparison flags mismatches at a 10-pixel threshold, so alignment accuracy within a few pixels is the requirement before a single flag means anything (if the images still sit several dots out of line, a 10-pixel flag can't tell misalignment from a real defect). [SAFE]
8. What still fails? - Severe warping beyond roughly 5 mm - a visibly tall bend: control points can't capture that much deformation; that would need a more physically based model, and it's rare in production. Past about 45 deg the feature matching itself breaks - the software can't find enough correct landmark pairs to even start aligning - the shipped pipeline handled up to 30 deg off-perpendicular (tilt away from straight overhead) and ~3 mm of visible warp (how tall the board's bend is). I name the boundary rather than imply universality. [SAFE]
9. Any bug you found by auditing your own code? - Yes. I used a single element of the homography matrix as a proxy for local scale - only valid under translation and uniform scale, wrong under rotation or shear, which is precisely what a homography models. In plain words: I read "how much the image is zoomed at this spot" off one number of the warp matrix, which is only correct when the image was merely shifted or evenly resized - never when it's tilted. The correct local scale is the square root of the determinant of the Jacobian of the projective map at that point - the standard math for measuring stretch at a point on a warped map. I found it by reading my own code, not from output - the output looked fine. [SAFE]
10. What would you do differently? - I implemented full SURF feature matching - a heavyweight algorithm that finds distinctive landmarks across images - before discovering simple board-outline contour detection, just tracing the board's outer edge, was sufficient for most boards - more general, but costlier and harder to debug. Start simple, add complexity only when it buys accuracy. [SAFE]
11. Any stakeholder disagreement during the dive? - Honest answer: no person-vs-person dispute - I built the suite solo. The friction was internal: my metric gate - my own rule that the numbers had to hit the target before I'd ship - against the temptation to ship an alignment that "mostly worked." I won't inflate that into a stakeholder fight. [PARTIAL - none documented]
12. Wasn't the homography fix enough, since it solved the real perspective case? - No - because it fixed the geometry I'd assumed was the whole problem, the camera tilt, and real photos still sat at 15-20 px - still image dots, still far past the 10-px flag rule. "Fixed the global case" - one recipe for the whole image - is not fixed on the factory floor; I held it to the target the 10-pixel threshold demanded instead of stopping at the plausible answer. [SAFE]
