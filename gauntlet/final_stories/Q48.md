# Q48 - tell me about a time when you had a blocker in your work and how did you resolve that blocker?

**LP:** Bias for Action (primary); Deliver Results   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the first EfficientNetV2S training run produced an unusable model (PREP STORY 14, backed by STORY 3). Training/dataset blocker - deliberately distinct from Q29's OpenCV.js deployment blocker; same event as Q11/Q44 retold here as blocker -> resolution.

## Core Answer

The blocker wasn't infrastructure - it was the deliverable itself. I was building the defect classifier for the PCB inspection suite - the PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device, and a "defect classifier" is a program that sorts a photo of one board part into one of a few labelled buckets: a cropped component photo in, a label out - good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing. The classifier was the heart of the tool, and everything downstream - the verification work, the ship date - sat behind it. My first training run gave me a model I couldn't use: 99% accuracy on training data, 72% on validation. In plain terms: "accuracy" is just the share of photos the model labels correctly; "training data" are the images it already studied during training, and "validation" is a practice set it checks itself against while training is still running - so it got 99 out of 100 right on work it had already seen, but only 72 out of 100 on fresh examples. The model was memorizing, not learning - textbook overfitting: it had memorized the answers instead of learning the rules. So the deliverable was blocked and the clock was running.

What I didn't do was retrain blindly at different settings, or grab a bigger model - a bigger model would have just memorized faster. I treated the gap itself as the diagnosis: 99 against 72 is what memorization looks like. That led me to the cause, which has a name - catastrophic forgetting. In plain words, I'd been fine-tuning - starting from an image model already trained on huge generic photo collections and continuing the training on my pictures - with the whole pre-trained network "open," meaning every borrowed layer was still allowed to change. The "classification head" is the new final layer that outputs my four labels, and a fresh head starts random, so early in training it fires big noisy gradients - the "you were wrong by this much" signals used to adjust a model - back into the pre-trained layers and overwrites the general features - edges, textures - that transfer learning (borrowing a trained model's eyes instead of teaching one from scratch) was supposed to give me for free. I was destroying the thing I was trying to reuse.

The fix was two-phase training: freeze the pre-trained base completely - lock the borrowed layers so they can't change - and train just the head until its gradients settled; then thaw the top layers of the base (unlock them again) at a much lower learning rate - the learning rate is the size of each adjustment step, so lower means gentler nudges - so they adapted to PCB images without being rewritten. I logged every run in Weights & Biases - an experiment notebook that records each training attempt so settings can be compared side by side - and measured instead of assuming - thaw too few layers and it underfits (doesn't learn enough), thaw too many and it overfits (memorizes again), and the middle was where it worked.

The unblock: 94% on a holdout of roughly 300 component images it had never seen - a holdout is the final exam: examples kept away from both the studying and the tuning, unlike the validation practice set the tuning process does peek at - so 94 out of 100 fresh photos judged correctly, with the gap between its training and validation numbers closed - that's what real generalization looks like: still right on material it has never met. The learning was that when the blocker *is* the result, bias for action doesn't mean guessing fast - it means moving fast on the cause, not the symptom.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: First classifier training run produced an unusable model - 99% training / 72% validation vs a >90% validation ship bar (the accuracy needed on the never-studied practice set before the tool was allowed to ship); the inspection deliverable stalled behind it.
- Customer: Manufacturing engineers; the inspection suite was live on the floor and the classifier was the heart of it.
- Insight: The 99/72 gap is the diagnosis - catastrophic forgetting: random head's early noisy gradients overwrite the borrowed pre-trained features (the new output layer starts knowing nothing; its early error signals damage the layers I was trying to borrow).
- Action (mine): Root-caused instead of retraining blind or scaling up the model; two-phase progressive unfreezing (frozen base + head, then thaw top layers at ~10x lower rate - adjustment steps shrunk tenfold); logged every run in W&B (the experiment notebook); found the thaw depth empirically (too few underfit, too many overfit).
- Result: 94% on the ~300-image holdout (final-exam images kept away from training and tuning alike); train-validation gap ~3 points; deliverable unblocked at the cost of roughly a week of schedule (estimate).
- Learning: When the blocker is the result itself, move fast on the cause - a flattering training number is not progress.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. What exactly was blocked? - The classifier was the core of the inspection deliverable; a model that memorizes can't be trusted on boards it hasn't seen - and a false negative means a defective board ships (the model calls a bad board good). The bar to ship was above 90% on validation, so nothing that consumed the classifier could move until it generalized - performed well on fresh examples, not just studied ones. [SAFE]
2. What was the exact recipe? - Phase one: whole EfficientNetV2S base frozen (the off-the-shelf image model I borrowed, locked so it can't change), train only the head, 10 epochs at 0.001 (an epoch is one full pass through all the training photos; 0.001 is the size of each adjustment step). Phase two: unfreeze the last 30 layers, 20 epochs at 0.0001 (10x lower), with early stopping at patience 5 (training stops by itself once the validation score stops improving for 5 rounds in a row). [SAFE]
3. Why two phases - couldn't you just lower the learning rate? - Lower rate (smaller adjustment steps) alone doesn't fix the ordering: the random head's gradients corrupt the base either way. Freezing while the head settles is what stops the forgetting; the 10x drop is what lets the thawed layers adapt without being rewritten. [SAFE]
4. How did you pick "30 layers" - was that luck? - I tested thawing fewer and more in logged W&B runs: too few underfit (didn't learn enough), too many overfit (memorized again) on my ~2,000 images, the middle validated best. Honest caveat: directional evidence across runs - I never measured run-to-run variance (how much the score moves when you repeat the exact same training twice - same recipe, different roll of the dice), so I defend it by mechanism plus the closed train/validation gap, not confidence intervals (a stated range showing how precise a number is). [PARTIAL]
5. Which set is the 94% on? Isn't there a "500-image test set" in your notes? - Yes, and I'd flag it. The 94% I lead with is the documented ~300-image holdout - the final-exam set, kept away from both training and tuning, unlike the validation practice set - 15% of ~2,000, split by component so the same part never appears on both sides (the split was done by physical component, not by photo, so two pictures of the same part can't sit on both sides of the exam). An older note says "500-image held-out test," which doesn't reconcile with that split; I keep them apart rather than blending. [GAP - disclose, don't merge]
6. Per-class numbers? - What I stand behind is recall above 90% for every defect category - recall means, out of every 100 real instances of a defect, how many the model catches, and "per-class" means the bar holds for each defect type separately, not just on average. My notes carry exact decimals per class, but at this set size each defect class is only a couple dozen images - one sample moves a point (one photo flipping from right to wrong swings that class's percentage by roughly a point) - so I won't quote them as defensible. [PARTIAL]
7. Did it hit the speed bar too? - The bar was above 90% on validation and under 100 ms per image. Inference benchmarked ~45 ms on CPU (inference = the model judging one photo; ~45 ms is the machine's thinking time on a regular processor) - but that's a single-image benchmark, hardware unrecorded, and the shipped path ran in-browser under TensorFlow.js (a version of the model that runs inside an ordinary web browser, no installs), so I call it a benchmark, not a production floor. [PARTIAL]
8. Was this yours to solve or to escalate? - Solo build (I was the only engineer), so it was inside my own pipeline with a concrete path - escalating only adds delay. The version where I'd raise it: a blocker on input I don't control, say labeled boards I couldn't get. [SAFE]
9. What did the week actually cost, and where did it go? - Roughly a week against the schedule - estimate, read off the dates on the logged runs. Each training run was only ~30 minutes on one GPU (a graphics card - the chip models like this train on), so the cost was diagnosis and the layer/rate comparisons, not compute. The payback: the same recipe retrained on new defect types in that half hour, so the pipeline could absorb them without stalling again. [PARTIAL - estimate]
10. How do you know 94% wasn't luck? - Component-level split blocks memorization from leaking into validation (because the same physical part never appears on two sides, the model can't pass the exam by having already studied the answer); the ~3-point train-to-validation gap is the generalization evidence (it performs nearly as well on fresh photos as on studied ones); per-class recall checks no majority class is inflating one aggregate (if most photos were one easy defect type, a single average could look great while a rare defect got missed - checking each type separately rules that out). Single final run, no repeated seeds (I did not rerun the identical training several times to see how much luck moves the score) - that's the limit of what I claim. [PARTIAL]
11. What would you do differently? - Use a learning-rate schedule like cosine annealing in phase two instead of a fixed rate (a standard recipe that eases the step size down smoothly over time instead of holding it constant); collect far more than ~2,000 labels, which would make the thaw-depth choice less delicate. And I'd assumed more unfrozen layers always help - the overfitting config falsified that. [SAFE]
12. Can you show me the code? - Partly. The repo's checked-in training script is a later hardened variant for a different binary task (a yes/no job with two labels, not my four); the 4-class pipeline behind this run is the documented run from my records, not a hand-over-able artifact - which is why I name the set and the logs instead. [GAP - own it first]
