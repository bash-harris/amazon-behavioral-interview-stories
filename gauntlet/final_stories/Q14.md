# Q14 - give me an example where you took a risk in a project and succeeded

**LP:** Bias for Action (primary); Are Right, A Lot; touches Think Big.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the alignment bet: TPS warp stacked on homography (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 2, canonical alignment telling; STORY 12/13 excluded).

## Core Answer

The risk: betting days of my time - solo developer on a tool already in use - on math I'd never implemented, to fix the step everything depended on. I built the PCB-vs-layout comparison - a PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device: photograph a board, align the photo to its CAD layout (the board's computer design drawing), flag mismatches. The textbook move is feature matching into a homography - matching recognizable points between photo and design, then applying one flat-image tilt correction that fixes a photo taken from an angle. Flat board, shot straight-on: fine. Factory-floor photos: not fine. Boards flex in the reflow oven (the oven that solders the parts on), the flex isn't uniform - the board bends by different amounts in different places - and one global tilt correction can't fix that by definition. Homography alone averaged 15-20 pixels of misalignment - picture dots, meaning parts in the photo sat 15-20 dots away from where the design says they are; a measured average from test boards I built myself, not a guess - so the comparison the manufacturing engineers used on the floor cried false alarms - it flagged good boards as suspect - on warped boards.

I bet on stacking a Thin Plate Spline warp on top - TPS: a bend-mapping technique that lines up a photo even when the board has physically warped, stretching it gently through anchor points called control points. The cost had two layers. Obvious: days of the only developer's time on unfamiliar math that might not pay off. Nastier: one bad control point drags the whole field - a single wrong anchor pulls the entire bend map - so you ship something that looks precise and is quietly wrong - loud false alarms traded for silent missed defects: instead of crying wolf on good boards, it could pass bad ones. That kind of wrong ends a tool's credibility.

A calculated bet, not a gamble: the warp was visible in the photos; the comparison's mismatch threshold (how far apart photo and design must be before a part gets flagged) only means something if alignment lands within a few pixels; the failure mode was measurable on a small set first; and the warp was its own stage - if TPS failed, I dropped it and nothing got worse.

So I de-risked, then trusted. Homography first, so control points matched against a roughly aligned image. I keyed points off known features - board corners, fiducials (the small reference dots machines use to locate a board), component pads (the metal dots parts get soldered to) - and rejected matches below a template-similarity threshold, meaning any photo patch that didn't resemble the design's picture closely enough was dropped. I built my own test set: twenty board images with ground-truth markers - hand-placed, known-true positions to measure against. Average offset 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8 - that is, parts landed 2.1 dots from their known spot on average, with individual results scattered about 0.8 dots around that; one run on my small self-built set, so directional, not production telemetry. The engineers overlaid the aligned photo on the layout and spot-checked pads, but I didn't hold the work for their sign-off - my measurement was the gate, their check the safety net. The real trade-off: speed against consensus, on a change I could reverse.

It paid. Misalignment came down from 15-20 pixels to 2-3, and comparison accuracy on moderate warping went from about 85% to over 98% - in plain terms, the share of times the tool made the right call (flagging real mismatches, clearing good ones) on bent boards, counted on the documented moderate-warp test, not a fleet-wide promise. The false alarms on warped boards stopped. The risk was never the math. It was admitting the finished-looking pipeline wasn't - and making the failure mode testable before I was allowed to trust it.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: Homography alone (the one flat tilt correction) plateaued at 15-20 px (picture dots) average misalignment on warped factory photos; the live comparison tool was firing false alarms.
- Customer: Manufacturing engineers using the PCB-vs-layout comparison on the factory floor.
- Insight: Reflow flex is non-uniform - no global transform can fix it; and the bet is only safe if the failure mode is made measurable before you trust the fix.
- Action (mine): Stacked the TPS stage myself; homography-first sequencing; template-matched control points (corners, fiducials, pads) with similarity-threshold rejection; self-built 20-image ground-truth test; kept go/no-go on the metric gate, ME overlay checks as confirmation.
- Result: 15-20 px -> 2-3 px; ~85% -> >98% on moderate warping; 2.1 px avg / sd 0.8 on the 20-image test; warped-board false alarms stopped (engineer-reported); handles up to 30 deg off-perpendicular (photo taken no more than 30 deg away from straight-down) and ~3 mm warp (a visible bend in the board).
- Learning: My "global transform is the end state" assumption was the actual risk; make the failure mode testable, then commit. And start with the simplest matching that works - board-outline contours, before full SURF (a heavyweight corner-matching algorithm).

## Follow-Up Bank
1. What exactly did you bet, and what was the cost if you were wrong? - Two layers: days of the only developer's time on unfamiliar math, and a sneakier cost - a bad control point drags the whole warp (one wrong anchor point pulls the entire bend map), so a board defect could read as aligned. Silent wrong beats loud wrong at destroying trust in an inspection tool. [SAFE - mechanism from STORY 2; the "days" is an estimate, solo build]
2. What made it calculated, not a gamble? - Three things: the warp physics were visible in the images; the target was forced by the spec - the comparison flags mismatches at a 10-pixel threshold (anything a part sits 10 picture dots or more away from its design spot gets flagged), so 2-3 pixel accuracy was the requirement, not a wish; and I could measure the failure mode on 20 images before shipping. Plus the blast radius was bounded - the warp was a separable stage, so the damage if I was wrong had a small, defined size. [SAFE]
3. Why TPS over elastic deformation or B-splines? - Elastic deformation (physics-based stretch models) needs material properties that don't have a clear physical meaning for a PCB. B-splines (another grid-based bend model) add a control-grid resolution - another knob (how fine the anchor grid is - a dial I'd then have to tune). TPS minimizes bending energy - its math spreads the bend as gently as possible - is deterministic given the control points (same anchors in, same warp out, every time), and OpenCV's implementation (the open-source image-processing library I used) is mature. [SAFE - STORY 2 wording]
4. Why not a learned registration model? - A U-Net predicting a displacement field (a trained neural network that outputs, for every spot in the photo, where it should move) needs aligned/misaligned pairs with ground-truth correspondences - example pairs showing exactly which point in the photo matches which point in the design; I had no such data and collecting it was its own project. TPS is classical - no training data. With thousands of labeled pairs a learned model might win under extreme conditions; real alternative, would revisit. [SAFE]
5. How were the numbers measured, and how much do you trust them? - Self-built harness: 20 board images with ground-truth markers, measuring marker offsets after alignment - average 2.1 px, sd 0.8. In plain terms: what it counts - how far each hand-marked part landed from its known-true spot after alignment, in picture dots, plus the spread (sd 0.8 = typical measurements sit ~0.8 dots around the 2.1 average). Where it came from - a test set of 20 boards I built and marked myself. How solid - small-n, my own set, single-run: directional, not production telemetry. That's exactly why I kept the engineers' overlay checks as the safety net - they caught a good-number-hiding-a-subtly-wrong-warp case. [PARTIAL]
6. How many control points, and what if one is wrong? - A 2D warp needs at least 4 (four anchors is the bare minimum that defines a bend at all); with only 4 there's no redundancy to average noise - no spare anchors to outvote a bad one - so I typically used 15-30 across the board. A wrong point pulls the field - hence template matching on known features and rejecting low-similarity matches (photo patches that don't look enough like the design's picture). [SAFE]
7. You didn't wait for sign-off - wasn't that cowboy behavior? - It was a deliberate trade: speed and a measurable bar over consensus. Justified because the stage was reversible - if an engineer's check had failed after I proceeded, I'd drop the warp and the tool returns to the homography-only state users already had. Their checks stayed in the loop as confirmation, not permission. [PARTIAL - judgment framing]
8. When does this still fail? - Severe warp beyond roughly 5 mm: control points can't capture extreme deformation - needs a more physically based model. Past ~45 deg the feature matching itself breaks; practical capture limit is 30-40 deg, and the shipped pipeline handled up to 30 deg off-perpendicular and ~3 mm of visible warp. In plain terms for that headline limit: what it counts - how far off straight-down the photo can be (30 deg) and how much physical bend the fix can still absorb (~3 mm); where it came from - the tested handling limit of the shipped pipeline; how solid - a named boundary, not universality: past it, it fails as stated above. I name the boundary rather than imply universality. [SAFE]
9. Hardest part? - Admitting the finished-looking pipeline wasn't. The global transform was my end-state assumption and it was wrong - and the fix carried a quieter failure mode than the problem (the old problem shouted "bad board" wrongly; the new one could whisper "good board" wrongly). [SAFE]
10. Any disagreement with stakeholders? - Honest answer: no person-vs-person dispute - I built the suite solo. The friction was internal: my metric gate (my measured pass/fail bar) against their comfort with the overlay checks. I won't inflate that into a stakeholder fight. [PARTIAL - none documented]
11. Scale / adoption of the impact? - The 85%->98% is on the documented moderate-warping regime; in plain terms: what it counts - the share of correct comparison calls (right flag, right all-clear) on moderately bent boards; where it came from - the documented test behind the alignment fix; how solid - solid for that regime only, not fleet-wide. "False alarms stopped" is engineer-reported, not instrumented. The suite was built solo, so the "days lost" downside was mine alone. [PARTIAL]
12. What would you do differently? - Two things. I implemented full SURF feature matching (a heavyweight corner-matching algorithm) before discovering board-outline contour detection was sufficient for most boards - more general, but costlier and harder to debug; start simple, add complexity only when it buys accuracy. And I treated TPS as the end state the day I shipped it, when >5 mm boards still defeat it - I'd have written the model's boundary down up front. [SAFE]
