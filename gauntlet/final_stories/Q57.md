# Q57 - walk us through how you answered "something you're particularly proud of"?
**LP:** Meta/prep question - asks how you build a "proud of" answer, not for a new event. No new story.   **Story:** none - points to Q9 and Q8.

## PREP NOTE

**Best existing answers:**
- **Q9 (`final_stories/Q9.md`) - primary.** The actual "proud of your work" answer: the PCB inspection suite I built solo. (PCB = the green circuit board inside almost every electronic device; my suite was the set of tools that checks a finished board against its design.) Three tools in the eight-week window - how long the whole build took; it's the window I worked to, from my own record, with no separate documentation - and the suite became how the manufacturing team verified boards: their routine check that finished boards were correct. Pride = ownership + the debug-view trust arc - I added an audit screen that showed, for each verdict the tool gave, the inputs and the rule behind it, and being able to see its reasoning is what made the engineers trust it - not one algorithm.
- **Q8 (`final_stories/Q8.md`) - alternate.** If "proud" is probing solo end-to-end execution - the whole job, start to finish, alone - or a bold call: the standalone single-file build. The tool became one file you simply open in a browser - no server program, nothing to install - which meant deleting the server, going against the normal assumption that software ships on one.

**Pick rule:** breadth/ownership/business impact, or "most proud" -> Q9 (adoption is the strongest close - the fact that real users relied on it in production). Solo technical depth / architecture call, meaning a big design decision -> Q8. Don't stack both - don't tell one inside the other; pick one and let follow-up questions open the second.

**How to deliver a "proud of" answer (the shape):**
1. Lead with the outcome, one sentence: "My tool stopped being my project and became the way the team worked."
2. ONE number: the BOM check went from 20-30 min -> a report in seconds. Plain terms: a BOM is the board's parts list (every component with its part number); the 20-30 minutes is per board, what an engineer said one hand cross-check took - engineer-reported, never stopwatch-timed; the "seconds" is the tool's own run time, not human time. "15 engineers / 2 sites / ~20 hrs/wk" counts how many people used it across the two plants and the weekly time they believed it saved - quote it only as labeled recollection, what the engineers remembered and estimated - never instrumented (no software ever tracked it), never quoted as a measured stat.
3. ONE human stakeholder: the Bosch manufacturing engineers who falsified my tool - tried to prove each of its decisions wrong - in the debug view and killed two wrong heuristics (the rules of thumb I'd coded into the matching logic) in week one, the debug view's first week of use. Users auditing me is the proof of real use.
4. ONE learning: measure what you intend to claim, or label it an estimate (Q9); design standalone - plan the one-file, no-server form from the start - from day one (Q8).

**Traps:** no third story; no team credit (the suite was solo - I was its only engineer); no buzzwords - plain words only; don't confuse "proud" with "technically hardest"; keep the reflection - the part about what you learned - because it's what stops "proud" reading as bragging. ~2 minutes spoken; follow-ups carry the detail.
