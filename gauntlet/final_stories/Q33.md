# Q33 - tell me about a situation where you had to dig deep, what was the outcome, how did you know you were focusing on the right thing, would you do anything different?

**LP:** Dive Deep (primary; touches Learn and Be Curious, Deliver Results)   **Story:** AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 9 - the data-analysis half: learning-curve read + label self-agreement audit (defect classifier, solo build)

## Core Answer

I'd point to the stretch where I stopped trying to make my PCB defect classifier better and spent the time figuring out what was capping it. (A PCB is the green circuit board inside almost every electronic device. The "classifier" is the part of my tool that looks at a photo of a board and sorts any fault it finds into a kind - missing part, wrong part, damaged part.)

The classifier sat at 94% on a holdout of roughly 300 images, split from about 2,000 labeled component images. In plain terms: it got 94 right for every 100 photos it had never seen before. (A "holdout" is a batch of examples deliberately set aside and never used for training - the final exam, so the score can't come from memorizing the answer key. "Labeled" means each photo had its correct answer written on it, by me, by hand. The 94% is measured on that exam - a real number, and every other figure in this story starts from it.) The obvious next move was more model work - a different architecture, learning-rate schedules, more epochs - which in everyday words means: swap in a fancier model design, adjust the training dials, run more passes over the same data. I held off, because I didn't know what the limit was. The question I dug into was narrow: is 94% a model-capacity ceiling or a data ceiling? Capacity ceiling = the model itself is too simple and needs a bigger "brain"; data ceiling = the photos are too few or too messy, and no smarter model would help. Those call for completely different work.

So I measured two things.

First, learning curves during training - validation performance against how much training data went in. A learning curve is a simple chart: feed the model more and more training photos, and plot its score on the set-aside practice photos after each step. The curve is the diagnostic: where it flattens, adding capacity buys less than adding data - a bigger model is wasted effort, more or cleaner photos are what pay off. Ours had flattened, and reading it gave me an estimate - doubling from 2,000 to about 4,000 images would land near 96%, and past that the gain looked marginal. About that 96%: it's a projection read off the shape of the chart - what the curve suggests would happen - not a model I actually trained at 4,000. Solid as a planning number; not a measured result. A number for the next labeling push, not a feeling.

Second - the part I'd almost skipped - I audited the labels. I'd made every one of them myself, so trusting the set was tempting. Instead I re-labeled the whole dataset a week later, without looking at my first answers, and compared. That check is called label self-agreement: how often I still agree with myself. I agreed with myself 92% of the time. What that counts: the share of the ~2,000 photos where my second answer matched my first; where it came from: my own blind re-label, a week apart; how solid: an actually-counted figure, but it measures whether my labels are consistent, not whether they're right. One label in twelve wasn't reproducible. And if I can't label the same image twice, the model can't be expected to learn that distinction - so part of that flattening was noise in the data - unclarity in the "correct" answers themselves - not capacity in the model. I went back through the 8% one by one and assigned the primary defect - when a photo could count as two kinds of fault, I picked the dominant one - so the set that trained the model was one I'd re-audited.

That combination is how I knew I was on the right thing. The curve gave the direction and size of the payoff; the audit showed part of the ceiling was label quality - a different fix from more labels or a bigger model. So I stopped treating model tuning as the bottleneck and pointed effort at the data.

What I'd do differently: batch labeling for near-identical images. Many crops were the same component at a slightly different angle, and near-duplicates add images, not information - exactly the padding that makes a learning curve look rosier than the data behind it: more photos to count, but nothing new to learn from.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: classifier plateaued at 94% (300-image holdout from ~2,000); next step unclear
- Customer: the inspection suite's verdicts - engineers trusting a model whose real limit was unknown
- Insight: "is 94% a capacity ceiling (model too simple) or a data ceiling (not enough or too-messy photos)?" - the two call for different work, so measure before tuning
- Action (mine): plotted learning curves; re-labeled the full set a week later, blind to pass one (without seeing my own first answers); re-adjudicated every disagreement to the primary defect (the dominant fault when a photo could count as two)
- Result: curve read - 2,000->94%, ~4,000->~96% (estimate), flat beyond; 92% self-agreement (re-label matched my first answers 92% of the time - consistency, not correctness), 8% resolved; effort moved from tuning to data quality
- Learning: audit your own labels before blaming the model; near-duplicates pad a curve without adding information (more photos counted, nothing new learned)

(Learning curve = accuracy on the set-aside practice photos, plotted as you add more training photos.) 2. Did you ever verify the ~96% by actually training on 4,000? - No. It's a read off the curve - extrapolated from the chart's shape, not a measured result - and I'd present it that way. The decision it supported - stop collecting at that regime - held either way, because the cost side was clear on its own.  [PARTIAL - honest estimate]
3. 92% agreement with yourself sounds low. How did you use it? - As a ceiling, not trivia. If one label in twelve changes a week later, that fraction is genuinely ambiguous and no model can learn a distinction I can't make consistently. (Self-agreement = how often a second, blind re-label by the same person matches the first - like taking the same test twice and seeing if your answers stick.) Part of the plateau was label noise, so the next move was data quality, not architecture. All 8% were re-examined and assigned the primary defect.  [SAFE]
4. Does 92% prove the labels are correct? - No - it measures stability, not correctness. Being sure twice isn't being right; a consistent habit of mislabeling the same way would pass this test untouched. Same labeler (me), a week apart, blind to the first pass. A second labeler - another person labeling the same photos so we could compare - would be the stronger check; there wasn't one on a solo project.  [PARTIAL - honest limit]
5. How do you know the model wasn't just under-trained? - I measured both sides instead of assuming. (Under-trained = the model simply hadn't learned enough yet - a training problem rather than a data problem.) Curve flattening is the signature of data-limited, not capacity-limited - a chart that goes flat says the photos, not the model's size, are the lever; the label audit independently showed the data itself carried noise. Two measurements pointing at the data is why I redirected effort there rather than running another tuning sweep.  [SAFE]
6. Why stop near 4,000 instead of collecting more? - Return versus cost. Past the flat part of the curve the gain is marginal, and labeling (~3 seconds an image - my own stopwatch timing for writing one answer onto one photo) is the cheap half - finding and cropping new board images takes far longer. Stop when the curve flattens, not when the count looks impressive.  [PARTIAL - 3s is my own timing]
7. Where did the 94% come from? - A holdout of roughly 300 images, a 70/15/15 split of the ~2,000-image set - meaning I divided the ~2,000 photos into 70% to teach the model, 15% to fine-tune it along the way, and the last 15% (~300) kept back untouched as the final exam. That's the number the curve read starts from.  [SAFE]
8. Weren't the labels just your own opinion? - They were, which is exactly why I tested their reproducibility instead of trusting them. Genuinely ambiguous images went to a skip file (a parked folder for "I can't honestly answer this," instead of a guessed label); the 8% disagreements were resolved one by one to the primary defect.  [SAFE]
9. How is this different from the training work where you improved accuracy? - That was model-side - what the network adapts and how. This was data-side - whether the data could support a better model at all. The decision here came from the curve and the audit, not a hyperparameter change (not fiddling with the model's training dials).  [SAFE]
10. Who pushed back on your conclusion? - Solo builder, so there's no documented stakeholder pushback to claim. The real disagreement was with my own first-pass labels on the borderline set.  [GAP - do not invent conflict]
11. Hardest part? - Not the analysis. The grind: the whole set labeled twice, then re-adjudicating roughly 160 borderline images (~8% of 2,000 - arithmetic, not telemetry: figured by percentage, not counted by any system) without just confirming my first guess.  [PARTIAL - 160 derived]
12. What breaks this approach at larger scale? - Self-agreement is the wrong audit with multiple annotators - my agreeing with myself says nothing about whether two different people see the same fault. I'd want inter-annotator agreement on a shared sample (how often two people label the same set of photos alike) plus an adjudication rule (who settles the disagreements and how) before a second person touches the set.  [SAFE - framed as what I'd do]
