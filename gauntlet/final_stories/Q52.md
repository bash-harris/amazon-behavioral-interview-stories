# Q52 - tell me about a challenge you faced

**LP:** Dive Deep (primary); Deliver Results (secondary - only the quality/persistence strengths are evidenced; see follow-up 8).   **Story:** BGSW PCB suite - the pin-count mismatch in BOM reconciliation (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 10); same event as Q4/Q17, distinct angle: the layered challenge and the investigation, not the failure-ownership arc or the pushback arc.

## Core Answer

The challenge I'd point to was a correctness problem in my BOM reconciliation tool - a "BOM" is the bill of materials, the list of every part that goes on a circuit board and how many of each, and "reconciling" it means checking that list against what the board was actually drawn with. It walked in looking like a tuning request, and every time I fixed a layer, it opened a deeper one.

Context: the tool compares a board's parts list against its layout (the design file showing where every part sits and where every solder connection goes) and flags discrepancies. My first version compared pin counts literally - eight pins in the BOM against sixteen pads in the layout got flagged as a mismatch. (Pins are a component's metal legs; pads are the solder spots on the board those legs sit on.) On boards with mixed component types, about 12% of the flags were false alarms - warnings about parts that were perfectly fine. (That 12% counts the wrong warnings among the flags on the boards in my sample; I gathered and judged those files myself, so it's a ballpark on my own sample, not a production measurement - follow-up 4 has the honest version.) Every one sent a manufacturing engineer to hand-verify a part that was perfectly fine - exactly the work the tool existed to remove.

The easy read was "too strict, loosen it." The challenge was finding the line between a normal manufacturing variation and a genuine mismatch - you can't tune your way to that line. So I went at the data. I pulled twenty BOMs with pin-count discrepancies and categorized every case - twenty is the count of real board files that had tripped these warnings, all collected and checked by hand by me, and they're the evidence base behind every number in this story. The pattern was clean and it was asymmetric. More pads in the layout than the part needs: almost always legitimate, because footprints get drawn for a whole package family and the extra pads are unused or no-connects. (A "footprint" is the pad pattern a part is meant to sit on; a "package family" is a group of parts sharing one outer shape but with different numbers of legs; a "no-connect" is a pad deliberately wired to nothing.) Fewer pads: always a real mismatch, because the part physically can't fit. Same discrepancy, opposite meanings depending on direction.

I rewrote the rule to follow that: more pads flags for review - the tool says "a human should look at this" rather than "this is broken" - and fewer stays a definite mismatch. That fixed the false alarms - and immediately exposed the next layer. Some components had a genuinely wrong footprint that just happened to be larger, and the new rule waved them through - let them pass with no warning at all. The first failure mode had been hiding the second.

So I went back in. The fact that separated them was component type - what kind of part it actually is. A larger footprint is only acceptable if the type agrees. I added a type check, a configurable ratio threshold defaulted from the data (a setting for how much bigger the pad pattern may be - a multiple of the part's leg count - before the tool stops letting it pass; I set the starting value from what the twenty files showed), and a per-component debug view showing the pin count, pad count, type match, and its decision - so an engineer can see why and override: each part's numbers and the tool's verdict on one screen, and they can second-guess it when they know the board better.

False alarms on mixed boards dropped from about 12% to under 2% - on the files I had, checked against a regression set to confirm nothing that matched before had changed. (A "regression set" is a stock of files already known to work that gets re-run after every change to catch accidental breakage. This before->after counts the share of warnings that were false on the boards in my sample; solid enough to show the direction of travel, not a measured factory-wide rate.) The engineers went back to trusting the report.

What it taught me: on a correctness problem, don't stop at the first drop in the number. Each fix tells you where the next layer is.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: literal pin-count-equals-pad-count rule produced ~12% false-positive flags - warnings on parts that were actually fine - on mixed-component-type boards; looked like a simple "loosen the tool" tuning request
- Customer: manufacturing engineers hand-verifying good parts from the report - the exact work the tool was supposed to remove; report credibility was the stake
- Insight: the pin-vs-pad discrepancy is directional - more pads = package family / no-connects (extra solder pads a part simply doesn't use: legitimate), fewer pads = part physically can't fit (genuine); and after the first fix, component type is what separates a legit larger footprint from a wrong one
- Action (mine): collected and categorized every case in 20 failing BOMs - the 20 board files that tripped the warning; rewrote the rule (more pads -> review, fewer -> definite mismatch); added component-type check; configurable 2:1 ratio threshold (up to twice as many pads as pins gets tolerated) defaulted from that data; built per-component debug view with engineer override; verified on a 15-BOM regression suite (5 with pin-count issues) - 15 saved, known-good board files re-run after the change
- Result: ~12% -> under 2% false alarms on mixed-type boards - my own categorization + regression re-run, not an instrumented production rate; regression: 5 fixed, 10 unchanged, no previously-matching component flipped (of the 15 files re-checked, the 5 with pin problems came out right, the other 10 stayed as they were)
- Learning: the first failure mode hides the second - iterate one layer past the first fix; pin count was a hypothesis, not a proven signal (reference designator + footprint name is the better one - the part's labeled identity on the board plus its pad-pattern name, which say what the part is supposed to be, not just how many legs it has); collect diverse files up front

## Follow-Up Bank
1. Why not just drop pin count from the comparison? - It catches the physically impossible fits: a 16-pin part on an 8-pad footprint is a real error - twice as many legs as solder spots, the part cannot fit. The fix was making the signal discriminating, not blinding the tool. [SAFE]
2. How did you pick the threshold? - From the 20 files: legitimate larger-footprint variations mostly ran at 2:1 or under - the pad pattern was at most about twice the size the part actually needed. Thin base - 20 files is a small, self-picked sample - so it's a configurable default, not a verdict; borderline ratios go to review, and the engineer who knows the board adjusts it. Residual risk is false negatives on same-type-letter wrong parts - a wrong part that carries the same type marking as the right one slips through unflagged; the type check plus debug view mitigate. A heuristic, not a proof. [SAFE]
3. Why not train a model? - I considered it. Only ~200 known mismatch examples at component level - roughly 200 individual parts with a confirmed wrong-spec match, which is how many worked examples you teach software from, and that's too few - not enough for a reliable classifier, and the rules were clear with enumerable edge cases, meaning the exceptions could be listed out by hand. Heuristics fit; I'd revisit ML if cases got messier. [SAFE]
4. How was 12% -> under 2% measured? - Honestly: my own categorization of the 20 failing BOMs plus a 15-BOM regression suite re-run after the fix. The number counts the share of warnings that were false, before vs after, across those files. No held-out set - no fresh examples deliberately kept back to score performance fairly - and no labeled ground truth, meaning no hand-verified right/wrong answer for every case. Not a production rate - a direction of travel on the files I had. If pressed for rigor: label ~200 components as ground truth and pick the operating point off a precision-recall curve - a chart trading "how many warnings are right" against "how many real problems get caught" at each strictness setting, and the operating point is the strictness you choose. [PARTIAL - self-categorized sample]
5. How did you know the fix didn't break working boards? - Full 15-BOM regression, 5 with pin-count issues: the 5 came out correct, the other 10 unchanged, no previously-matching component flipped status - nothing already judged a match changed sides. Safety net on files I had - not certification on unseen boards. [PARTIAL]
6. What happens at a 3:1 ratio? - Three times as many pads as pins, past the 2:1 default: flagged as a mismatch under the default. Deliberate: match, mismatch, and review are three distinct decision states - "fine," "broken," "a human should look" - not degrees of one. From there the engineer verifies - legit variation (raise the threshold) or wrong part (investigate). Judgment stays with the person who knows the board. [SAFE]
7. How did you catch the second failure mode? - That's the heart of the challenge. After the direction-based fix dropped the false alarms, a genuinely wrong component with a larger footprint slipped through a review. The fix itself exposed the gap: I'd traded false positives for a false-negative path - fewer wrong warnings in exchange for a new way to silently miss a real problem. That's what forced the component-type check. [SAFE]

What would you do differently? - Collect diverse sample BOMs from the floor - board files from actual production, not just the ones I happened to have - before writing the rule, instead of discovering edge cases one production file at a time. And treat a matching signal as a hypothesis until it's held across varied samples - pin count wasn't the right signal; reference designator plus footprint name is (the part's labeled identity on the board plus its pad-pattern name).
