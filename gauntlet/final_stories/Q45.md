# Q45 - a time when you did something which was very difficult and how you dealt with negative feedbacks

**LP:** Earn Trust (primary); Learn and Be Curious; Deliver Results   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - non-rigid alignment stage (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 2), canonical alignment telling; difficulty primary, feedback = documented corrective input on that work

## Core Answer

The hardest thing I built was the alignment stage of the PCB-vs-layout tool. A PCB is the green circuit board inside electronic devices, and the "layout" is its design drawing; "alignment" means lining up the photo of the real board against the drawing so they match point-for-point. The comparison flags a mismatch at 10 pixels - a part whose photo position sits more than 10 picture-dots away from the drawing's position gets marked - so the photo had to line up to about 2 or 3 pixels or everything after it was just noise. A normal shop-floor photo isn't a flat, straight shot, and a plain perspective correction - the standard software fix that straightens a photo taken at an angle, as if it were aimed head-on - left the far edge of the board off by 15 to 20 pixels (those numbers are distances measured in picture-dots, or pixels, on my own test photos, before and after the fix). Closing that gap is the difficulty.

To close it I needed non-rigid warping - not just straightening the image, but bending it to follow the board's actual curves, like flexing a rubber sheet. I chose thin-plate splines over elastic deformation and B-splines - three different mathematical ways of bending an image; the thin-plate spline imitates how a thin metal plate flexes, spreading each anchor's pull smoothly with distance. I picked it because it minimizes bending energy (the bend it produces is as gentle as possible), it's deterministic once you fix the control points (same photo, same anchors, same result every time - "control points" being the anchor spots matched between photo and drawing, which the rest of the image follows), it needs no physical constants like stiffness that don't mean anything for a board, and OpenCV - the standard free image-processing library - supports the math. The catch was that no JavaScript library did it, so I implemented it from the original Bookstein 1989 paper (the academic paper that first defined the method), in a browser.

What made it genuinely hard wasn't the formula, it was the failure mode. The spline bends the whole field through its control points, so one badly matched point pulls the entire warp the wrong way - you get output that looks precise and is silently wrong: everything appears neatly lined up, no error is reported, and the result is still bad. Correctness lived in rejecting weak control-point matches, not in getting the equation right. I also over-built it first: I wrote full SURF feature matching - an advanced technique for automatically locating distinctive reference points across two images - before I found that simple board-outline contour finding (tracing just the board's outer edge) handled most boards.

The feedback was concrete. Engineers told me the comparison was crying wolf - false alarms, meaning it flagged mismatches on warped boards that were actually fine. And when I brought them in to overlay the aligned photo on the layout - placing the two images on top of each other so a person can see whether the parts line up - their eye caught boards where my average looked good but the warp was subtly off at the edges. That eyeball-on-top-of-each-other review is the overlay spot-check, and it is the feedback: a plain-language correction from real users, not a formal review.

I didn't defend the average. I treated their overlay as a real check, not a courtesy, reproduced each flagged board, and kept the human overlay in the loop afterward, because an average can hide one wrong board. Now I ask for the failing board, not a verdict.

With the spline stage in, far-edge misalignment went from 15 to 20 pixels down to 2 or 3, and comparison accuracy on warped boards went from about 85% to over 98% - that's the share of warped-board comparisons the tool got right, measured on my own test boards, before versus after. The false alarms stopped. The lesson I keep: my number is the hypothesis, their observation is the data, and on a task this sharp the only way to settle it is to reproduce the case, not defend the metric.

## Memory Anchors

- Problem: 10-pixel mismatch threshold forced alignment to ~2-3 px; perspective-only left the far edge 15-20 px off, so downstream results were noise
- Customer: manufacturing engineers who'd asked for the comparison tool and were getting false alarms on warped boards
- Insight: the error is structural - reflow warps boards non-uniformly, so no global transform can fix a mid-board bow; you need a non-rigid warp, and the metric's average can hide a single wrong board
- Action (mine): picked TPS over elastic/B-splines on stated grounds; implemented it from Bookstein 1989 in-browser; gated control-point matches by template similarity; added the human overlay check after the spot-check disagreed with the metric; owned the SURF over-build
- Result: 15-20 px -> 2-3 px far-edge misalignment; comparison accuracy ~85% -> >98% on moderate warping; false alarms stopped
- Learning: the number is my hypothesis, the user's failing case is the data - reproduce the case, don't defend the average; start with the simple method and add complexity only when data demands it

## Follow-Up Bank

1. What was actually hard, apart from the feedback? - Implementing thin-plate splines from the primary literature with no JS library, in a browser. The math is a radial-basis sum plus a linear term solving an (n+3)x(n+3) system - in plain words: every anchor point contributes a smooth ripple that fades with distance, and all those ripples are solved together as one block of simultaneous equations - but the operational danger is that one bad control point bends the whole field the wrong way, so a wrong result looks right. [SAFE]
2. Why TPS and not elastic deformation or B-splines? - It minimizes bending energy (gives the gentlest possible bend), is deterministic given the control points (same inputs, identical bend every time), needs no physical constants that have no meaning for a rigid-ish PCB, and is mature in OpenCV. I chose it on those grounds, not by default. [SAFE]
3. Was that really "negative feedback," or a QA finding? - Two documented pieces, and I'll be precise. Engineers reported the tool crying wolf on warped boards - a user complaint ("crying wolf" = reporting mismatch alarms when nothing is actually wrong). And their overlay spot-check corrected my result: it caught cases my metric passed but the warp was subtly wrong. The one formal team-review event in my record is the five-day-prototype review, which I use for other questions and am not re-telling here. I won't inflate this into a manager one-on-one that didn't happen. [PARTIAL - premise labeled, never inflated]
4. How did you keep from getting defensive? - I treated the claim and my metric as different kinds of evidence. My average - a single summary number across my test photos - said the alignment was fine; their eyes said a specific board was off. I asked which board and reproduced it, instead of arguing the average. [SAFE]
5. How did you verify the fix landed? - Quantitatively, a 20-image controlled test: average far-edge offset 2.1 px, standard deviation 0.8 px. In plain terms: across 20 board photos I tested under the same conditions, the far edge sat an average of 2.1 pixels from where the drawing said it should be, and the standard deviation of 0.8 measures how much individual photos varied around that average - typically within about a pixel of it. It's my own measurement on my own small test set, not a lab study, but it is the solid, repeatable one. Qualitatively, the engineers overlaid the aligned image on the layout and checked pads lined up - "pads" are the small metal contact areas where components solder on - that's what caught what the average hid. I kept both checks. [SAFE - these are the canonical alignment numbers]
6. What did you change because of the feedback? - Two things. The human overlay check became a standing part of how I validated alignment, not a one-off courtesy - it stayed in my routine rather than being done once to be agreeable. And I started asking for the specific failing case, because a case is reproducible and a verdict isn't. [PARTIAL - "standing" is my practice claim; the overlay-as-validation is documented]
7. How big is the warp it handles, and where does it still fail? - It handles photos up to about 30 degrees off-perpendicular (the camera tilted up to roughly 30 deg away from pointing straight down at the board) and roughly 3 mm of warp (a board buckled or lifted by up to about 3 millimetres). Above about 5 mm of deformation the control points can't capture it and a physically based model - one that simulates how the board actually flexes - would be needed - rare on the floor. I named the boundary instead of implying the warp was universal. [SAFE]
8. What's the real lesson, not the polish? - I over-built. I implemented full SURF matching (the advanced automatic reference-point technique) before finding board-outline contour detection (simply tracing the board's edge) was enough for most boards - more general, but slower and harder to debug. I should have started simple and added the non-rigid stage only where the data demanded it. [SAFE]
9. What's the judgment call in here? - Build vs reuse: I could have reached for an elastic or B-spline method, or a server-side library, and instead implemented the one method whose properties matched a deterministic in-browser check. Simplicity vs generality: I later rejected my own over-general SURF choice for the simpler contour method. Both were mine to decide. [SAFE]
10. Who did the work? - Me. The PCB suite was built solo; I was the only engineer on it. The alignment stage, the Bookstein implementation, and the response to the overlay check were all mine. No "we" in this story. [SAFE]
