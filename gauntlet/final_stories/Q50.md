# Q50 - tell me about a time when you didn't know what to do next or how to solve a challenging problem
**LP:** Learn and Be Curious (primary); Dive Deep; Bias for Action.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - implementing Thin Plate Spline non-rigid warping with no JavaScript library (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md STORY 5; alignment telling = AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 2).

## Core Answer

The hardest "I didn't know what to do next" moment was implementing Thin Plate Spline (TPS) warping - a mathematical way of smoothly bending one image onto another, like flexing a clear sheet until marked points line up - when there was no JavaScript version anywhere. JavaScript is the language that runs inside a web browser; "no version" meant there was no existing browser-ready code of this method I could borrow. I knew what I needed - a non-rigid correction on top of the global alignment. Non-rigid means the bend can vary from one area of the board to another, not one fixed tilt for the whole picture; the global alignment is the single tilt-and-shift correction that lines the photo and the design up roughly.

The setup. I was matching photos of PCBs - the green circuit boards inside electronic devices - to their CAD layouts, the board's digital design drawing. A homography (one flat tilt-and-turn correction applied to the whole image) fixed the global tilt, but boards come out of a reflow oven - the oven that melts the solder and can warp the board - bent, and the bend isn't even - the middle bows more than the edges. A single global transform can't undo a local bend, so the comparison only meant something with a non-rigid stage. I searched for a JS TPS library - ready-made browser code for that bending method - and found none. The problem flipped from "call the right function" (use someone's finished piece of code) to "derive the algorithm" (work the math out myself), and that's a different kind of stuck.

Before I wrote code I picked the method. Elastic deformation - a warping method that simulates a stretchy material - needs material constants, stiffness numbers, that don't mean anything for a PCB. B-splines, another warping technique, add a control-grid resolution - how fine a grid of bending anchors to use - I'd have to guess. TPS minimizes bending energy, meaning the math keeps the bend as gentle as possible, and is deterministic once the control points - the anchor locations where photo and layout are known to match - are fixed. Deterministic means same inputs, same output every time, which mattered, because with no reference build - no existing implementation to compare my results against - I needed something to check against, not to tune.

The way out was the source. I read the original paper, Bookstein 1989 - the academic paper that first defined this method - because the easy explanations skipped the math. I implemented it piece by piece and checked each intermediate result against the worked examples in the paper. With no library to test against, those examples became my test - my controlled check: a repeatable, purpose-built test rather than just looking at day-to-day results. I wired it in after the homography and chose control points by template-matching known features - the software finds each landmark automatically by searching the photo for a small picture of it - corners, fiducials (small reference marks printed on boards for positioning), pads (the metal dots parts solder onto), dropping any weak match, because one bad point drags the whole warp.

The payoff was real. Far-edge misalignment - how far photo and layout still miss each other at the board's far edge, counted in pixels, the tiny dots a picture is made of - dropped from about 15 to 20 pixels down to 2 or 3, and the engineers stopped getting false alarms (boards flagged as defective that were actually fine) on warped boards that used to flag. In plain terms: the pixel drop is from a controlled test I set up on purpose (20 images - solid); the false-alarm relief is the engineers' own reports, not something I measured. I kept the boundary honest: it holds to roughly 30 degrees off - the board tilted that far from facing the camera straight-on - and 3 mm of warp; beyond that it breaks.

What I took from it: learn just enough for the next feature, then build it. I learned Canny (a standard algorithm that traces the edges and outlines in an image) for edges, CLAHE (an automatic contrast fix for uneven lighting) for lighting, TPS for the warp, only when each blocked me. A smaller version bit me too - I built full SURF matching (a heavyweight method for finding the same physical points in two images) before finding contour detection - just tracing the board's outline - was enough for most boards, and I lost about three hours to a TensorFlow.js tensor-shape bug. TensorFlow.js is Google's machine-learning library that runs in the browser; a tensor is a multi-dimensional grid of numbers and its "shape" is the order of that grid's layers, and mine were stacked in the wrong order. Now I check shapes before I run anything.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: No JavaScript implementation of Thin Plate Spline non-rigid warping existed, and global homography structurally cannot fix reflow-oven board warp.
- Customer: The manufacturing engineers running the inspection line (I built the suite solo; the engineers were the users and validators).
- Insight: With no reference build, pick the method whose ground truth you can verify - TPS minimizes bending energy, is deterministic given control points, so the original paper's worked examples could serve as the test.
- Action (mine): Searched and found no JS TPS; chose TPS over elastic deformation and B-splines on verifiability; implemented from Bookstein 1989, validating each intermediate stage against the paper's examples; wired it after the homography; selected control points by template matching with low-similarity rejection.
- Result: Far-edge misalignment ~15-20 px -> 2-3 px; engineers' false alarms on warped boards resolved; the comparison's 10-px threshold became reliable; holds to ~30 deg off-perpendicular / 3 mm warp.
- Learning: Learn just enough for the next feature, then build it; start simpler (I over-built full SURF before contour detection sufficed); check tensor shapes up front (lost ~3 h to a TF.js shape bug).

## Follow-Up Bank
1. Why not an existing library or a simpler method? - I searched; there was no browser JS TPS. And "simpler" wasn't: elastic deformation (the stretchy-material simulation) needs material constants with no clear meaning for a PCB, B-splines add a control-grid resolution I'd have to guess. TPS is deterministic (same inputs, same output every time) and verifiable, which is what I needed with no reference to test against. [SAFE]
2. How did you validate an implementation with nothing to compare it to? - Against Bookstein 1989 itself. The paper has worked examples, so I checked intermediate results stage by stage rather than only eyeballing the final image. Template-matched control points plus low-similarity rejection (any auto-found landmark that only vaguely matched its picture was dropped) kept a wrong point from silently pulling the warp. [SAFE]
3. Explain TPS simply. - It fits a smooth deformation through a set of control points while minimizing a bending-energy measure - like flexing a clear sheet so it passes exactly through marked dots while staying as unstrained as possible - so the warp is exact at the points and smooth between them. I supply correspondences (this photo point -> this layout point); it returns the local warp a global transform (one uniform tilt/shift for the whole image) can't represent. [SAFE]
4. How was 15-20 px -> 2-3 px measured, and how reliable? - It's far-edge misalignment: how far the photo and layout still miss each other at the board's far edge, in pixels. A 20-image controlled test - a repeatable test I built for the purpose on 20 board images, not day-to-day use - averaged 2.1 px, sd 0.8 (standard deviation: the typical spread around that average, so most results land near 2 px). Comparison accuracy - the share of board comparisons judged correctly - went ~85% -> >98%. The false-alarm fix on warped boards is engineer-reported recollection, not an instrumented count. [PARTIAL - the outcome half is engineer-reported, not logged]
5. What does it NOT solve? - It handles up to ~30 deg off-perpendicular (tilted that far from facing the camera straight-on) and ~3 mm of visible warp. Severe warp beyond about 5 mm still defeats it (would need a more physically based model - one that simulates how a real board bends), and steep angles past roughly 45 deg break feature matching (the automatic landmark-finding) outright. I'd say the boundary rather than imply it's universal. [SAFE]
6. How did you avoid being stuck for days? - I changed the question. "Find the function" (look for a ready-made piece of code) had no answer once no library existed, so the tractable version was "pick a method with a verifiable ground truth" (a known-correct answer you can check against), then build against it. The paper's examples gave checkpoints, so even with no next answer I always had a next test. [SAFE]
7. A real mistake in here? - Yes. I implemented full SURF feature matching (a heavyweight method for finding the same physical points across two images - works on almost anything, but slower and harder to debug) before discovering board-outline contour detection (tracing just the outer edge of the board) was sufficient for most boards. SURF is more general but costlier and harder to debug; building it first was the wrong order. Now I start with the simplest thing that could meet the threshold and escalate only if it fails. [SAFE]
8. The three-hour debug you mention. - A TensorFlow.js shape bug: TensorFlow.js is Google's machine-learning library for the browser; a tensor is a multi-dimensional grid of numbers and its shape is the order of that grid's layers. I fed the model a [1, 3, 224, 224] tensor when it wanted [1, 224, 224, 3] - same total of numbers, layers stacked in the wrong order. It "ran" but produced garbage, which is why it took ~3 hours to isolate. I now assert (automatically test) the input shape before inference (running the model on real data) as a reflex, so that failure is one line instead of three hours. [SAFE]
9. Was this depth worth it vs a workaround? - The comparison runs on a fixed 10-px threshold (a cut-off: any photo-vs-layout difference bigger than 10 pixels counts as a mismatch), so if alignment is off, everything downstream is noise. The warp was visible in the images and the requirement concrete, so the depth was tied to whether the tool worked at all - not polish. Had a simpler fix met the threshold, I'd have taken it; that's why I compared the three methods before committing. [SAFE]
10. Could you point to the exact paper examples and residuals you matched? - Honestly, I validated qualitatively (comparing by eye against the paper's figures) against the paper's worked examples as I went; I did not log per-example numeric residuals (the exact error distance for each test point). The durable evidence was the 20-image test and the engineers' spot-checks. [GAP - no per-example residuals recorded]
11. Hardest part and any disagreement? - The hardest part wasn't the coding, it was the self-doubt of no reference: a warp can look right while being quietly wrong, so discipline (checkpoints against the paper, not eyeballing) was the actual work. No teammate disagreement to report - I built the suite solo, so the engineers served as external validators, not collaborators. [SAFE]
