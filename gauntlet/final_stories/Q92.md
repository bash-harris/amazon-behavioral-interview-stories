# Q92 - tell me about a time you used data to solve a problem

**LP:** Are Right, A Lot (primary); Dive Deep; Deliver Results.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 9 + STORY 3) - the learning-curve and labeling-economics read: using data to decide when to stop collecting data and ship. Same event as Q23/Q33, distinct lens; distinct from Q63 (make-vs-buy cost call), Q27 (4-signal engine), Q80 (pin-count rule from 20 BOMs).

## Core Answer

There was a time data solved a problem that wasn't about the product - it was about my time. It told me when to stop working.

I built a PCB defect classifier solo. A PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device; a "classifier" is a program that sorts photos into categories. A cropped component image goes in; it answers: good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing. "Labeling" means a human writing down the correct answer for each photo so the machine has examples to learn from - in my case, my own typing hours. Mine reached 94 percent on a holdout set I built myself. A "holdout set" is the batch of photos I kept back and never used in training - about 300 of them, like a final exam the model has never seen - so 94 percent means it sorted 94 of every 100 of those fresh photos correctly, and that's a measured figure, not a guess. And I hit the classic question: keep labeling, or ship?

"More data helps" is true, and useless. Of course more data helps. The only question worth answering was what the next hour of my time buys. You can't feel that, so I measured.

Cost first. I'd built a keyboard labeling tool - one key per label, auto-advance - and it came out around three seconds an image. That "about 3 seconds" counts the time to label one photo with my tool; it came from my own timing while I worked, a personal stopwatch-style measurement, not software telemetry. Cheap. But the dataset grew only as fast as I could type, and my typing was the schedule.

Then the return. I logged accuracy against how much training data went in - a learning curve, which is just a chart tracking "how right is the model" as I feed it more and more photos. The shape of that curve is a price list. It projected that doubling the dataset would buy roughly two more points - meaning about 2 more correct answers per 100 photos on that same exam - and flatten toward nothing after that. So: two points for twice my labeling hours, then a shrinking fraction of that.

The data also fixed what I was measuring. Most components on a line are good; a model that always said "good" would have scored respectably and been useless. So I weighted the rare defect classes and judged the model on per-class recall, not the headline number. "Per-class recall," in plain words: for each category of defect on its own, what share of the real defects of that kind did the model actually catch?

The decision: stop collecting, ship. The honest footnote is that more data would have helped generalization - I still think so. "Generalization" means doing well on the brand-new boards it meets on the factory floor, not just on the photos it was tested on. But the curve said the remaining effort was buying fractions, and shipping on schedule got the tool in front of the engineers who were waiting on it.

What I learned: "more data is better" is a true statement that solves nothing until you price it - hours against points. The data didn't make my model better. It told me when better wasn't worth it.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: Classifier was "good enough" with no rule for stop-vs-grind; every label cost the solo builder's typing time, so dataset growth = schedule risk.
- Customer: Manufacturing/test engineers waiting on the inspection suite; also my own labeling hours.
- Insight: "Is more data good?" is the wrong question; "what does the next hour of labeling buy?" is the solvable one - the learning curve (accuracy-vs-data chart) is a price list.
- Action (mine): Built the keyboard labeler and timed it; logged accuracy vs training-set size during training; read the projected flat point; weighted rare defect classes and switched to per-class recall (catch-rate within each defect type) over raw accuracy; set the stopping point from the curve.
- Result: Shipped at 94% on a ~300-image holdout (kept-back photos the model never trained on) with every defect class above 90% recall, on schedule, instead of grinding for a projected ~2 points.
- Learning: An unpriced truth ("more data helps") is not a decision; data told me when better wasn't worth it - and I say out loud what I gave up.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. What exactly did the curve show? - 2,000 images put it at 94%; the curve projected about 96% at double the data and marginal beyond. That's a read off the plotted curve, not a trained 4,000-image model - I present it as a projection. [PARTIAL - projection, labeled]
2. What were the labeling economics, really? - Before: manual CSV entry (typing each photo's answer into a spreadsheet file), about 30 s an image. After my keyboard tool: about 3 s. The 2,000 images took roughly 100 minutes instead of an estimated 10 hours. Doubling the set  another ~100 minutes for the projected ~2 points. That's the price the curve was quoted against. [SAFE - STORY 9's own figures]
3. Was the 3 s/label measured? - My own small-sample timing while working in the tool, with the manual CSV method as the before. Personal harness, not instrumented telemetry. [SAFE - self-measured, say so]
4. How reliable is the 94%? - Held-out ~300 images, a 70/15/15 split of ~2,000, meaning of my ~2,000 labelled photos 70% taught the model, 15% tuned its settings, and the last 15% (~300) were kept back purely for the final scoring exam. Split at component level so the same component couldn't leak across train and test - one physical part never appears in both the study pile and the exam pile; ~3-point train-to-validation gap, meaning the model scored only about 3 points worse on the tuning data than on the data it learned from, so it wasn't just memorizing; every defect class above 90% recall (92 wrong-value / 96 wrong-orientation / 98 missing - on small per-class samples, so I lead with ">90"); ~45 ms per image on CPU - that's how long the program takes to judge one photo on an ordinary processor with no graphics card. [SAFE]
5. Why not just report accuracy? - "Good" components outnumbered defects roughly 5:1; always predicting "good" scores about 83% - i.e., a model that cries "defect" never once would still look 83% right, which is why the headline number misleads. I weighted defective examples 5x and made precision-recall the primary read - precision = when the model flags a defect, how often the flag is real; recall = of the real defects, how many it caught. Accuracy was the vanity metric the data warned me off. [SAFE]
6. Did you trust your own labels? - I labeled the whole set twice, a week apart, without looking at the first answers: 92% self-agreement. That counts how often my two independent passes gave the same answer for the same photo - 92 of every 100 - a check that the labels themselves, the ground truth under every other number, were stable; my own audit, not an external one. The 8% were borderline defects; I re-examined each and assigned the primary defect, so the set the model trained on was re-audited. [SAFE - same facts/numbers as Q23/Q33]
7. Was any other part of this data-decided? - Model size: bigger EfficientNet variants (larger, more parameter-heavy versions of the same family of image model - "parameters" being the internal knobs training tunes) bought accuracy I didn't need at latency I couldn't have - slower per-photo answer times - so I kept the ~21M-parameter version; the train/val gap held at ~3 points on it. [SAFE - canonical STORY 3/14 set]
8. Hardest part? - The waiting. Stopping feels wrong when more data always helps in the abstract; I had to trust a flat curve I'd read myself and ship under the projection's error bars. [SAFE - qualitative]
9. Disagreement? - No external pushback on the record for this call - I owned the labeling time and the ship decision; the engineers consumed the result, they didn't review it. I won't invent a fight; the tension was internal, grind vs ship. [GAP on external disagreement - concede]
10. Isn't this the same story as your ownership / dive-deep answers? - One event, three true faces: Q23 = making the labels trustworthy was nobody's job; Q33 = diagnosing whether the model or the data was the ceiling; here = pricing the next hour of data and deciding to stop. Numbers identical on purpose - that's the defense, not the tell. [SAFE]
11. Do differently? - Collect more up front - ~2,000 was on the low side for generalization (doing well on new, unseen boards) - and batch-label near-identical crops; duplicates of the same part at a new angle add images, not information, which makes a curve look rosier than the data. I'd still stop by the curve, not by feel. [SAFE]
12. Was 94% actually enough for them? - The defined need was a defect triage filter ahead of human verification: the model's job was to sort obvious goods from possible defects so engineers spent their eyes only on the flagged ones, with recall above 90% per class and an interactive ~45 ms CPU pass on the factory machines (fast enough that it feels instant to a person clicking through boards). Floor time saved is engineer-reported recollection, not a number I instrumented. [PARTIAL - no invented hours]
