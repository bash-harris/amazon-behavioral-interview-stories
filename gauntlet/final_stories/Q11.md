# Q11 - tell me about a time you dove deep and optimized something

**LP:** Dive Deep (primary); touches Invent and Simplify + Deliver Results.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - EfficientNetV2S defect-classifier training (PREP STORY 14, backed by STORY 3).

## Core Answer

The real call here wasn't a hyperparameter (a dial you turn in the model's settings). It was whether to trust a number that looked great. I built the defect classifier for a PCB inspection suite - a cropped photo of a component gets sorted as good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing. I'd never trained a production model from scratch, and the tools were already on the floor with manufacturing engineers using them.

My first run printed 99% training accuracy - getting 99 of every 100 right on the very photos it had just studied. Easy to high-five. But validation was 72%, and validation is the check against photos kept back from training - the test data the model is scored on while it's being tuned. That gap is a model memorizing, not learning. I could have shipped the flattering headline and found out on the floor. Instead I chose to chase the cause rather than throw a bigger model at the symptom, and to reuse a pretrained network's features - a model that had already learned to "see" on millions of ordinary photos - instead of training from scratch, which is the whole point of transfer learning.

The cause was catastrophic forgetting - the borrowed knowledge getting erased. My classification head, the network's final decision-making layer, starts random, so early on it fires big noisy gradients - the error signals that tell each layer how to adjust - back into the pretrained layers and wipes out the general features - edges, textures - I was trying to borrow. So I split training into two phases, a technique called progressive unfreezing: let the head settle with the base frozen - locked so nothing in it can change - then gently thaw the top layers, unlocking them, at a much lower learning rate - the size of each self-correction the model makes, so a lower rate means gentler changes - so they adapt to PCBs without being rewritten.

What made it a dive, not a guess, was the auditing. I logged every run in Weights & Biases - a training journal that keeps every attempt on record so results are compared from data, not memory - instead of trusting memory, and I tested assumptions I'd normally just accept - how many layers to thaw (too few underfit, i.e. too limited to learn the job; too many overfit, i.e. memorize the training photos; so I found the middle empirically - by measurement, not by rule of thumb), and whether the usual learning-rate gap between the two phases really held on my data. On the data side I rebuilt the split so two photos of the same component never landed on both sides - the same physical part can't be in both the study pile and the test pile, because that leaks familiarity into the score (data leakage), corrected the wild floor lighting with CLAHE, an image filter that evens out brightness, and up-weighted the rare defect classes - made a missed defect cost the model more than a misread good part - because "good" outnumbered every defect several to one - so raw accuracy lies (a model that just guesses "good" every time still looks mostly right) and recall is the honest metric: of all the real defects, how many did we catch.

The result: validation climbed from 72% to about 94% on the roughly 300-image validation holdout - the set-aside photos the model never trained on, used only for checking - and the train-to-validation gap closed to a few points, about 3, so the study score and the test score nearly matched: it was genuinely generalizing now - working on unseen photos, not just the ones it studied - with recall above 90% for every defect category, meaning in each category it caught more than 9 out of 10 real defects.

What I'd change: I'd assumed more thawed layers always help - the overfitting config proved that wrong - and I'd have wanted far more labeled images - photos with the correct answer attached by a human - than I had.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: First training run memorized - 99% training (graded on the photos it studied) but 72% validation (graded on photos kept aside for checking).
- Customer: Manufacturing engineers, tools already live on the floor.
- Insight: The train/val gap = catastrophic forgetting; protect the borrowed features, and trust the validation number + recall (share of real defects caught), never the training number or raw accuracy.
- Action (mine): Two-phase progressive unfreezing (lock the borrowed layers first, then unlock the top ones at a gentler step-size); logged every run in W&B (the training journal); tested layer-count and LR-ratio (the step-size ratio between the two phases) instead of assuming; component-level 70/15/15 split to block leakage (the same part appearing in both study and test piles); CLAHE (brightness-equalizing filter) for floor lighting; up-weighted defect classes.
- Result: 72% -> ~94% validation on the ~300-image holdout (checking-only photos never trained on), small train/val gap, >90% recall every category.
- Learning: Transfer learning doesn't prevent overfitting (memorizing instead of generalizing); more unfrozen layers isn't always better; a single number needs a named set and a logged comparison, not memory.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. What exactly was the two-phase recipe? - Phase one: whole EfficientNetV2S base frozen, train the head 10 epochs at 0.001 (an epoch = one full pass through all the training photos; 0.001 = the learning rate, i.e. the size of each adjustment the model makes to itself). Phase two: unfreeze the last 30 layers, 20 epochs at 0.0001 (10x lower), early stopping patience 5 (training halts by itself if the check score stops improving for 5 straight rounds). [SAFE]
2. Why does phase order matter? - Random head = large noisy gradients (error signals) that overwrite ImageNet's general features - ImageNet being the huge public photo collection the base model first learned on - if the base is open from the start. Freezing it while the head stabilizes is what stops the forgetting. [SAFE]
3. How did you pick the number of layers to thaw - was 30 luck? - I tested fewer and more in W&B: too few underfit (couldn't learn the job), too many overfit (memorized the training photos) on ~2,000 images, 30 was the middle. Honest caveat: the evidence is directional across runs; I never took run-to-run variance (never repeated the same config from different random starting points to see how much the number bounces), so I defend it by the mechanism and the closed train/val gap, not confidence intervals (statistical error bars around a number). [PARTIAL]
4. Why that learning-rate ratio between phases? - Conventional 10x split - the second phase's step-size is one tenth of the first's. I verified it rather than trusted it - tried higher base rates in logged runs and kept the one that validated best (scored best on the set-aside checking photos). [SAFE]
5. How did you split, and why component-level? - 70/15/15, stratified (each class present in proportion in every slice), split by component so the same part never appears in two sets. Otherwise memorization leaks straight into validation - the model gets graded on photos of parts it already studied - which is ~300 of ~2,000. [SAFE]
6. How did you handle imbalance, and what was your real metric? - Good parts outnumbered defects about 5:1, so an always-good model scores ~83% (says "good" every time and still gets the majority right by default). I weighted defect classes 5x in the loss (the mistake-score the model tries to minimize, so a missed defect now counts five times as heavily) and tuned on precision-recall, not raw accuracy - judging both how many real defects it caught (recall) and how many of its alarms were genuine (precision), instead of the overall percent-right that the imbalance flatters. [SAFE]
7. Can you give per-class recall? - What I stand behind is above 90% for every category. My notes carry exact per-class decimals, but at this set size each defect class is only a couple dozen images - one sample moves a point (a single photo flipping right-or-wrong visibly shifts the percentage) - so I won't quote them as defensible. [PARTIAL]
8. Which set is the 94% on? Isn't there a "500-image test set" in your notes? - Yes, and I'd flag it. The 94% I lead with is validation - the score on the ~300 checking-only photos - on the documented ~300 holdout (photos excluded from training and used only to score). A separate note records the same figure on a "500-image held-out test," which doesn't reconcile with 15% of ~2,000. I keep them apart rather than blending them. [GAP]
9. What about the 45ms / retrain time? - Inference benchmarked ~45ms per image on CPU against a <100ms bar - 45ms = the time to classify one photo on an ordinary computer chip with no special graphics hardware, and <100ms was the speed target the tool had to beat - single-image benchmark, hardware unrecorded, and the shipped path was TensorFlow.js in-browser (the model converted to run inside a plain web browser), so it's a benchmark, not a floor. A retrain took about 30 minutes on one GPU, so the pipeline could absorb new defect types. [PARTIAL]
10. Alternatives you rejected / what you'd do differently? - Not reaching for a bigger model (would've just memorized faster); reused ImageNet features + CLAHE (the brightness-equalizing filter) instead of a bespoke network; I'd use a cosine schedule rather than a fixed phase-two rate - a standard recipe that eases the step-size down smoothly over time - and collect far more labels. Controlled lighting would've helped but wasn't an option on the floor. [SAFE]
11. Your repo's script has a different config. Which is real? - Both, different times and tasks. The checked-in `pcb_training_v2_fixed.py` is a later hardened variant for a different binary present/absent dataset - a simpler yes/no question, "is the part there or not," rather than the four-way sort (its own comments log the changes: more unfrozen layers, lower rate, clipping - a cap on runaway error signals). Bigger dataset -> more layers you can afford, so its numbers don't contradict this run. I won't fuse the two records. [SAFE]
12. Then which script produced your 94%? - None I can hand you. The 4-class pipeline isn't in the repo; the shipped artifact's head is a binary `Dense(1, sigmoid)` - a single yes/no output layer, i.e. a part-present-or-not detector, not the four-way classifier. So the 94% is the story-bank's documented run, not an artifact - the first mismatch a skeptical interviewer would find, which is why I name it myself. [GAP]
13. Hardest part / how reliable were results? - Hardest was resisting the good-looking number and proving *why* it was wrong. Reliability: configs compared as logged W&B runs; headline figures are single final-run measurements - no repeated seeds (the same setup was never rerun several times to see how far the numbers wander). [PARTIAL]
14. Biggest disagreement with your earlier self? - I assumed more unfrozen layers always help; the overfit config falsified it, and I assumed CLAHE fully fixed lighting; some extremes survived it (a few too-bright/too-dark photos still fooled the filter). [SAFE]
