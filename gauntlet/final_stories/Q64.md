## Core Answer

First, a boundary, because this question comes glued to someone else's scene: an internship, an ETL pipeline, a legacy backend, a manager who wanted it done differently. That's the scraped poster's situation, not mine, and I won't wear someone else's memory.

Second, I won't invent the manager disagreement either. I built the inspection suite - my tools that check factory-built circuit boards against their design drawings - solo; no manager appears anywhere in my record. **[GAP]**: there is no lived event of being overruled from above. What I can give you is the pattern my record does show, labeled as a framework.

The closest real disagreement was with my own assumption. I'd aligned board photos to the CAD layout - the board's engineering design drawing - using an affine transform, because it was the simple textbook move and worked on flat straight-on shots. An affine transform, plainly, is basic image-alignment math that stretches or shifts the whole picture evenly and keeps parallel lines parallel. It was wrong by construction - affine keeps parallel lines parallel, and a photographed board doesn't. I didn't argue preference; I measured. First I moved to a homography - a perspective fix that accounts for the angle the photo was taken from. Homography fixed the global perspective but real photos still averaged 15-20 pixels off, because boards flex in the reflow oven - the hot oven that solders the parts on, which bends a board slightly. (That number in plain terms: on average a part in the photo sat 15-20 pixels away from where the design drawing said it should be, and that gap is what makes the comparison tool cry wolf.) So I added a thin-plate spline warp - a fine, local flex correction that bends the image piece by piece, like pressing a thin sheet from behind - and the average came down to 2-3 pixels. The test settled it. The same reflex went against a rule: the tool matched parts on raw pin counts - the number of little metal pins sticking out of each part; I thought that was wrong, so I sorted twenty real BOMs - bills of materials, the parts list for each board - into three mismatch buckets before I said a word.

So, if a manager hands me a direction I disagree with:

1. **Try to disconfirm myself first.** Ask what context I'm missing. Half of all disagreements should die here, and one did: a manufacturing engineer's floor knowledge corrected my variant parser - the code that reads parts-list notation, where `R1||R2` means one board position where either of two interchangeable parts is acceptable - and I changed it instead of defending it.
2. **Bring the smallest decisive evidence.** One discriminating test - a check whose result can't fit both sides of the argument - beats a volume of opinion.
3. **Disagree once, privately, framed as a risk to the customer or the business** - not as who's right.
4. **If I'm overruled, commit fully** and ask what evidence would reopen the question. On pin count I kept the signal and made it discriminating - sharp enough to actually tell good from bad - instead of half-implementing it to prove a point.
5. **Escalate only on values.** Disagree-and-commit is for reversible calls; safety, compliance, or a broken customer promise are the one line where committing isn't the whole answer. I haven't lived it - a principle, not a scene.

That's the honest shape: the data reflex and the commit discipline are documented; the manager half is a framework - labeled as one.
