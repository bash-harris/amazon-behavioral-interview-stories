# Q7 - tell me about a time you failed

**LP:** Dive Deep (caught a misleading metric) + Earn Trust (owned the wrong assumption).   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the defect classifier that "passed" by predicting good every time (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md class-imbalance finding).

## Core Answer

A model I trained nearly reached production looking great and being useless, and the failure was that I trusted the wrong number.

My PCB inspection tool had to sort each part photo into good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing. To train that classifier I gathered around two thousand component images, and the data was heavily lopsided: roughly five ordinary boards for every one genuine defect, because good boards are what the line actually produces. I trained a standard image classifier and read the headline number back. Accuracy sat high - about 83 percent - and I took that as success.

It wasn't. With data that imbalanced, a model that answered "good" every single time still scores about 83 percent, because most examples really are good. My accuracy number was measuring how well the model agreed with the majority label, not whether it could catch a defect - the one thing it existed for. Read per-class, its ability to find real defects was effectively zero. I had built, and nearly shipped, a rubber stamp that would wave bad boards through while looking healthy in a report.

I caught it before deployment only because I pulled up the per-category detail instead of trusting the top-line figure. Seeing the defect classes at near-zero recall made it obvious the "83%" was an illusion. I owned it as my mistake: I had picked a metric that flattered the imbalance instead of one that tested the job.

The fix had two parts. I rebalanced how much the training process cared about each class, so missing a rare defect cost the model more than missing a common good part, and I switched what I judged the model on - from accuracy to precision-and-recall curves per category. Then recall on the defect classes rose to the above-90-percent bar the line needed, and the number finally meant what I claimed it meant.

What I changed permanently: never quote a single accuracy figure for an imbalanced problem. Show the per-class result, and pick the metric that matches which error is actually dangerous - here, a missed defect ships a bad board, while a false alarm just costs a few seconds of human checking. Those costs are not equal, so the scoring can't treat them equally either.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: defect classifier looked strong at ~83% accuracy.
- Cause of failure: ~5:1 good-to-defect imbalance + I judged on accuracy, the wrong metric.
- What the model was really doing: answering "good" almost always, so "accuracy" only mirrored the majority class; defect recall ~0.
- Detection: I opened per-class results before shipping and saw defect recall near zero.
- Fix: reweight classes so rare defects matter more; judge on per-category precision/recall, not accuracy; defect recall cleared the >90% bar.
- Learning: choose the metric that matches the asymmetric cost (missed defect >> false alarm); report per-class.

## Follow-Up Bank
- "class imbalance" / "reweighting" plainly: the dataset had about five good parts for every defective one, so the model learned that always guessing "good" scores well; reweighting tells the trainer "a missed defect costs more than a missed good part."
- "precision and recall" plainly: recall = of all the real defects, how many did it catch; precision = of everything it flagged as a defect, how many really were. For safety-critical sorting, recall on the rare, dangerous class is what matters.
