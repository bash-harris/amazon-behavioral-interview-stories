# Q55 - tell me about a time you had to motivate a team after a demoralizing event

**LP:** Earn Trust (primary); Think Big - *no team and no demoralizing event exist in the record; the motivating scene is a pure GAP*   **Story:** GAP. Closest documented truth: recovering the manufacturing engineers' trust after they distrusted the tool (STORY 11). No team invented; the question's college gesture-device project is the thread poster's, not the candidate's.

## Core Answer

Honestly, up front: I built the PCB inspection suite solo (a PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device). There was no team for me to motivate, and I won't borrow the college gesture-controlled device from the question - a student project where you control a gadget by waving your hand - that's the poster's story, not mine. So the motivating-a-team scene is a pure gap. The closest true thing I have is a group that lost faith in my work - and what they lost was trust, not morale. I won't stretch that further than it goes.

The manufacturing engineers didn't believe the BOM report - the bill of materials, the official list of which parts belong on each board. They re-checked everything by hand because the tool's verdicts came without reasons. My first move wasn't encouragement - it was naming the facts plainly. The parser - the part of the code that reads those parts-list files - worked on about 70% of real BOMs and then broke in production; I said so. (That 70% is a before-the-fix figure counted on real files, not a formal benchmark.) The pin-count rule - the check that counts a connector's little metal legs to confirm the right part sits in the right place - threw false alarms, meaning it flagged as errors things that were actually fine, on roughly 12% of mixed boards, which are boards packed with many different part types; I didn't defend it - I pulled the failing boards, sorted every mismatch into categories, and changed the rule.

Second, I pointed at a direction worth walking into. The factory floor killed the server approach - the original design needed a shared computer on the network running the software, and the factory had no internet, no installs, no IT tickets. So the blocker became the design: the whole suite as one self-contained file that just opens - a single file you double-click and it runs, no setup - tens of megabytes, seconds to load. (The size and load time are kept deliberately rough: a ballpark description, not a stopwatch number.)

Third, I made the next step small, visible, and mine. I shipped an auditable debug view so they could check every decision - a "show your work" mode where the report explains the reason behind each flag it raises. In the first week they surfaced two real edge cases, unusual boards the rule hadn't anticipated; I fixed both. I did the hardest analysis myself and sequenced the tools so each finished piece paid for the next.

Then they stopped hand-checking and started relying on it in production. That's verifiable action, not motivation.

The framework I'd bring to a real team is the same order: facts, a defensible direction, a small visible win, take the hardest part yourself, follow-through they can audit. The lived event itself has to come from real team experience - this record can't honestly supply it.
