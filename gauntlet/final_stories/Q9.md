# Q9 - tell me about a time you were proud of your work

**LP:** Ownership (primary, best-evidenced); Deliver Results (the 8-week window); Think Big (narrow: the unified-suite direction).   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - three-tool suite delivered solo and used on the production floor (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 5; `AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 12).

## Core Answer

I'm proudest of the PCB inspection suite I built at Bosch. A PCB is the green circuit board inside almost every electronic device. I owned it end to end - and it stopped being my project. It became how the manufacturing team verified boards.

The process had three verification steps, and engineers did all three by hand with different software. Does the bill of materials match the schematic? (The bill of materials - the BOM - is the board's parts list: every component with its part number. The schematic is the design drawing showing how the parts should connect.) Does the physical board match its design? Does a new board match a known-good reference - a board already checked and accepted as correct? Document-to-document, image-to-document, image-to-image - different alignment, different failure modes. The BOM step alone was 20-30 minutes of cross-referencing per board; that baseline is the engineers' own reported time for one manual check - what they said it took, not a stopwatch study. I was the only engineer on the suite.

My job: deliver all three as one suite - reliable, usable by non-technical engineers, maintainable by one developer, standalone on locked-down floor machines: one self-contained file that just opens in a browser, on factory computers that block installing new software.

The design: share the infrastructure, specialize the logic. One skeleton - upload and validation, preprocessing (tidying the inputs before comparison), auto-save, reports, UI - built once. Three separate comparison engines: entity matching for the BOM - pairing each line of the parts list to its counterpart on the drawing even when the wording differs; homography alignment for the board-vs-layout images - math that warps a photo taken at an angle so it lines up square with the flat design drawing; and a multi-signal pipeline for board-vs-board - several independent image checks combined instead of trusting any one. I considered one universal engine and rejected it: the inputs and alignment differ fundamentally; the abstraction would add complexity without value. I paid in duplication, knowingly. The UX was identical everywhere - drag-and-drop, green match, red mismatch, blue unknown. Learn one tool, learn all three. Fix auto-save once, all three improve.

I built in impact order - BOM first, the biggest time sink - and delivered all three inside eight weeks.

The part I'm proudest of happened after delivery. Engineers didn't trust the BOM verdicts. I didn't argue. I built a debug view so they could audit every decision: raw text, preprocessing steps, the rule behind each verdict. In its first week they found two heuristics I had wrong - heuristics are the rules of thumb I'd coded into the matching logic; users caught two bad ones in week one. That audit trail made them willing to rely on it for production.

Honest note on the numbers. Per board, 20-30 minutes became a report in seconds - the minutes are engineer-reported, the seconds are the tool's own run time - and the hardened tool caught three real mismatches in early production use that manual review had missed; three actual defective boards, each a documented find, not an estimate. Adoption I never measured. The suite ran on both sites' lines - the two plants - that's certain. "Fifteen people, twenty hours a week" - about 15 engineers using it and roughly 20 hours a week of their time they believed it saved - is engineers' recollection and estimate, not my stat. My pride doesn't rest on it. It rests on users being able to falsify my tool's reasoning - try to prove each call wrong - and doing it.

If I did it again: shared infrastructure before the first tool, floor BOMs - real parts lists from the production line - before the parser, and measure what you intend to claim.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. What was the hardest part of owning all three tools? - Balancing shared infrastructure against specialized logic. Getting the skeleton right - the common plumbing every tool uses (uploads, saving, reports) - kept the tools consistent and maintainable by one developer; the risk was over-abstracting, forcing three genuinely different comparisons into one generic engine, so I deliberately kept the comparison engines separate. I accepted the duplication cost knowingly. [SAFE]
2. How did you deliver three tools in eight weeks on your own? - Impact order: BOM tool first, the most time-consuming manual process on the floor; each tool stood on infrastructure the previous had paid for; I learned OpenCV.js - a free, open-source image-processing library that runs inside the browser - on the alignment-heavy second tool rather than waiting to know it. [SAFE]
3. Why not one universal comparison engine? - The three comparisons are document-to-document, image-to-document, image-to-image - text against text, photo against drawing, photo against photo. Different formats, different alignment algorithms (the math that lines two pictures up despite angle and scale differences); an abstraction layer would have added complexity without value. The trade: more code duplication, clearer separation of concerns. [SAFE]
4. What's the evidence it actually mattered? - What I stand behind: both sites used it for production verification; the BOM step went from 20-30 minutes to a report in seconds - the minutes are the engineers' reported baseline for one manual cross-check, the seconds are the tool's own run time; the hardened parser (the code that reads the parts-list files) caught three real mismatches manual review had missed - three actual defective boards, each a documented find; engineers found two wrong heuristics (rules of thumb in the matching code) in the debug view's first week - users auditing me is evidence of real use. What I can't stand behind as measurements: the ~15 users and ~20 hours a week - how many people used it and how much time they believed it saved; my recollection and their arithmetic on the per-board baselines, never instrumented. [SAFE]
5. How did you make it usable by non-developers? - Identical UX across all three (same drag-and-drop, green/red/blue output), so the workflow was learned once. Browser-based by requirement: it had to deploy standalone - a single self-contained file that just opens in a browser - on machines where installing software wasn't an option; no install, no IT ticket between an engineer and a comparison. [SAFE]
6. Which of the three was hardest, and why? - PCB vs Layout: steepest learning curve, learning OpenCV.js (the browser image-processing library) and implementing the CAD alignment and parsing side - lining a photo up with the computer-aided-design drawing files and reading those drawings. Good Board was the most algorithmically complex - its matching logic was the trickiest - but it stood on infrastructure the first two had paid for. [SAFE]
7. What went wrong along the way? - Twice, publicly. Early after the BOM tool deployed, engineers reported misses on a meaningful fraction of boards - boards the tool passed that weren't actually fine - I chased root causes systematically instead of patching the boards I happened to look at, and every fix went into a regression suite: a stored set of representative production BOMs the tools re-run after every change so old mistakes stay fixed. And the results initially weren't trusted at all - that pressure is what produced the debug view (a screen showing, for each verdict, the inputs and the rule behind it), the feature the tool needed most. [SAFE]
8. What did the architecture cost you? - Deliberate duplication between tools; the three ended up with slightly different error-handling patterns (ways of reacting when something goes wrong) because they were built at different times; the parser is tightly coupled to SAP export formats - SAP is the company's business-management software, and my code reads the fixed file layout its reports come out in, so if that layout changes the reader breaks - accepted because the constraint was real and immediate. Those are the debts I'd name unprompted. [SAFE]
9. Was there disagreement? - With the users, yes: they didn't trust the BOM verdicts. I didn't argue or blame them - I built the audit path (raw text, each preprocessing step, the result, the rule that produced it, plus a decision tree and dashboard) and let them falsify it - try to prove each call wrong. Two of my heuristics - the rules of thumb I'd coded - died in week one. That converted the skeptics better than any defense would have. [SAFE]
10. Did it scale beyond those two sites? - My sources only ever cover the two sites' lines - the two plants where the suite ran. Honest answer: "It was scoped to the two plants where it ran; I can't tell you about rollouts beyond that." Do not invent a larger footprint. [GAP]
11. How were the numbers measured? - One by one, plainly: the per-board baseline (20-30 min) counts how long one engineer's manual parts-list cross-check took, reported by the people doing it - reported, not timed; the seconds-long report is the tool's own run time, not human time; the three mismatches are documented early-production finds after the edge-case fixes - actual defective boards, each a recorded case. Adoption counts were never tracked at all - which is exactly why the core story leans on "both sites, production verification, users auditing me" instead of a tracked stat. [PARTIAL - per-board and mismatch facts solid; adoption honestly disowned]
12. What would you do differently? - Build the shared infrastructure before the first tool instead of retrofitting it; collect representative BOMs from the floor - real production parts lists, not idealized examples - before writing the parser; and if you're going to claim an outcome, measure it - or say it's an estimate, like I just did.
