# Q70 - tell me about a time you disagreed with a senior engineer

## Core Answer

The honest thing first: I can't point to a real disagreement with a senior engineer. I built the inspection suite solo, and the strongest backbone episode in my record is against a tool's measurement rule, not a person. Have Backbone is my thinnest area - I'd rather say that than glue something together to fit the question. In plain terms: the inspection suite is the software I wrote, entirely on my own, to check finished circuit boards for mistakes; a "measurement rule" is the fixed standard the software itself uses to call something an error; "Have Backbone" is Amazon's name for standing your ground, with data, when you think the plan is wrong; and "glue something together" means inventing a scene just to fit the question.

So here's what's real, then what I'd bring.

What's real: the pin-count rule. My reconciliation tool compared BOM pin counts to footprint pad counts, literally, and flagged any difference as a mismatch. In plain words: the BOM is the full parts list for a board; a component's pins are its little metal legs; a footprint's pads are the solder spots drawn on the board for those legs to sit on; the tool simply counted legs against solder spots and raised an alarm whenever the two numbers differed. I thought that was wrong - a bigger footprint is often a legitimate package variant. A "package variant" means the same electrical part is offered in more than one physical casing, so a different number of solder spots can be perfectly legitimate, not a defect. But "I thought so" is a preference, not a disagreement. So I pulled 20 BOMs with pin-count mismatches and sorted every one into three buckets: footprint larger, footprint smaller, exact. That 20 is the number of real parts lists the rule had flagged on this point, and the three "buckets" are just the categories I hand-sorted them into - my own count of the files, not an instrumented statistic. Larger was almost always valid; smaller can't physically work. That turned an argument into a finding the team could act on, and the rule changed - with pin count kept in as a discriminating signal, not deleted, because I owned the leftover risk. "Discriminating signal" means the pin count stayed in as one useful clue weighed together with the other checks instead of firing a stand-alone alarm; the "leftover risk" is the real mistakes the softer rule might now miss, and I said out loud that I was answerable for them.

Also real: I update when the evidence says better. A controlled test showed my affine alignment 15-20 pixels off at a bent board's far edge while homography sat at 2-3, so I dropped the simpler method I'd built. Plain words: "alignment" is warping the design picture so it lands exactly on top of the photo before the two are compared; "affine" and "homography" are a simpler and a richer kind of that warp, and only the richer one follows a board that's bent or tilted; "pixels" are picture dots, so being 15-20 dots off at the far edge is visibly wrong; a "controlled test" is the same boards photographed the same way so both methods get a fair, repeatable comparison. And when a manufacturing engineer told me my parser was dropping valid `R1||R2` variants, he knew the floor and I didn't - I fixed the code. The "parser" is the code that reads the parts-list file; `R1||R2` is the notation for a line listing interchangeable alternative parts; "knew the floor" means he knew how the factory actually builds boards and I didn't.

What I'd bring to a senior - labeled as a framework, not a memory: assume they've seen failure modes I haven't, so I ask what I'm missing before I assume they're wrong. Then disagree privately and directly, with the specific data and the specific risk, never in a group thread. Argue the finding, not the org chart, and say out front what evidence would flip me. That last phrase means the evidence, not the job title, decides - and I name my own conditions for changing my mind up front. If they still call it against me, I build it like it was my own decision and don't relitigate it sideways. "Sideways" means no quietly re-arguing it afterwards one-on-one with other people, and no doing the new plan grudgingly or badly to prove a point. Half-committing is the worst outcome. One line I'd hold: if it's not a reversible business call but a compliance, safety, or customer-trust obligation, I'd raise it up the chain, documented - I've never had to, and I have. In plain words: a reversible business call is a choice that can be undone without harm if it turns out wrong; compliance, safety, and customer trust are not; and "up the chain, documented" means going above that person, in writing.
