# Q85 - describe a time when you needed to deep dive to solve a problem

**LP:** Dive Deep (primary); Are Right, A Lot (touch - changed my belief about the score on evidence).   **Story:** BGSW PCB suite - the code audit that found the letterbox-padding bias in the SSIM component score (BAR_RAISER_TECHNICAL_PREP.md Finding 3). Distinct from Q11/Q16/Q20/Q33/Q52: this is the scoring-metric audit, and it is diagnosis-only - the fix is scoped, not landed, and I claim exactly that.

## Core Answer

The deep dive I'd point to is one where the numbers looked fine and the metric - the formula that turns a comparison into a score - was quietly wrong. I was building the component comparison in the PCB inspection tool - crop a part from the board photo and from the layout, score how different they are, flag the ones that differ. ("Crop" = cut out the small picture of one part; "layout" = the board's design file; "score" = one number saying how much the two cut-out pictures match.) The pipeline produced plausible scores. I didn't trust the pass rates - how often parts sail through as OK - so I went into the scoring code to see exactly how the number was made.

The first thing I found was a normalization step - a make-the-pictures-the-same-shape step taken before comparing - doing more than it admitted. To compare crops of different shapes, I letterboxed each one to a 128x128 tile (a square picture 128 dots across by 128 down), padding with a constant gray to keep the aspect ratio - the width-to-height proportions - the way a widescreen movie gets bars on the sides instead of being stretched. Then SSIM - structural similarity - was computed over the entire tile, padding included, and reduced to a single number by taking the mean of the similarity map: instead of reporting how alike each spot is one by one, all those spot-scores get averaged into one figure. SSIM is a standard, published formula for how much two pictures resemble each other - not something I invented - and on its scale 1 means a perfect match.

That's where the dive paid off. The padding is identical in both images by construction - by the very way the framing step works, same gray, same rectangle - so local SSIM there is essentially a perfect match. The mean drags the score toward "pass," in proportion to how much of the crop is padding. A part with a 2:1 aspect ratio - twice as wide as tall, or the reverse - is about half padding, so half its score is guaranteed "identical." That "half" is plain geometry, not a measurement: a 2:1 picture forced into a square frame is half border by area. The detector was systematically most blind on exactly the parts I cared about - tall connectors and wide ICs (integrated circuits - the chips on a board).

I read the scoring path - the whole chain of code that produces the score - end to end, checked the full-tile mean was live in every copy of the file, and traced the aspect-ratio dependence to the one step that correlated with shape. That part of the work is done and solid.

The fix I want to be precise about: it's diagnosed and scoped, not landed - I worked out what's wrong and exactly what the change would be, but I have not made the code change and nothing has been re-measured. The change is small - restrict the mean to the valid region using the crop bounds the letterbox step already computes, meaning the code already knows where the real picture sits inside its frame, or pass a validity mask - a per-pixel flag marking which dots are real image and which are just padding. It's on my to-build list. I didn't make the change, I didn't re-measure it, and without a labeled set - pictures with the right answer recorded by a human - I couldn't quote a recall delta, i.e. how much better at catching real problems the fix would make the tool - so I won't.

What I keep from it: a metric is only as good as the region it's averaged over, and a normalization step can inject a bias that never crashes anything - it just makes the numbers plausible and wrong. And a diagnosis is something I did; a fix and its delta are things I'd have to show.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: component-similarity scores looked plausible but pass rates didn't hold my trust; metric suspected quietly wrong
- Customer: factory engineers relying on the flags - the detector was least sensitive on connectors/wide ICs, the parts whose absence matters most
- Insight: letterbox padding is identical in both images -> local SSIM  1 there -> full-tile mean dilutes real content in proportion to padded fraction; bias scales with aspect ratio
- Action (mine): read the scoring path end to end; isolated padding as the only shape-correlated step; confirmed the full-tile mean live in the shipped code; scoped the fix to the already-computed crop bounds (ROI-restricted mean or validity mask)
- Result: a documented, mechanistically-explained bias + a precise fix plan - diagnosis landed; fix NOT applied, NO measured delta claimed
- Learning: metrics are only as good as the region they average over; normalization can inject silent bias; claim the diagnosis, not an unshown fix

## Follow-Up Bank
1. How did you find it - did something crash? - No. Nothing crashed; that's what made it worth the dive. There was no score anomaly to chase - I just read the scoring path, the entire chain of code from the cut-out pictures to the final number, end to end, and padding was the only step correlated with shape. [SAFE]
2. Walk me through the mechanism. - Crop padded to a 128x128 square - 128 dots by 128 dots; that size is a configuration value set in my own code and read straight from the shipped file, so it's exact, not a measurement - with constant gray. SSIM computed over the whole tile, reduced by a mean. In the padding the two images are identical by construction, so local SSIM is essentially 1 - the top of its scale, a perfect match; the mean is pulled toward "pass" in proportion to the padded fraction, which rises with aspect ratio. Real-content similarity gets diluted by guaranteed-perfect padding. [SAFE]
3. Have you fixed it? - No, and I want to be exact. I identified the fix - reduce the SSIM map over the offset/width/height rectangle the letterbox step already computes, in plain terms: average only the pixels inside the real picture, whose position and size the code already stores - or pass a validity mask, a per-pixel marker of real-vs-padding - but the code still averages the full padded tile, and my prep notes list "fix and measure" as a to-build item. I won't present designed work as built. [SAFE]
4. What would measuring it take? - A labeled set: pictures of parts with the right answer recorded by a human - roughly two hundred components, deliberately heavy on non-square connectors and ICs, run before and after the ROI restriction (ROI = region of interest, the real-picture rectangle), with a precision-recall curve so the fix has a real before/after - a chart of how many of the flags are genuine defects versus how many genuine defects get caught. The ~200 is my planned sample size, an intent, not a dataset that exists yet. My own training-data collector already does crop labeling - that's the tool I'd reuse. [SAFE - framed as plan, not result]
5. How long to land the change? - The code change itself is a couple of hours - my own estimate as the person who wrote and audited that code, not a tracked figure. The honest part - labels plus the before/after measurement - is the longer loop, and that's the part I hadn't closed. [SAFE - estimate, labeled as such]
6. What else did you find in that function? - Two smaller warts: an intermediate value computed and then recomputed - the code did the same arithmetic twice, with a comment admitting the first pass was a hack - and an allocation that was never used and deleted at the end - memory the program reserved for a value it never read. No effect on results, but they told me the path had never been scrutinized. [SAFE]
7. Why did a bug like that survive? - The scoring functions were pure - they just take inputs and return outputs without touching anything outside themselves - but lived in files that touched the DOM at load time, the live structure of a web page, so nothing could import them and no headless test - an automated test run without opening a browser window - could run. If SSIM had been testable in isolation, one aspect-ratio case would have exposed the bias immediately. Making the core importable is the fix behind the fix. [SAFE]
8. Why not quote how much SSIM counts for in the overall score? - Because my stories give different weight formulations for the comparison engine, and blending them would invent a number. The bias mechanism doesn't need the weight - a biased signal is biased whatever its weight. [SAFE - single-telling discipline]
9. So what did you actually change? - The dive was the change to my understanding, and it's verified work: full read of the scoring path, the aspect-ratio dependence traced to padding, the bug confirmed live in the shipped code. The code change is scoped and scheduled, not shipped. [SAFE - the critic's grounding: "gets work done" = the completed audit]
10. Hardest part? - Saying "I found the bug and I'm not going to pretend I fixed it." The temptation was one confident sentence closing the story; honesty left it open-ended, which is truer. [SAFE]
11. What would you do differently? - Test pure scoring functions headlessly from the start, and treat any averaged metric's denominator - every pixel the average sweeps up, gray borders included - as a first-class thing to audit. And if I'm going to flag a bias, budget the labeled set in the same pass so diagnosis and proof ship together. [SAFE]
