# Q17 - tell me about a time when you had to implement a direction you didn't agree with

**LP:** Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit (primary; touches Are Right, A Lot, Earn Trust) - *the "disagrees with peers and supervisors" strength is a GAP; no lived imposed-direction event exists in the record*   **Story:** No superior-issued-direction event documented. ## Core Answer

I don't have a documented case of a manager handing me a direction I disagreed with and me executing it anyway. I built the inspection suite solo - it's a software tool that checks factory circuit boards against their design files - and there's no boss scene in my record, and I won't invent one.

What I do have is how I actually behave when a call that isn't mine stands in front of me. The clearest instance is the variant decision. My BOM parser treated `R1||R2` references as invalid and dropped them. (BOM = "bill of materials": the parts list for a circuit board, saying which component goes in which spot. `R1||R2` is shorthand meaning one spot on the board can take either of two interchangeable parts.) A manufacturing engineer told me they were valid - one position, either part. My first instinct was that my rule was right, because dropping them kept the comparison simple. But he had the domain intent and I didn't. Once I saw that, I didn't keep a piece of my old logic out of pride. I adopted his semantics completely - his meaning for the notation, not a half-measure of mine - and rewrote the splitter, the code that reads those references. That's the commit part, and it's real.

The disagree part is also real, just not aimed at a person. The reconciliation tool matched parts by comparing BOM pin count to layout pad count, literally. (Pins are the metal legs of a chip; pads are the solder spots on the board where those legs land.) I thought that rule was wrong - footprints legitimately carry more pads than the chip uses (a "footprint" is the physical solder pattern a part takes up on the board; some designs include solder spots nothing connects to), so it was raising false alarms - warnings on things that were actually fine - and the engineers stopped trusting the reports. I pushed back with data instead of opinion: twenty mismatched BOMs sorted into three cases. Larger footprint: flag for review, not mismatch. Smaller: genuine mismatch. Pin count stayed in the comparison because the requirement was to kill the false alarms without blinding the tool to wrong parts, so I made the signal discriminating - able to tell ordinary design quirks apart from real problems: component-type check (are the two parts even the same kind of part), configurable two-to-one threshold (the adjustable ratio the tool allows between extra solder spots and legs before it treats the difference as meaningful) - and shipped a debug view (a screen showing the raw values behind each warning) so an engineer can see the raw values and override the tool. False positives on mixed boards went from about 12% to under 2%. (Mixed boards = boards combining several different kinds of parts; what those percentages count and how solid they are is in the follow-ups below.)

So the honest shape of my answer: state the counter-argument once, with data; once the call stands, implement it fully, as if it were mine; if execution turns up a fact nobody saw, raise that once, then respect it. **[GAP]** - what's missing is a real time a person above me made the call against my view and I executed anyway. That example has to come from team experience, not from this record.
