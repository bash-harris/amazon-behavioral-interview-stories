# Q93 - tell me about a time when you worked on something outside your comfort zone

**LP:** Learn and Be Curious (primary - had to reason like an attacker, a trade I don't practice); Invent and Simplify (replaced an expensive model call with a cheap deterministic pass); Ownership (personal build, the security call was mine)   **Story:** Tab Scroller - the anti-phishing tab quarantine: my first design (per-tab AI scans) was wrong on cost and defensibility, and the stretch was building a Zero-Trust heuristic risk check plus a quarantine UI from first principles of how scams actually look (background.js:1293-1307, 4370-4376; content.js:796-802, 1547-1549; progress.txt Task K); distinct from Q35 (same extension, MV3/state spine) and Q42 (same extension, JSON-contract spine)

## Core Answer

The uncomfortable part of this work wasn't a language or a framework - it was having to think like a scammer, which is not my trade. I build machine-vision and data tools. I was extending my personal Chrome extension - Tab Scroller, a searchable strip of all your open tabs - and decided it should warn about phishing tabs: pages that pretend to be your bank or a famous service to steal a sign-in. [PARTIAL - the shipped code and completion log are the evidence; "first design was AI-per-tab" is my account of the iteration recorded in the plan]

The obvious move for my skill set was to ask an AI model to score each tab. I did the math before committing and it was wrong three ways: one online call per tab change against a free allowance the extension can exhaust in seconds; a slow verdict; and an undefendable one - "the model felt sketchy" is not a reason to trust a page. Security reasoning was outside my experience, and paying a model didn't move it inside.

So I went the opposite way from my instinct: a deterministic pass - same input, same output, every line explainable - built by studying what fake pages actually look like. Three families of tells. Look-alike hostnames where digits impersonate letters - paypa1 with a one, micros0ft with a zero, the swap that fools the eye but not a string comparison. Throwaway top-level domains - the tail of an address - that scam infrastructure favors. And the tell that taught me most: mismatch between claim and location - a title asking to verify, sign in, or secure an account while naming a bank or brand, on an address that isn't that brand's. That's the attack in one line: it exploits the gap between what you read and where you are.

A flagged tab gets quarantined in the interface - visually marked, clicking never blocks but asks: "This tab looks suspicious. Are you sure you want to switch to it?" It defers to the human, because only they know their own context - a company intranet login, a test bank site.

What I kept from working outside my field: the right answer wasn't the technique I knew. For a security UI, simple, explainable, and free beat smart and expensive - because the human makes the call anyway, and my job was to hand them a reason, not a vibe.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: wanted phishing-tab warnings in my extension; comfort-zone first design (per-tab AI scan) failed on call cost, latency, and defensibility
- Customer: the extension's user - me first, personal build, no adoption claims; the "risk customer" is whoever clicks a fake sign-in page
- Insight: phishing works by a claim-vs-location mismatch, so the check can be deterministic tells, not judgment; explainability is the feature, not a bonus
- Action (mine): replaced the per-tab AI scan with a heuristic risk function (look-alike brand hosts, scam-favored TLDs, brand+auth-keyword claims on foreign hosts); on tab updates, flagged tabs get a quarantine mark; clicking asks an explicit confirm, no hard block; ran on every tab change, zero network calls
- Result: instant, free, per-line explainable screening shipped as the extension's anti-phishing task; no false-positive rate measured - bounded claim: it catches the pattern families it encodes [GAP]
- Learning: outside your field, resist importing your favorite tool; first principles (how the attack works) chose a dumber, better design than my instinct did

Why "Zero-Trust"? - Don't trust a tab's self-presentation: the risk comes from cross-checking what the page claims (its title) against where it actually lives (its address), and even a positive verdict still routes through a human confirm. It's my label for the rule design, not a certification. [SAFE - labeling honest]
3. Why replace the AI scan? - The optimization plan records the heuristic landing "to replace expensive per-tab AI phishing scans": free-tier call budgets, and a verdict you can defend line by line. Both the cost argument and the auditability argument. [SAFE]
4. False positives? - A legitimate page whose title says "verify your account" on an unusual domain will flag. The confirm click is the safety valve - it never blocks, it warns. A measured FP rate doesn't exist. [GAP]
5. Why confirm instead of block? - Personal tool: inform, don't adjudicate. Only the user knows context - a work intranet login, a test bank page, a redirect chain. Hard blocking someone's own legitimate tool is the worse bug. [SAFE - reasoning held as mine]
6. How did you learn the tell families? - From how impersonation works: character substitutions against big brands (the pattern list is in the code), throwaway TLDs that abuse reports keep naming, and the claim-vs-address gap. A hand-built pattern list, no training data. [SAFE]
7. What was genuinely outside your comfort zone? - Attacker-modeling and security UX. My background is making machines look at boards; this was making a human pause before they click. The design tools were different - patterns and friction, not accuracy. [SAFE]
8. Does it catch real phishing? - Unverified - I make no detection-rate claim. The bounded statement: it flags the pattern families it encodes, which is exactly what the tells are, and nothing more. [GAP - no evaluation set]
9. What would you do differently? - Log which tell fired so the rules can be tuned from evidence, instead of the current boolean OR of all families; and pull from a maintained blocklist source rather than only my hand-written patterns. [SAFE]
