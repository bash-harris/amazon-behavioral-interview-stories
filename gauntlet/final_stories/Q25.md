# Q25 - tell me about a time where you gave someone wrong information

**LP:** Earn Trust (primary); touches Ownership.   **Story:** [GAP] - no documented personal event; honest bridge to documented tool-output behavior + labeled framework.

## Core Answer

[GAP: I have no documented moment where I personally handed someone wrong information, so I won't invent a scene. Here's what I actually have.]

My tools gave the manufacturing engineers - the people on the factory floor who use my software to check circuit boards - wrong information more than once, and that's a fair proxy for how I'd handle it happening in person.

The clearest case was the BOM reconciler. A BOM is the bill of materials: the exact parts list for one circuit board. It compared BOM pin counts (how many metal legs a part has) to layout pad counts (how many solder spots the board has ready for those legs) literally, so on boards with mixed component types it raised false alarms - it flagged a problem where there wasn't one - about 12% of the time: an engineer reading that report believed a part was wrong when it wasn't. That's wrong information in front of a user. I didn't blame the data. I pulled 20 problem BOMs, sorted them into three buckets - footprint (the board's solder pattern for that part) bigger than the part, smaller, or exact - and changed the rule so a bigger footprint became a "review," not a mismatch. False positives dropped under 2%. In plain terms: of 100 problems the tool reported, the ones that weren't real fell from about 12 to under 2 - and those figures are my own manual counts from reviewing the early reports, not something the software tracked automatically. I kept the pin-count signal and owned the leftover false-negative risk - the chance of missing a real problem - instead of deleting it to make the report look clean.

Two smaller patterns. The debug view - a mode that shows, step by step, how the tool read the parts list into the computer - showed raw parse output, so when the parser was wrong, meaning the tool misread the file - it happened twice in week one - the engineer saw exactly where, and I fixed it fast. And the AI analysis, right about 85% of the time, was labeled "AI Analysis" with a confidence number - a score for how sure the AI claims to be - and a correct/incorrect button. Never presented as a verdict.

So if I give someone wrong information directly, my pattern is: say so myself, immediately, before they act on it. Correct it concretely - the right fact and the impact, not a vague sorry. No blaming the tool or the question. Explain where my error came from so they can recalibrate trust - relearn how much to lean on each kind of answer from me. Fix the process so it doesn't recur, and ask them to flag me next time.

That's the honest version: a real track record of owning wrong information my systems produced, and a stated plan for the interpersonal case I can't claim.
