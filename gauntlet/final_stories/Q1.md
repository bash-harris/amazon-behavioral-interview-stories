# Q1 - tell me about a time you used genai to improve personal or team productivity

**LP:** Invent and Simplify (primary); touches Deliver Results and Learn and Be Curious.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the GPT-4o "AI eye" analysis feature (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 8).

## Core Answer

The biggest GenAI win I've had wasn't flashy. It deleted a manual step from someone else's day. I was the only engineer on a PCB inspection suite at Bosch. A PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device. My tools took a photo of a finished board and compared it to the board's design file, then circled in red every place the two didn't look alike. The tool was great at saying *where* something was off, but not *what* it meant - was a part the wrong value, was it turned around backwards, was it missing, or was the red mark just light reflecting off the board? A manufacturing engineer had to zoom in and judge each red mark by eye. By their own estimate it took about 30 seconds per mark, and one busy board could have five to twenty of them. That human step was the last slow part left.

The idea was to add an AI that looks at one flagged spot, identifies the part, and tells the engineer what kind of mistake it is - wrong value, wrong orientation, missing, extra, or a false alarm - with a confidence level. (I used a large language model that can see images - specifically GPT-4o - as that "expert eye.")

The obvious-but-heavy route was to train my own custom image-recognition model. I didn't. A custom model needs thousands of labelled example photos, special hardware, and constant upkeep - none of that fits a lightweight browser tool. Instead I used the off-the-shelf AI model as-is and spent my real effort on everything wrapped around it: for each red mark, I cut out that component from both the design drawing and the photo, sent those two pictures together to the AI with a strict instruction, and asked it to reply in a fixed, machine-readable form (part type, mismatch type, confidence, one sentence). I chose the model by running the same test spots through a few candidates and comparing, not by building a formal scoring system.

My first instruction failed. I asked the AI to "describe what you see" and it rambled. The fix was to demand a strict answer format with an example for each mismatch type - then it became actually useful.

What changed: the engineer's "zoom in and figure it out" became a "read the suggestion and confirm" step. The AI answers in about five seconds - that's the time for the software to talk to the online service, not the engineer's time; the real saving is most of their ~30 seconds per mark disappearing. What I never did is install tracking on the feature itself, so I cannot give you usage counts. What I do have is the engineers' own word: the labels helped them decide which marks to open first on a busy board.

What I learned is to design as if the AI will be wrong. Its output shows as a suggestion with a confidence score, never a final verdict - because one confidently-wrong "that's nothing" could let a bad board pass. And if there's no network or no account set up, the AI switches itself off quietly and the red-marking tool keeps working. The AI is a bonus on top, not something the tool collapses without.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: the tool flagged *where*, not *what*; the engineers' manual zoom-and-decide (~30 s each, 5-20 marks on a busy board) was the last bottleneck.
- Customer: Bosch manufacturing engineers on the factory floor.
- Insight: use a ready-made image-capable AI model as the "eye," put my engineering into the trimming-and-instructing around it, and keep the AI optional so the tool never breaks when it's absent.
- Action (mine): solo; tested GPT-4o against GPT-4V, Claude 3, Gemini Pro Vision and an open model side by side; built the crop-and-instruct pipeline; fixed the failed first prompt; added confidence display, a "right/wrong" feedback button, and graceful offline fallback.
- Result: "zoom-and-decide" -> "read-and-confirm"; ~5 s AI turnaround; most of the ~30 s per mark saved; engineers said the labels drove their triage order; no per-feature usage numbers exist.
- Learning: start with the simplest strict instruction and refine; the labelled test set of examples the AI had never seen - which I should have built on day one - is the one I never built.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. How did you actually measure the productivity gain? - The "30 seconds" was the engineers' own estimate of how long one zoom-and-decide takes; it was not a stopwatch study. The "5 seconds" is how long the AI takes to answer - computer time, not human time. The engineer still spends a moment reading and confirming; I never costed that step. So the honest claim is "it removed most of the ~30 seconds per mark," plus their verbal feedback that the labels changed their triage order. [PARTIAL - human baseline is a self-estimate, not timed]
2. How accurate was the AI? - Two different things, kept apart:
   - The AI's own wrong/right rate: roughly **85%** correct. That's my rough self-assessment from tallying the engineers' "this analysis was right / wrong" button clicks - not a formal test on a separate set of examples the model had never seen. (A "held-out set" is exactly that: fresh examples kept back from training to score accuracy fairly - I didn't build one, so 85% is a judgment, not a measured figure.) The other ~15% were pictures too blurry or parts too tiny for the AI; I showed a low confidence on those up front.
   - The underlying **comparison engine** (the red-marking math, before any AI): that one *was* properly validated - it scored about **92%** on a "F1" measure across **100 board-photo pairs** that had known, hand-checked defect locations, versus about **78%** for a naive "just subtract the two pictures and flag the differences" method. In plain terms, F1 is a single accuracy score that balances two failure modes - not missing a real defect, and not crying wolf on something fine; 100 board pairs is the test size. That number is about the red-marking engine, *not* the AI's descriptions, so I never mix them. [PARTIAL - 85% is judgment; 92% is measured but for a different component]
3. Why GPT-4o and not another model? - I tested candidates on the real job. GPT-4V took about 8 seconds a request vs ~3 for GPT-4o - that's the delay before the answer comes back. Claude 3 saw the images well but its developer tools were less mature at the time. Gemini Pro Vision was quick but weaker on very small surface-mount components (the tiny parts soldered straight onto the board, hardest to read). I also tried an open model (LLaVA) I could run on my own computer instead of online, but it needs a graphics card with at least 8 GB of memory - which the locked-down factory machines don't have - so it was ruled out on hardware, not preference. [SAFE]
4. What complexity did you avoid? - No custom model to train or maintain. The AI is a swappable "eye" behind a simple interface, so a better model is a drop-in later. All my engineering went into trimming the images and shaping the instruction - that's the reusable part. A full scoring benchmark would have been over-engineering for a "which of these two feels better on the same examples" decision. And the AI can vanish with no harm because the red-marking keeps working. [SAFE]
5. What did you do for when the AI is wrong? - It's labelled "AI Analysis" with a confidence, framed as a suggestion, never a verdict; every analysis has a "right / wrong" button so repeated mistakes get reported back to me; if the service is down, marks still show with "AI analysis unavailable." The design principle is that the two kinds of error cost very different amounts - a wrongly-reassuring "nothing here" lets a defective board ship, while a wrongly-alarming "check this" costs the engineer ~30 seconds to dismiss. Because those costs aren't equal, the human stays the final judge. [SAFE]
6. Was it OK to send board photos to an online AI service? - It went through the security team, who approved it because the images hold no trade-secret content. For the offline "one file" version a user pastes in their own key (a "password" that lets software use a paid AI service) - the photos go straight from their machine to the provider and never through our servers. No key, and the AI feature simply isn't offered. [SAFE]
7. Did anyone push back? - Not that I can point to, and I'll own that as the thin part. The security review was the only formal gate and it passed; people adopted a tool they already used on their own. I never dug into the "wrong" button tallies hard enough to report real skepticism. [GAP - no documented resistance]
8. Does this scale? - At the everyday load (5-20 marks per board, one board at a time) the service never told me to slow down. (A "rate limit / 429" is the service's way of saying you're calling too often and to back off; I capped retries at 3 and it never tripped.) The pressure at true fleet scale would be cost and keeping the model version stable. The next design - a staged "cascade" where cheap classic image code screens every mark and only the unsure ones get sent to the paid AI, to cut cost - was drawn up but not built; its projected savings (a cost figure split into bands and roughly a 25x cheaper average) are design estimates, not measured results. [PARTIAL - projected, not measured]
9. Who used it, and how much? - I have no per-feature usage counter. For the suite as a whole: about **15 engineers across 2 sites** within roughly **2 weeks**, with team time saved estimated **~20 hours/week** - but that covers all three tools, not just the AI feature. (That ~20 hours is what the engineers told me they believed they were saving - a reported recollection, not something I instrumented.) [PARTIAL - suite-level, engineer-reported]
10. What would you do differently? - Start with the simplest strict instruction and improve from there (I got there late). Build that held-out labelled test set *before* shipping descriptions, so "how accurate?" has a real denominator. And cost out the self-hosting option from day one. [SAFE]

## Risk Check
1. "30 s -> 5 s" could be misread as a human-time before/after. It isn't: 5 s is the computer's reply time and the confirm step was never costed. Defensible only in that exact wording; a stopwatch study would be the real number.
2. The 85% has no held-out test set behind it - under pressure I state it as a judgment, and I never let it blend with the engine's separately-measured 92%. [GAP: no measured AI-description accuracy exists]
3. No per-feature usage data and no named skeptic - adoption is argued only qualitatively or via clearly-labelled suite-level, engineer-reported numbers. [PARTIAL]
