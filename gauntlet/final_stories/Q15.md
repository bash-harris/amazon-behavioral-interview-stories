# Q15 - a time when you help your friend or co-worker

**LP:** Earn Trust (primary); Hire and Develop the Best touched - peer enablement, honestly not formal coaching.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - unblocking a manufacturing engineer with the BOM-reconciliation debug view (STORY 11) plus shared-UX enablement (STORY 5).

## Core Answer

Quick honest note first: I built the PCB inspection tools solo - a PCB is the printed circuit board, the green board inside every electronic device - so my "co-worker" here was a manufacturing engineer who used them. Closest thing I had to a colleague on that project. But the help was real, and I can point at one specific person.

There was one engineer who owned the SMT line - the surface-mount line, the assembly line that solders the tiny parts directly onto boards. He brought me a board where the BOM tool had flagged a mismatch. (The BOM, the "bill of materials," is the official parts list for a board - which parts go where.) The mismatch was a single BOM line, "R1||R2," but two separate designators in the layout - a designator is the label marking where each individual part sits on the board's design file. He wanted to trust the flag, but the tool gave him no way to see why it had fired. So he was verifying every line by hand against a printout with a pen, which he told me cost him twenty to thirty minutes a board - that's his own rough estimate from conversation, a remembered feeling, not something anyone timed. And every question he had he parked on a sticky-note list to ask me. I was turning into a ticket queue.

What his visit told me: the tool couldn't explain itself. When software can't show its reasoning, distrust is rational. The trade-off I made was what to build. I could have written a how-to guide, but a manual can't answer "why did this flag on my board." So I exposed the full reasoning, step by step - I call it the debug view: add `?debug=true` to the tool's web address and it shows its work. You see the raw BOM text, the parsed reference after each cleanup step - prefix stripping (removing the label letter, like the "R" in R1 that stands for resistor) and hyperlink extraction (pulling out a link hidden inside a spreadsheet cell) - then the matched layout entry, the result, and the exact rule that fired. One rule: if any step was missing, he'd still have to ask me, and the point would be lost. While I was building, I asked him to teach me the naming: what did "||" actually mean on the line? Parallel resistors - one BOM part, two designators, meaning two resistors wired together doing one job but listed on a single line. That became the variant-splitting rule. I also kept all three tools in the suite identical in look and feel - same drag-and-drop, green match, red mismatch, blue unknown - so learning one meant learning three.

Then I went back and walked him through the view on his own board, the same part. The before-after that told me it worked: a week later the pen-marked printout was gone. He read the report in seconds - I watched him do it, it's my own observation of him at his desk, not a measured stat - and hand-checked only the red rows. And he trusted it enough to keep flagging real issues - using the debug view, he found two naming edge cases himself and reported them (two, a concrete count from my project notes, not an estimate: unusual label formats my rules didn't handle); I fixed both. His sticky-note list for me stopped growing. What I learned: the debug view should have existed from day one - I'd treated transparency as an afterthought.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: engineer couldn't verify why the BOM tool flagged a mismatch -> distrusted results, hand-checked everything, queued questions for me.
- Customer: the manufacturing engineer who owned the SMT line (stakeholder, not teammate - said so up front).
- Insight: trust comes from verifiable reasoning, not from my availability; a manual can't answer "why this flag on my board."
- Action (mine): walked him through the `?debug=true` view (the tool's show-its-work switch) on his own board; asked him to explain the "R1||R2" convention (became the variant-splitting rule); built full step-by-step transparency; uniform UX/colors across all three tools.
- Result: his ~20-30 min/board hand-verification (his reported estimate, not timed) -> reads report in seconds, spot-checks only red rows; he found 2 naming edge cases week one and reported fixes (both fixed); his question list for me stopped growing.
- Learning: transparency was an afterthought - build the "show me why" layer from day one.

6. What did you learn from him? - The "R1||R2" parallel-component convention came from him, not from any spec; and the two naming edge cases he found were real gaps my own testing had missed. [SAFE]

8. Hardest part? - Swallowing that their distrust was correct. The tool couldn't explain itself, so skepticism was the rational response. Treating "I don't believe you" as a bug report changed how I built. [SAFE]
9. What would you do differently? - Build the debug view from day one instead of after the trust gap appeared; I'd have caught the naming edge cases weeks earlier. [SAFE]
