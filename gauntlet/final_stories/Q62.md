# Q62 - a time when you got negative feedback from a higher up

**LP:** Earn Trust (primary), Learn and Be Curious - this telling centers one feedback -> one durable validation change, distinct from Q18 (reception on the same event) and Q42 (now a different feedback event - the extension JSON-contract fix)   **Story:** the five-day prototype and the one-hour team review (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md STORY 8); process lessons from STORY 10/11

## Core Answer

The most useful negative feedback from above came through my engineering lead - and the channel. He took my rough prototype and put it in front of the manufacturing team within an hour; the critique that came back was theirs - that one hour is the review's turnaround: from him sharing the unfinished tool to their problems coming back, all inside the same hour. Nobody sat me down one-on-one. But his action was itself a judgment: you don't hand unfinished work to real users unless you think it isn't ready. I won't inflate it.

The setup: engineers had wanted an automated board-comparison tool for months - a tool that checks a photo of a finished circuit board (the green boards inside electronic devices) against its design drawing and shows where the two don't match. It kept stalling as too complex to scope - too hard even to pin down what to build. So I skipped scoping and built - five days spent getting the first rough version built, one file that opens in a web browser with nothing to install (an HTML file), its code written straight into that same file (inline JavaScript - JavaScript is the language browsers run), and the simplest possible way to spot differences: a basic pixel comparison, which checks two images dot against dot, a pixel at a time. It worked under conditions I could control and was rough everywhere else.

The problems: the pixel comparison broke when the lighting changed. There was no alignment - nothing that lines the photo up with the design drawing so the same point on the board sits in the same spot in both, which meant boards had to be photographed at exactly the same angle - and no way to get a result off the screen.

The feedback that actually changed how I work was the alignment one. It wasn't the hardest fix - the reason it landed is that it couldn't be blamed on a function. The pixel diff was a function to swap - a function is a self-contained piece of code that does one job, and the "diff" is the code that finds where two images differ. Export was a feature to add - a way to save or share results instead of just reading them on screen. Alignment said something worse: the tool only worked under conditions I had arranged - same light, same angle - and real factory photos were neither. That isn't a bug in my code, it's a bug in how I validate. I'd let a demo pass on staged inputs - examples I picked by hand precisely because the tool handled them well - and called it working.

The change wasn't "build a better prototype" - I already believed in rough prototypes, and that hour proved why. The change was what I count as evidence. Before, I judged work by whether it looked right in front of me. After, I collect real inputs first - actual factory photos, actual BOMs (a "bill of materials": the list of every part that goes on a board) - and test under their conditions before I say it works. On this tool that meant alignment first, then illumination (handling the lighting differences), then export, and production-ready in two weeks - good enough for the engineers to actually use. The numbers plainly: five days counts the building, two weeks is start-to-usable; it's one record of mine, told the same way in every version of this story.

That hour cost my pride and fixed what "working" means.

## Memory Anchors

- Problem: a tool engineers wanted for months, stalled as "too complex" to scope -> my counter-move: build it rough in five days instead
- Customer: the manufacturing engineers who take real factory photos - their camera conditions were the actual spec; my arranged conditions were not
- Insight: the alignment hit was the one that couldn't be blamed on a function - the tool passed only under conditions I'd staged, so the flaw was my validation, not my code
- Action (mine): owned the critique with honest attribution (team's words, lead's escalation = his judgment); made "collect real inputs first, test under the users' conditions" the standing bar for "works"; debug views from day one on the next tool - a debug view is a display mode showing not just the tool's answer but how it reached it; failure-case -> pattern loop on the next tool
- Result: alignment -> illumination -> export, production-ready in two weeks; one hour of review out-learned months of scoping
- Learning: the feedback wasn't about the prototype - it was about what I was allowed to call working

## Follow-Up Bank

1. Was that really feedback "from a higher up"? - Honest shape: I demoed to my lead, he put it in front of the team immediately, and the team produced the critique within the hour. The higher-up element is real in one specific way - a senior reviewer judged the build worth exposing to users rather than shielding, and that exposure itself said it wasn't ready. What it was not is a private one-on-one, and I'd rather say that plainly than invent a conversation that isn't on record. [PARTIAL - premise labeled; never inflate]
2. What exactly did the lead say to you? - Nothing is on record beyond that he shared it immediately. I won't put words in his mouth. The critique belongs to the team, delivered through the review he set up. [GAP - no quote exists]
3. Why single out the alignment issue when the whole list was the team's? - Alignment was the only problem that generalized. Illumination (the lighting fix) was a broken function, export (getting results off the screen) was a missing feature - both fit inside the tool. Alignment was a mismatch between my conditions and the users', which indicts how I decide something works. That lesson transfers to any build; the other two were fixes. [SAFE]
4. What specifically changed, and what's the evidence it stuck? - Two habits carried onto later work: on the next tool's illumination, rotation, and scaling problems - photos taken at different angles or showing the board at different sizes - I collected failing cases, analyzed the patterns, fixed root causes (the underlying reason, not the symptom), and added them to the regression set instead of patching the first example I saw - a regression set is a saved collection of known failures I re-run after every fix, so old problems can't sneak back; and on a later BOM tool, the debug view - a display mode showing not just the tool's answer but how it reached it - existed from day one instead of arriving after trust eroded; it had been an afterthought there, and from day one it would have let users show me exactly which rule produced a wrong answer instead of me guessing. Both are on the record as standing practice, not a one-time apology. [SAFE]
5. How did you stay non-defensive? - I'd documented the prototype's limitations before the demo, so the review confirmed known gaps instead of exposing hidden ones. Shipping a known limitation is different from shipping a bug, and the prototype existed precisely to collect this feedback - defending it would have defended the wrong thing. [SAFE]
6. Is "collect real inputs first" measured? - No, and I won't pretend it is. It's a recorded practice change: sample BOMs from the floor before writing code, diverse real photos before claiming an edge case is handled - an edge case is one of the unusual situations where a tool tends to break. I never counted the inputs - the evidence is the changed habits on two later tools, not a metric. [PARTIAL - process claim, no number, said plainly]
7. Is the timeline solid? - One record: five days prototype, review on day five, nine days of refinement, two weeks total to production-ready - read plainly, five days building plus nine days fixing adds up to the two weeks from start to usable. What it counts: my own time on that work. How solid: the same numbers I use in every telling of this event, and the fix order was alignment, then illumination, then export. [SAFE]
8. What alternatives did you reject? - Going back to requirements scoping, which had already stalled for months; framing the lesson as "fix the pixel comparison," which is symptom-level; and polishing privately longer, which trades away the only thing the prototype was for - contact with real users. [SAFE]
9. Did you disagree with any of the feedback? - Not on substance; every problem was real. My only pushback was on emphasis: the list treated every fix as co-equal, and I sequenced alignment first because without it no comparison on a re-shot photo - one photographed a second time, so lined up slightly differently - can be trusted. The team set the problems; I owned the order. [SAFE]
10. Hardest part? - Not the code. Admitting the bar was wrong, not just the build: my standard had been "looks right in front of me," and the review showed that standard was letting broken work through. [SAFE]
11. What would you still do differently? - Collect the representative real inputs before the first version instead of after the review. The record names this as the same lesson on both the BOM parser and this tool - I discovered the conditions reactively each time, and I've since front-loaded that step. [SAFE]
12. Does this discipline apply beyond tooling? - Yes, as a rule I can say in one line: a demo passing on inputs I staged is evidence about the demo, not about the work. Anything I call "working" needs the users' conditions inside the sample first. [SAFE - stated as principle, no new events]
