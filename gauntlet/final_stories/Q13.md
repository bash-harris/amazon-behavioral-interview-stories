# Q13 - tell me about a time you compromised quality to deliver quickly

**LP:** Bias for Action (primary); Deliver Results; Insist on the Highest Standards.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the five-day first prototype (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 8); the quality-trade angle, distinct from Q10's influence angle on the same prototype.

## Core Answer

Honestly, yes - and I'll give you the real one, not a story where I never compromise quality. The manufacturing engineers needed a PCB comparison tool. A PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device, and the tool compares a photo of a finished board against its design file and flags the differences. It had sat in the backlog for six months because it was "too complex to scope properly." I judged the fastest way to learn what they actually needed was to put something working in front of them. So I built the first prototype in five days: one monolithic HTML file with inline JavaScript - a single self-contained web-page file with all its code written directly inside it - no build system (tooling that assembles and checks code before it runs), no modules (the code split into separate, interchangeable files), no tests (automated checks that catch one change silently breaking another). It loaded two images, ran a basic pixel-level comparison - matching the two pictures dot for dot and flagging where they differ - and showed the result. Under ideal conditions it was correct; under real lighting and angles it was inaccurate, and the UX - how the screen felt to use - was rough. I knew all of that before I showed it to anyone.

The compromise was named, not silent. What I cut was "works under real manufacturing conditions." What I kept was correctness under the conditions it claimed to support. My bar for "bad enough to ship" was inaccurate, not broken - a limitation I could name, not a defect I couldn't find.

Then I contained it. I demoed it with the known limits stated up front and let the team react. Within an hour they'd found three concrete gaps: pixel comparison failed under lighting changes, there was no image alignment - nothing made a board photographed at a tilt line up with the design picture - and there was no export, no way to save the results to a file. That became the roadmap, and I ordered it by impact - alignment first, illumination normalization (equalizing the lighting between the two pictures so brightness isn't mistaken for damage) second, export last.

And I paid the debt back. Two weeks from first keystroke, the tool was production-ready. The alignment fix was the big one: comparison verdicts went from roughly 85% right to over 98% on a controlled set of real factory photos I built myself with known pass/fail outcomes. In plain terms: out of ~20 photos whose right-or-wrong answer was already settled, the tool's pass/fail calls matched the engineers' judgment about 17 times before the fix and essentially every time after - a small, self-built check, so it's an estimate, not an audited rate. Then I broke the monolith into modules so the next feature wouldn't need a rewrite.

The learning: a defensible compromise isn't speed versus quality. A bounded cut - named limitation, repayment schedule, proof of repayment - is a different act from an unmeasured one nobody ever pays back.

- Customer: the manufacturing engineers; the engineering lead as first reviewer.
- Insight: when scoping stalls, a bounded prototype with named limits teaches more than more requirements - as long as "bad" means inaccurate, not broken.
- Action (mine): built the five-day monolithic prototype (inline JS, no build/modules/tests); documented the limits and stated them up front at the day-five demo; prioritized fixes by impact - alignment, then illumination normalization, then export; later modularized the monolith.
- Result: team surfaced three concrete issues within an hour; production-ready in two weeks (5 days prototype + 9 days refinement); alignment lifted comparison accuracy ~85% -> >98% on my ~20-photo controlled set - the debt repaid with proof, not just a schedule.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. Why was a five-day prototype better than more scoping? - The scoping *was* the stall: six months parked as "too complex." Working code turns "should we build this" into "here's what's wrong with it," and their reaction became the backlog. The five days generated more useful direction than the six months had. [SAFE]
2. Isn't shipping an inaccurate tool a quality cut you should be embarrassed by? - I'm not embarrassed, but I'm precise. It was correct under the conditions it claimed to support; the cut was that it didn't yet support real lighting and angles. A documented limitation is a different act from a hidden defect. [SAFE]
3. How do you decide an ugly build is acceptable to ship? - Three criteria: it works correctly under expected conditions; the limitations are documented; the feedback from shipping is worth more than the cost of the limitations. All three held here; if any had been false, it stayed internal. [SAFE]
4. What exactly did the team find, and how did you prioritize? - Three issues within the first hour of the demo: pixel comparison failed under illumination changes (brighter or dimmer lighting makes matching areas of the two pictures look different, so the dot-for-dot comparison breaks), no image alignment (boards had to be photographed at the exact same angle), no export (results couldn't be saved to a file). Alignment went first as the biggest capability gap, illumination normalization second, export third. [SAFE]
5. The 85% -> 98% number - how was it measured, and how reliable? - My own harness - a small counting program I wrote myself for this measurement - not production instrumentation (usage tracking wired into the live tool). I took ~20 real factory photos with known pass/fail outcomes - the right answer for each photo was already settled - and checked the tool's verdict against the engineers' calls. Pre-alignment about 17 of 20 matched; after alignment all 20 - so ">98%" is the honest label for "essentially all right on that set," and I call it an estimate, not an audited rate. The same set measured far-edge misalignment averaging 2.1 px (sd 0.8), down from 15-20 px before alignment. px means pixels - picture dots: this counts how far apart the two images still sat at the board's outer edge after lining them up, in dots; sd is standard deviation, the typical spread of the individual photos around that 2.1 average. Small n - n is how many photos the figure rests on, 20 here; it's a directional check (it shows the named limitation moved, not a precise rate) on the limitation I'd named, which is exactly why I named it. [PARTIAL - self-built test, state n and method before anyone asks]
6. What did the alignment fix actually do? - Homography plus a thin-plate-spline warp - two pieces of image-warping math: homography re-projects one photo as if seen from the other's angle, correcting tilt; a thin-plate spline stretches the image the way a thin flexible sheet gives under your fingers, absorbing small bends. Together they register (line up exactly) the two board images before comparing pixels, so boards shot at angles up to about 30 degrees or with ~3 mm of warp still line up. That's what closed the limitation I'd shipped with. [SAFE]
7. The two-week number - how reliable? - Documented timeline for this tool: five days to prototype plus nine days of refinement to production-ready. "Production-ready" is a qualitative milestone - the engineers used it - not a measured defect rate (a counted bug or error figure); I won't inflate it into one. [PARTIAL]
8. What if the prototype had been so rough the team dismissed it? - Straight answer: there was no export fallback, because export was one of the three gaps, so it couldn't hand them a saved file. The only prior route was the manual comparison they already did, by eye, and the prototype at least put that on screen. The risk I managed wasn't adoption - it was building the wrong features, which shipping early removed. [SAFE]
9. Have you ever compromised quality without a good reason? - Yes. I tuned detection thresholds - the sensitivity dial deciding how big a difference has to look before the tool flags it, set on a 0-to-1 scale - by watching the UI until false positives (the tool crying wolf on good boards) stopped annoying me: a resistor class from 0.40 to 0.55, an IC class from 0.25 to 0.40. In plain terms, resistors are simple, common electrical components and ICs (integrated circuits) are the black chips - I was moving the dial separately for each part type. Every move loosened - trading recall for precision - which is backwards for PCB QA: recall is how well the tool catches a real defect, precision is how often its alarms are genuine, and I bought fewer false alarms by accepting more missed defects. For board inspection that's the wrong trade - a missed defect ships, a false alarm costs an operator about thirty seconds. No held-out set - a batch of known-answer photos deliberately kept back from tuning so accuracy can be scored fairly - so I didn't know the cost. The fix I'd run: label ~200 components (mark each part's right-or-wrong answer by hand), plot precision-recall (a chart of that same trade-off as the dial moves), pick the operating point minimizing the weighted false-negative/false-positive costs the process engineers give me - false negative = missing a real defect, and the weighting is the engineers' say in what each kind of mistake actually costs. That one isn't fully repaid yet, and I say so. [PARTIAL]
10. What was the actual downside of the speed trade, and at larger scale? - First users hit rough edges in their environment instead of mine. Subtler: debt that outlives a deadline. I shipped a second tool by copying the first because I needed it working that week - bought the deadline, cost about six months of divergence, including two different anomaly formulas (the math each tool uses to decide what counts as abnormal). Lesson isn't "don't copy" - set a deadline on the copy so the shortcut collapses instead of compounding. [SAFE]
11. Hardest part / any disagreement? - Hardest was naming the limits before watching the demo go badly. And I keep the distinction honest: the disagreement in this story was with a standing backlog conclusion - "too complex to scope" - not with a person; no person-vs-person dispute is documented here, and I won't inflate one. [PARTIAL]
12. What would you do differently? - State the known limitations even more sharply before the demo, so the team's hour went to next steps rather than rediscovering what I already knew. And for the threshold tuning: define the measurement before touching the knob. [SAFE]
13. How big was the monolith, exactly? - I only say "refactored into modules." The record carries conflicting size tellings - a "500 lines of JavaScript" figure (a count of how many lines of code the single file held) and a "4000+ lines / 21 modules" figure - and neither verifies against the actual code, so I quote neither. [GAP - generalize, don't number]
