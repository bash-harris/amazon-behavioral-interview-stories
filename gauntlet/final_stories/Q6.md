# Q6 - a time when you needed to influence a peer who had a differing opinion about a shared goal

**LP:** Ownership + Bias for Action (I moved the disagreement by shipping, not arguing).   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the board-vs-layout comparison tool stuck in the backlog as "too complex to scope," unblocked by a working prototype (behavioral/AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md STORY 8).

## Core Answer

The disagreement wasn't between me and one person - it was the whole team's settled view versus my bet, about something we all wanted. The shared goal was clear: the manufacturing engineers needed a tool that compared a photo of a populated board against its design and flagged the differences. The sticking point was that nobody could agree what the tool should actually do, so the request had sat in the backlog for six months as "too complex to scope properly." Every attempt to define it first - in meetings, in documents - stalled, because people were picturing the finished product and debating its edges.

My differing opinion was that we had the sequencing backwards. I believed we couldn't define it by talking, only by reacting to something real - so I asked to spend a few days building a rough, deliberately incomplete version instead of another spec. I won that argument only by making it cheap: a few days, throwaway, nothing locked in.

I shipped a prototype in about five days - upload a board photo and a design, align them, flag mismatches - rough enough that it clearly wasn't the answer, which was the point. In an hour of people using it, the abstract deadlock collapsed into concrete, ordered feedback: it flagged whole boards as wrong because the photo and the drawing weren't lined up, the flat lighting hid subtle defects, and there was no way to export a result the engineers could act on. Three disagreements about scope became three named problems with a clear cost order - alignment first (everything depends on it), then lighting, then export - and the thing that had been unspeakable for six months was now a two-week plan.

What shifted them wasn't my argument; it was that a working artifact gave them something to react to instead of something to debate. The lesson I keep: when smart people can't agree whether a thing is possible, stop trying to win the point and put a rough, real version in front of them. Opinions about a blank page are endless; opinions about a working screen converge in an hour.
