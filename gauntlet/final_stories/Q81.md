# Q81 - tell me about a time when your project deadline was very near, but you still took time to verify or test the data/code before submission

**LP:** Insist on the Highest Standards (primary); Deliver Results.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the image-alignment verification before shipping the PCB-vs-layout tool (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 2, canonical alignment telling; fixed-window deadline from AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md STORY 12). Distinct from Q46 (standalone parity harness) - here the verified thing is alignment accuracy against ground truth (checked against known-correct reference positions), not build parity (two versions of the tool producing identical outputs).

## Core Answer

The story I keep coming back to is the PCB-versus-layout tool - one milestone inside a fixed delivery window for my inspection suite, and the piece with the steepest learning curve, because it was browser-based computer vision: a program running inside an ordinary web browser that lets the computer "see", automatically checking photos of finished circuit boards (PCBs - the green boards inside electronic devices) against their design drawings. With a date like that, the pressure to skip verification isn't loud. Nobody asks you to ship something broken. It's quieter: the aligned overlay looks right - the board photo laid on top of the design drawing so the two line up - and "looks right" starts to sound like a finish line.

I didn't ship on the overlay because of the math downstream. The comparison engine - the part of the tool that automatically checks photo against drawing and circles differences - flags a mismatch at ten pixels of disagreement between photo and layout. Plain terms: a pixel is one dot of the image, so any spot where the photo and drawing sit more than about ten dots apart gets called a mismatch - and that ten is the tool's written spec, a design requirement rather than a measurement. Alignment error eats into that budget fast - misalign by a few pixels and every report after it is noise: every defect call inherits the mis-shift, so none of them can be trusted. So verification became a condition of submission, not something I'd run if time allowed.

First I measured. I put markers at known positions on board images - reference marks whose exact spots I already knew, so any leftover error could be computed rather than judged - ran the alignment, and measured the pixel offset left afterward (how far, in image-dots, the aligned photo still sat from where the marks truly were). The offsets landed inside the two-to-three-pixel band the threshold demands - a real fact instead of a hope, because homography alone had still been leaving the far edges fifteen to twenty pixels off - homography being the math that squares a photo taken at an angle to the flat drawing. In plain terms that's the before/after: 15-20 image-dots of error at the board's far edge before the fix, 2-3 after; counted on my own 20-image marker test - small and single-run, so it's a directional number, not factory telemetry. A global transform on a warped board - one correction applied evenly across the whole picture, on a board that's slightly bent - can look perfect on screen while quietly drifting at the edges.

Then I added a human lens. I had the manufacturing engineers overlay the aligned photo on the layout and confirm the pads lined up - pads are the small metal spots on a board where components are soldered - by eye. I kept that check because a single metric can pass while the product is wrong - an average hides a local warp too small to move the number but big enough to flip a flag, and the spot-checks caught exactly that kind of case.

The verification changed what I could honestly claim. The tool went from "built" to "trustworthy": alignment sat inside the tolerance the comparison needed, and the false alarms stopped - that's the tool crying "defect" on boards that were actually fine. And I named the limit instead of hiding it - severely warped boards still defeated the method. What I'd change is process: start simpler and add complexity only when it buys accuracy. What I wouldn't change is verifying before shipping, deadline or no deadline.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: PCB-vs-layout tool was the weeks-3-4 milestone of a fixed eight-week window - eight weeks end-to-end for the whole suite, a documented plan date rather than my estimate, with this tool scheduled for weeks 3 and 4; the quiet pull was to ship once the aligned overlay looked right.
- Customer: Manufacturing engineers whose trust in a brand-new tool would have died on false alarms (defect flags on good boards) from a noisy ten-pixel comparison.
- Insight: "Looks aligned" is not evidence at that tolerance; verification is a condition of submission, not an optional task to run if time allows.
- Action (mine): Built solo the ground-truth measurement (checking against known-correct reference marks) - 20 board images with markers at known positions, pixel offset measured after alignment (avg 2.1 px, sd 0.8: on average the aligned photo still landed about 2 dots off, and 0.8 is the typical spread around that average - results clustered within roughly one dot of it; my own single-run test, so directional); kept the engineers' by-hand overlay pad-check (a visual confirm that the metal solder dots line up) as the second, qualitative lens; defined "verified" before shipping.
- Result: 15-20 px -> 2-3 px (dots of misalignment at the board's far edge, before vs. after, counted on the 20-image test); comparison accuracy ~85% -> >98% on moderate warping (the share of the tool's match/flag calls that come out right on slightly bent boards - the tool's documented before/after, not something I instrumented); false alarms stopped; one envelope - ~3 mm / ~30 deg handled (the tested working range: boards bent up to about 3 millimeters, photographed up to about 30 deg away from straight-down), severe warp beyond that failed, stated honestly.
- Learning: One metric can pass while the product is wrong - use two independent lenses; start simpler, add complexity only when it buys accuracy; deadline near = verify more, not less.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. How near was the deadline - did verifying delay you? - The whole suite had a fixed eight-week window (eight weeks from kickoff to delivery - a documented project plan, not a guess) and this tool was the weeks-3-4 milestone (the slice of that plan this tool had to fit), so every stage was on the clock. The validation was a bounded, defined step - one pass over a fixed image set - not open-ended polish, and it was cheaper than shipping an alignment that silently corrupted the comparison and had to be re-diagnosed later. [SAFE]
2. What exactly did the verification consist of? - Two checks. Quantitative: 20 board images with ground-truth markers at known positions (ground truth = reference marks whose exact positions were known ahead of time, so the error could be computed rather than judged; 20 images is the size of my personal test set); align each, then measure the pixel offset afterward - average 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8 (on average the aligned photo was still about 2 image-dots off; the 0.8 standard deviation is the typical scatter around that average - most images within about one dot of it; one pass, small sample - a directional figure, not a production measurement). Qualitative: the engineers overlaid the aligned photo on the layout and visually confirmed pads lined up - that caught cases where the good average hid a subtly wrong local warp. [SAFE]
3. Where did the 2-3 pixel requirement come from? - Forced by the spec, not a wish: the comparison flags mismatches at a ten-pixel threshold (the tool's written rule: photo and drawing more than about ten image-dots apart anywhere counts as a mismatch - a design requirement, not a measured finding), so alignment has to land within a few pixels before any flag it produces means anything. [SAFE]
4. What did verifying actually change in the outcome? - It moved the tool from "built" to "trustworthy": far-edge misalignment 15-20 px -> 2-3 px (how many image-dots off the photo was at the board's far edge before the fix versus after - the after figure from my 20-image test), and comparison accuracy roughly 85% -> over 98% for moderately warped boards (the share of the tool's match/flag decisions that came out right on slightly bent boards - the documented before/after). Without the measurement I wouldn't have known whether the ten-pixel threshold was reliable. [PARTIAL - offsets measured by me; accuracy is the tool's documented outcome]
5. How reliable are those numbers? - The 20-image set is my own small, self-built harness (a test setup I built for myself, not a company QA system): single run, small n (n = the number of test images - just 20), directional - not production telemetry (no data collected from real use on the factory floor). That's exactly why the engineers' overlay spot-checks stayed in as the second lens. [PARTIAL]
6. What was the working envelope? - The "working envelope" means the range of conditions under which the tool still performs reliably. The pipeline handled photos up to about 30 degrees off-perpendicular (aimed up to about 30 deg away from straight-down) and up to roughly 3 mm of board warp (a bend of about 3 millimeters - roughly the thickness of a couple of stacked coins), which is what made the ten-pixel comparison reliable instead of noisy. Both are the method's stated, documented limits from development testing, not production measurements. [SAFE]
7. What still fails? - Severe warping beyond the handled envelope - roughly 3 mm: the control points (the marker spots the correction math is anchored to) can't capture that much deformation; it would need a more physically based model (software that simulates how the board actually bends and stretches - a much heavier build). Rare in production, and I told the engineers the limit rather than implying it was solved for every board. [SAFE]
8. Isn't this the same verification story as your standalone-build harness? - Different subject. That one verified that a compiled offline artifact (the single downloadable file version of the tool, run without a server) matched the server version - output parity on 15 BOMs and 10 images (BOM = bill of materials, a board's parts list; parity = both versions giving identical outputs on the same 15 parts lists and 10 photos). This one verifies the alignment pipeline's accuracy (how well the photo-straightening steps line the photo up) against ground truth, measured in pixels, before the comparison tool shipped. Same discipline, different risk. [SAFE]
9. Who pushed you to skip the tests? - No person-vs-person pressure - I built the suite solo, so the squeeze was against my own schedule. The trade-off was real either way: visible progress on the next milestone versus invisible insurance (quiet testing that prevents a loud blow-up later). [PARTIAL - self-imposed, none documented]
10. Alternatives rejected? - Shipping on the overlay (a couple of pixels of local error is invisible to the eye but flips the comparison); a single metric with no human check (averages hide local error); and full feature matching (software that finds and pairs up hundreds of individual landmarks between photo and drawing), before I found simple board-outline detection (matching just the outer edge of the board) covered most boards. [SAFE]
11. What would you do differently? - Start simpler and add complexity only when it buys accuracy, and lean on the qualitative overlay check earlier. The ground-truth measurement itself I'd keep - it's what made the claim a fact instead of a hope. [SAFE]
12. Did it hold up in use? - The suite served about 15 engineers across 2 sites (a headcount of users at two factories, from my recollection of adoption); they told me it saved roughly 20 hours a week (their own estimate of hours saved, for the whole suite) - engineer-reported, not instrumented (nothing in the software measured it). [PARTIAL]
