# Q5 - tell me about a time you directly interacted with a stakeholder for your project or work

**LP:** Customer Obsession (primary); touches Earn Trust and Ownership.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the manufacturing-engineer stakeholder on the BOM reconciliation tool (variant semantics from STORY 1, transparency work from STORY 11; told only in the form the code supports).

## Core Answer

My stakeholders were the manufacturing engineers who used the BOM reconciliation tool I built, part of my PCB inspection suite - a PCB, or printed circuit board, is the green board inside electronic devices. A BOM - the bill of materials - is the parts list for a board: every resistor, chip and connector it should carry. "Reconciliation" just means checking that list, line by line, against the board's actual design drawing. Before it existed they verified boards by hand - cross-referencing the SAP-exported BOM (the parts list exported from SAP, the company's production-management software) against the Altium layout PDF (the board's drawing made in Altium, the design software, saved as a PDF), entry by entry. The baseline they gave me was 20-30 minutes per board - that counts how long one engineer believed it took to hand-check one board's list against its drawing. That was their estimate, not a measurement I took.

The first important interaction was a correction, not a request. My parser - the part of the tool that reads the files and works out what matches what - treated references like `R1||R2` and `R1/R2` as invalid and silently dropped them. A "reference" is a component's label on the drawing (R1 = resistor number one), so `R1||R2` marks one spot that lists two possible parts. A manufacturing engineer explained that those notations mean the same physical position can accept either part, depending on the board variant - the factory builds slightly different versions of the same board that take different parts in the same spot - so the system should match either reference. He had the manufacturing intent and I didn't. I built a variant splitter that normalizes both separators and matches either one - in plain terms, a small rule that reads both spellings of the notation, the double bar and the slash, and accepts either part as correct. That change came from the customer; I would not have found it from the data alone.

The second interaction was about trust. The engineers were using the tool but didn't fully trust it: when it flagged a mismatch they couldn't see why, and they were hesitant to rely on it for critical decisions. Fixing that was my responsibility, and I added a debug view - an extra screen that shows the tool's working, not just its verdict - to the server build, the version of the tool that runs on a shared factory computer rather than as a single file on one machine. For a given component reference it returns the raw evidence behind the decision - which pages of the layout PDF that reference appears on, the matched text spans with their position and color classification (the exact words the tool matched, where they sit on the page, and that text's colour), and the nearby hyperlinks and DNP keywords the matcher weighed (DNP means "do not populate": a part listed on the drawing but deliberately left unsoldered on some versions of the board). I also put a stats dashboard above every reconciliation - schematic component count, BOM entries, matched, issues flagged - in plain words: how many parts the drawing shows, how many the list names, how many lined up, how many raised a flag - so they could see the shape of a run before drilling into any decision. I kept the diagnostics on a separate route, its own web address, so the main view stayed clean. The intent wasn't to prove the tool right; it was to let them check the decisions themselves.

The result: that audit capability turned into an informal test channel - in the first week the engineers used it to surface two edge cases with unusual component naming - two odd label spellings the tool hadn't handled - and I fixed both. A full report - one board checked end to end - came back in seconds of computer time against their reported 20-30 minute per-board human baseline, and it caught three real mismatches in week one that manual review had missed: three genuine disagreements between the parts list and the drawing that a hand check had passed over. The honest scope: one team, one plant, one week of numbers, and every figure here is engineer-reported or from my written record - none of it measured with timers or counters. What changed most was the trust dynamic - once they could see the reasoning, they reported being more willing to rely on the results for production verification.

What I'd do differently: ship the transparency from the beginning instead of after their skepticism, and collect representative BOMs from the floor before writing the parser. Same lesson - get the customer's real artifacts in front of me earlier.

## Memory Anchors

- Problem: manual BOM-vs-layout verification - hand-checking the parts list against the board drawing - was slow and the automated replacement wasn't trusted.
- Customer: manufacturing engineers at one plant - one team, one week of real use.
- Insight: the customers held domain semantics - the factory's meaning behind the notations - I couldn't derive from data, and they'd only trust a verdict they could audit.
- Action (mine): variant splitter (the either-part-counts rule) from the engineer's correction; debug view (extra screen showing the raw evidence behind one part's decision) + stats dashboard, kept on a separate route in the Flask server build (Flask = a lightweight way to run a web app from Python).
- Result: debug view became a feedback channel - two naming edge cases found and fixed in week one; report in seconds of computer time vs. their reported 20-30 min of human time; three real mismatches caught; they reported more willingness to rely on it.
- Learning: ship auditability from day one; get real customer artifacts earlier.
