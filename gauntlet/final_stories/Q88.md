# Q88 - a time where you got negative feedback and it was your fault

**LP:** Earn Trust (primary - owned a real fault without excuse, repaired trust with transparency); Learn and Be Curious   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the pin-count false positives in BOM reconciliation (AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md STORY 10). Same event as Q4, different spine: Q4 = the failure and its root-cause analysis; this one = the users' feedback, the admission, and the trust repair.

## Core Answer

The negative feedback I earned came from the manufacturing engineers who used my first BOM tool - BOM meaning bill of materials, the list of parts that go on a board - and it was my fault: I'd shipped a matching rule (the check that decides whether a parts-list entry and a spot on the board layout are the same part) built on an assumption I never tested, and it flagged correct boards as wrong.

The tool cross-checks a board's parts list against its layout. My rule forced the BOM's pin count - the number of metal legs a part has - to match the layout's pad count - the number of solder spots drawn for it. I'd assumed pin count was a reliable signal that two things were the same part. It isn't - footprints (the solder patterns drawn on the layout for each part) get drawn for several package variants - the same part in different physical housings - and the extra pads are no-connects: placeholders drawn for the bigger variant but wired to nothing, so a legitimate part often lists fewer pins than its footprint has pads. My tool called that a mismatch. On boards mixing component types - boards with many different kinds of parts - it kept flagging good parts. That's a false positive: the tool crying wolf on something that's actually fine.

The engineers told me exactly that: the report was flagging parts that were fine. They were re-checking every flagged pin count by hand - the exact work the tool was supposed to remove - and that's how my false alarms surfaced. Worst feedback possible on a QA - quality-check - tool: every false alarm spends their time and teaches them to distrust the report. And they were right, with no defense from me: not bad data, not an upstream bug. I'd written a rule on a signal I never validated, tested only on the boards I happened to own, and none of those had the case that broke it.

So I told them the rule was mine and wrong, then fixed the semantics - what the rule is actually supposed to mean. More pads than the part needs is a possible match - I flag it for review. Fewer means the part physically can't fit - that stays a real mismatch. I could have dropped pin count entirely; it catches genuine errors, so the call was to keep it and constrain it. The repair I'm proudest of is a debug view: for each part it shows the parts-list count, the layout count, and the decision my code made, so an engineer can check my logic instead of trusting it - and override it when I'm wrong.

False positives on those mixed-type boards - the share of the tool's flags that were actually good parts - dropped from about 12% to under 2%, and the hand re-checking stopped. In plain terms: out of every 100 alarms the tool raised, about 12 were cries of wolf before my fix and fewer than 2 after. Where those numbers came from: a small test set I assembled and sorted by hand myself, re-run after the fix - solid within that sample, not a monitored production number. What I kept: a signal is a hypothesis until it fails on varied samples; now I collect the rule-breaking files from the users before I write the rule. And the deeper one - pin count was never the right signal; the reference designator (a part's own label on the board, like R7 for a resistor) plus the footprint name is. Apologies don't rebuild trust. Showing your work does.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: my first BOM rule compared pin count (the part's legs) to pad count (the board's solder spots) literally; footprints carry package variants and no-connects (pads drawn but wired to nothing), so the tool produced false alarms - flagged correct parts - on mixed-type boards
- Customer: the manufacturing engineers whose report it was - every false alarm spent their time and taught them to distrust the tool
- Insight: the feedback was un-defendable because the fault was an assumption I never validated on samples I never collected; trust repair = admit plainly + fix the semantics (what the rule means) + expose the decision
- Action (mine): told the users the rule was mine and wrong; rewrote it (more pads -> review-flagged possible match, fewer -> real mismatch); kept pin count but constrained it; shipped a per-component debug view - counts and decision laid bare - with a manual override
- Result: ~12% -> under 2% false positives on mixed-type boards (share of all flags that were actually fine; self-categorized 20-BOM sample + 15-BOM regression re-run - a stored set of known test files re-checked after the fix - labeled, not a validated production rate); hand re-checking stopped
- Learning: treat any matching signal as a hypothesis until it fails across varied samples; collect the breaking files from users first; reference designator (the part's board label, e.g. R7) + footprint name is the right signal; show your work to rebuild trust

## Follow-Up Bank
1. Was the feedback really your fault, or bad data? - Mine. The data was fine - real footprints legitimately have more pads (solder spots drawn) than the part fills with pins (legs). I built a rule on an unvalidated assumption and tested on the files I happened to have. That's a decision I made, so it's on me, and I said so to the people whose time it cost. [SAFE]
2. Who told you - a meeting, an email? - No recorded conversation exists and I won't invent one. What's on record: after the fix, engineers no longer needed to hand-verify every pin count - which says they were doing it before, hitting my false alarms (good parts flagged as bad) in their own work, and telling me the report flagged good parts. The feedback anchors to that mechanism, not a staged sit-down. [PARTIAL - anchored to STORY 10's result line]
3. Why not drop pin count entirely? - It catches a genuine error class: a 16-pin part against an 8-pad footprint - a part with 16 legs against a solder pattern with only 8 spots - physically can't fit. Dropping it would let those through. The fix was being smarter about when the signal counts, not silencing it. [SAFE]
4. What exactly was the new rule? - More pads than the BOM count -> potential match, flagged for review; fewer -> definite mismatch; plus a component-type check (does the part kind fit the pattern?) inside a configurable ratio threshold - a limit the users can change - default 2:1, meaning the layout may show up to twice as many pads as the part has pins and still count as a possible match. The 2:1 came from categorizing twenty failing BOMs - the twenty boards whose flags were wrong - legitimate variants ran 2:1 or less. Thin base, so it's a default, not a verdict; at 3:1 the engineer decides. [SAFE]
5. How was 12% -> under 2% measured, and how much should I trust it? - Plain first: the two numbers are the share of the tool's flagged mismatches that were actually fine, before vs after the fix. Honestly: my own categorization of the twenty failing BOMs plus a fifteen-BOM regression re-run after the fix - a regression re-run means re-testing a stored set of known sample files to confirm the fix works and broke nothing else - the five pin-count files came out right, ten unchanged, nothing that matched before flipped. No held-out labeled set (a batch of examples deliberately kept aside, with human-verified answers, used to score the final tool fairly), so it's a sample-scoped rate, not a validated production number. Same label I give this event anywhere. [PARTIAL - self-categorized sample only]
6. What can the heuristic still miss? - Heuristic = a practical rule of thumb, not a guarantee. The remaining risk is false negatives - real errors the tool waves through silently: a wrong component with a larger footprint and the same type letter (two parts whose board labels start with the same letter, like two resistors) can satisfy ratio and type and pass. Unlikely, possible. The debug view mitigates - the engineer sees counts, type match, ratio, and decision, and overrides. A heuristic, not a proof. [SAFE]
7. A failure inside the fix? - My first version of the new rule had its own hole: a wrong component with a larger footprint waved through. I'd repeated the original habit - trusting an untested signal. That's what forced the component-type check. [SAFE]
8. How did you keep from getting defensive? - Nothing to defend. They were naming the exact failure the tool existed to prevent, it reproduced (I could trigger it on demand), and the assumption behind my rule was one I knew I'd never tested. First move was asking for the failing files, not arguing. [SAFE]
9. Why a debug view, not just the fix? - Trust. They'd learned to distrust the report; a lower false-positive rate doesn't prove itself case by case - on any single flag, the engineer has no way to see *why* it fired. Exposing the inputs and the decision, with an override, let them verify me on their terms. Observability (the tool showing what it saw and how it decided) from day one became standard after this. [SAFE]
10. Why not train a model for the classification? - Considered it. Around two hundred known mismatch examples at component level - two hundred individual part positions with hand-verified right/wrong answers - too few for a reliable classifier (a trained statistical model needs far more examples to learn from), and the rules are clear with enumerable edge cases. Revisit if the cases get messier. [SAFE]
11. Did anyone push back on the fix itself? - Nothing on record; I won't manufacture a second conflict. What is on record: the threshold went configurable so the users held the judgment calls themselves. [GAP - stated plainly]
12. If designator + footprint name is the better signal, why keep pin count? - They answer different questions - the key (the part's board label plus its footprint name) decides which part a position should hold; the pin-vs-pad relation still gates physically-impossible fits (legs that can't reach the solder spots). With representative data up front I'd have gone straight to the better signal instead of patching the weaker one. [SAFE]
13. What would you do differently? - Collect diverse files from the floor before writing a rule - the boards that broke this weren't in my test set. And build the debug view from day one - it was an afterthought, and during development it would have shown me the pin-count behavior before the engineers had to. [SAFE]
14. At larger scale? - Volume shifts the risk to false negatives (real errors slipping by) slipping past the ratio-plus-type rule. That's why the threshold is configurable, borderline ratios defer to review, and the measurement plan is real: label ~200 components as ground truth (hand-verified correct answers), plot precision-recall - precision = when the tool flags, how often it's right; recall = of the real errors, how many it catches - pick the operating point (the balance between false alarms and misses) from the false-alarm-vs-miss cost the process engineers give me. [PARTIAL - plan, not executed]
