# Q19 - tell me about a time when you had to make a difficult decision to meet a tight deadline

**LP:** Bias for Action (primary); Deliver Results; Are Right, A Lot.   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - the five-day first prototype (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 8); the deadline-decision angle, distinct from Q13's quality-tradeoff and Q10's influence tellings of the same prototype.

## Core Answer

Start with the honest part: nobody handed me a date. The real problem was a stalemate. The manufacturing engineers wanted a PCB comparison tool - a PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device, and this tool checks a photo of a built board against its design drawing - and it had sat in the backlog, the team's parking list of work nobody has scheduled, for six months because it was "too complex to scope properly." (The six months is what I was told when I joined the thread - how long the idea had been parked, not something I tracked.) I decided the bottleneck was definition, not capability, so I set my own deadline: demo in five days. (Five days = calendar days from deciding to build to standing in front of the team.) Waiting for agreement would have meant waiting another six months.

The hard call was what to cut. The version I'd have been comfortable showing had image alignment (lining the photo and the design drawing up so identical parts sit on top of each other), illumination normalization (correcting brightness differences so the lighting doesn't read as a defect), and export (saving the result as a file an engineer can send elsewhere) - that version ended up taking two weeks. On day one I had three options: keep collecting requirements, build the trustworthy version and show up late, or cut to the smallest end-to-end slice (the tiniest version that walks the real job from start to finish) that answered the only question the demo had to answer - can this thing see a mismatch? - and show it on the date. I took the slice: upload, a pixel-level comparison (checking the two images point by point and marking where they look different), a visual result. I cut the three things that made the result trustworthy. That meant boards had to be photographed at the same angle in the same light, which almost never happens on a real line (a factory's production line, where lighting and camera angles wander). I knew that. I shipped it anyway.

What was at stake was credibility - mine and the tool's. A demo the team waved off would have sent the idea back to the backlog, this time on my work rather than on the fog around the problem. I judged that risk smaller than the risk of spending months building the wrong features. I drew one line: cut features, not correctness. The prototype was right about the conditions it claimed to support. I wrote the limitation down and said it out loud in the demo.

The lead shared it with the team immediately, and within an hour (the first hour after the live demo) they'd given me three problems: comparison failed under lighting changes, no alignment, no export. That was exactly what I'd bought five days for. I fixed them in impact order (biggest blocker first) - alignment first because nothing worked without it, illumination second, export last. Two weeks from the first line of code, the tool was production-ready. (The two weeks = 5 days to the demo plus 9 days of fixes, counted from my own work record, not a ticketing system; "production-ready" here means the engineers used the tool for real work - a milestone, not a measured quality score.) I'd hit the date I picked.

What I'd do differently: bring a couple of real board photos to the demo, so the team's hour went to next steps instead of rediscovering what I already knew. What I'd still defend: cutting scope to a named limit is how I met the deadline - not lowering the standard I could verify.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: PCB comparison tool the manufacturing engineers wanted; six months parked in the backlog as "too complex to scope properly."
- Customer: the manufacturing engineers; the engineering lead as the first demo audience.
- Insight: the bottleneck was definition, not capability - a working slice forces the opinions that six months of scoping could not.
- Action (mine): set my own five-day demo date; kept upload + pixel comparison + visual result; cut alignment, illumination normalization, export - with the limitation written down and stated out loud at the demo; ordered fixes by impact (alignment -> illumination -> export).
- Result: demoed on day five; lead shared it immediately; three concrete issues inside the first hour; production-ready two weeks from first keystroke (5 + 9); the date I set was met.
- Learning: meet a self-imposed deadline by cutting scope to a named limit, never by cutting the standard I can verify; bring real photos to the demo so feedback lands on next steps.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. Was five days a real deadline? - Self-imposed, and I say so first. No external date existed; the real pressure was a six-month stalemate. I compressed on purpose to force a decision instead of another week of analysis. The "difficult decision" is the compression itself. [SAFE]
2. How did you pick what to keep versus cut? - The demo had to answer one question: can it see a mismatch at all. Upload, comparison, and a visible result answer that. Alignment, illumination handling, and export make the result *trustworthy* - they don't change whether the concept works. The trustworthy half was the deferrable half. [SAFE]
3. Isn't shipping a knowingly inaccurate tool a bad decision? - The cut was "works under real line conditions," not "works correctly." It was correct under the conditions it claimed to support, and the limitation was documented before the demo. A named limitation is a different act from a hidden defect. [SAFE]
4. Who did you consult before cutting scope? - Nobody, and that was the point. The only opinions available lived in the meetings that had produced six months of nothing. My consultation *was* the demo, scheduled early enough that the answer was still cheap. I bet on scope, not on permission - and I made the bet reviewable. [PARTIAL - honest limit on "consults when appropriate"]
5. What if the team had dismissed it? - There was no fallback artifact: export was one of the three cuts, so nothing could be saved out of it. The prior route was the engineers comparing board images by eye, and the prototype at least put both boards on one screen. The risk I was managing wasn't adoption - it was building the wrong features, and shipping early is what removed that. [SAFE]
6. How do you know the decision, not luck, produced the outcome? - The outcome was specific and directional: three concrete issues within the hour, that list became the priority order, production-ready followed in two weeks. I'm not claiming the tool was good on day five - only that cutting scope to a date is what produced the roadmap. [SAFE]
7. How reliable are the numbers? - All durations are my own work record, not instrumented: five days to prototype, nine more to production-ready. "Production-ready" is a milestone - the engineers used the tool - not a measured defect rate, and I don't upgrade it into one. [PARTIAL]
8. Did the speed create debt? - Yes, in two places. I broke the monolith (one big tangle of code in a single place) into modules (separate, self-contained pieces you can change without rewriting everything) after the first two weeks so the next feature wasn't a rewrite. And I later shipped a second tool by copying the first because I needed it working that week - that bought a deadline, then the copies drifted for months. Lesson: put a deadline on the copy too. [SAFE]
9. How big was the monolith, exactly? - I only say "modularized it." The record carries conflicting size tellings ("500 lines of JavaScript" and "4000+ lines / 21 modules" - those count lines of code in the program file and separate code files, i.e. the program's raw size, not anything about quality), and neither verifies against the actual code, so I quote neither. [GAP - generalize, don't number]
10. How did you order the fixes? - By impact and complaint frequency: illumination was the most frequent complaint, but alignment was the biggest capability gap - nothing worked without it - so alignment went first, illumination second, export third. My prioritization as the builder, not a negotiated call with the team. [SAFE]
11. Who disagreed with you on this? - Nobody, and I won't inflate it. The opposition in this story was a standing backlog conclusion - "too complex to scope" - not a person. [PARTIAL - stated plainly]
12. Bigger scale / where did this lead? - I built the suite solo, so the five-day call was mine alone. The prototype's pixel compare later became the aligned comparison engine - the part that decides whether a board matches its design and flags the differences. The accuracy figures for that fix live in my quality-compromise story, and I keep that single-told rather than restating it here. [PARTIAL - cross-ref Q13]
13. What would you do differently? - Collect a couple of real board photos before the demo, so the team's hour went to next steps instead of rediscovering the lighting and angle problems I already knew about. [SAFE]

If an interviewer demands an imposed date, concede and keep the story standing - never inflate an external deadline that didn't exist. [SAFE as reframed]

3. "Production-ready in two weeks is unmeasured" - defensible as a documented milestone with the 5 + 9 split; never present it as a quality rate. [PARTIAL - framing only]
