# Q10 - describe a time when you needed to influence a peer who had a differing opinion about a shared goal

**LP:** Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit - *the "disagrees with peers and supervisors using data" strength is a GAP; no lived event exists in the record* ("Have Backbone" is Amazon's label for standing your ground on a decision; "Disagree and Commit" for arguing your side, then fully supporting the final call even if it goes against you.)   **Story:** No peer-disagreement event documented. Closest truths (cross-ref Q6): committing to the engineer's variant decision + customer-obsessed advocacy; plus prototype influence (STORY 8) and pin-count backbone vs an assumption (STORY 10)

## Core Answer

I'll start with the honest part: I don't have a documented case of a peer holding a differing opinion about a shared goal and me moving them. I built the inspection suite solo - there was no teammate across the table in the record - and I won't invent one to fit the question. (The suite checks PCBs - the green circuit boards inside every electronic device - by comparing a photo of a finished board against its design files.)

So let me give you the closest true things, and where each one stops short.

The closest to commitment is the variant call in my Q6 story. Boards often allow interchangeable parts at one spot. A manufacturing engineer told me `R1||R2` references were valid - that notation in the parts list means "this one position accepts either part R1 or part R2" - one position, either part. My parser - the piece of software that reads those files - had decided they were invalid and dropped them. He was right, I was wrong, and I adopted his reading of the notation completely and rewrote the splitter, the code that breaks a combined part reference into its pieces. That's the commit half of disagree-and-commit, exercised fully.

The closest to customer-obsessed advocacy is the same story: the engineers distrusted the tool's verdicts, and I pushed for an auditable `?debug=true` decision path - a "show your work" mode where adding that switch to the page makes the tool explain every call it made - rather than letting distrust stall production use. That was standing up for what was best for the customer - but nobody opposed it, so it wasn't influence against resistance.

The real backbone in my record was against an assumption, not a person: I disagreed with literal pin-count matching - treating two parts as interchangeable only if they have exactly the same number of electrical connector pins - categorized 20 BOMs (bills of materials: the full parts list for a board) into three footprint cases (footprint = the pattern of mounting points a part needs on the board), and changed the rule - false positives, meaning good boards wrongly flagged as mismatched, on mixed boards dropped from about 12% to under 2%. The real influence was the first prototype: a six-month "too complex to scope" backlog position - the team's to-do list had written it off as too complicated to even estimate the work - answered with five days of working code, which moved the engineering lead and the team - not a peer.

The method these show is the one I'd bring to a true peer dispute: name the shared goal, get the data before the argument, put something reviewable in front of them, adopt their idea if it's better, then commit and stop relitigating.

But I want to be exact about what this answer is: a bridge to what actually happened. The peer-disagreement event itself is a gap in my documented record, and I'm treating it as homework - to be told with a real example from team experience, not assembled from adjacent truths.
