## Core Answer

The question trails off at "vs," so I'll name what I balanced: my users' need for the AI features to just work, against a paid service's right to tell me to slow down. In plain words: an AI provider counts how often you ask in a window of time; pass the allowance and it answers "too many requests" - the technical name is a 429, a status code meaning exactly that - and stops responding.

Tab Scroller is my personal Chrome extension - one searchable strip of all your open tabs - and typed commands like "group my research tabs" have an AI model pick the tabs. Several of those features leaned on the same hosted model. The balance went sharp when that model's free allowance ran out mid-session: every AI feature froze on the same limit. Users didn't see a clever degradation story; they saw buttons that did nothing. [PARTIAL - the cascade is my account of behavior I watched; no availability was instrumented]

There were two easy, wrong answers. The technically convenient one: pass the error straight through - "service busy" - honest but useless to someone mid-task. The people-pleaser one: retry quietly forever; the user stares at a spinner while my retry storm digs the hole deeper.

I built the middle path, in one wrapper every AI call goes through. On a rate-limit failure - the 429 code plus the phrasings providers actually return - it does two things. The limited model goes into a cooldown: a per-model timer stops me asking a refusing service for more. The call retries automatically down a tiered registry of models, and the user's toast is honest: "X rate-limited. Using Y" - the feature moves, and they know it moved.

The judgment call I'd defend is the failure I refused to hide: when every model in the chain is limited, the extension does not fake patience. It reports the dead end with the number of models it tried and the actual instruction - wait a few minutes. A tool that lies about being alive trains users to distrust it when it's truthful.

What the balance bought: users keep working through one provider's limit; the service stops getting hammered while it's refusing me. What I deliberately didn't claim: any speedup or uptime number - the fix is behavior I watched, not a metric I measured.

## Follow-Up Bank
1. How do you detect a rate limit that reliably? - A status of exactly 429 throws a typed marker (AUTO_FALLBACK_CODE.js:284-286), and a broader matcher catches the phrasings providers actually use: "rate_limit", "quota exceeded", "too many requests", "resource_exhausted" - different providers, same meaning, in plain words: "you're over your allowance" (background.js:339-348). [SAFE]
2. How does the cooldown work? - A map of model -> "limited until" timestamp; in-cooldown models are skipped on the next call; rate-limit hits get a longer cooldown than ordinary failures (background.js:331-361, 1746-1750). Exact minute values are configuration, not a claim. [SAFE - labeled]
3. What does the user actually see? - Two messages, verbatim from the code: a warning toast when a command falls back - "X rate-limited. Using Y" - and an error toast when the whole chain is limited, stating the count of models tried and "please wait a few minutes" (AUTO_FALLBACK_CODE.js:396, 403). [SAFE]
4. Why a tiered registry instead of one backup model? - A single backup shares the same free-allowance economics and fails the same way; tiers spread calls across models with different limits so one well isn't the whole water supply. Registry structure is in the code (AUTO_FALLBACK_CODE.js:9 onward). [SAFE]
5. Wasn't hiding the fallback kinder? - No - and this is the judgment. A silently swapped model changes behavior quality; the user deserves the note. And on the total dead end, faking patience trains distrust. [SAFE - reasoning, held as mine]
6. Alternatives rejected? - Pass-through error (useless mid-task), silent infinite retry (spinner + deeper limit hole), and queueing everything to the top-tier model (burns the biggest budget on the smallest tasks). My option-matrix is reasoning, not a documented debate. [PARTIAL]
7. Numbers - any measured win? - None. This is a personal build; nothing instrumented availability before or after. The extension's published performance figures are design targets, and I keep them out of my mouth entirely. [GAP - no metrics; stated as behavior]
8. Who is "the customer"? - The user of the extension, which is primarily me plus anyone who tried it; no adoption count claimed. The balance still holds: the provider's limit is a real constraint, not an excuse. [SAFE - honest scope]
9. What would you do differently? - Budget-aware routing: local rules and the on-machine model handle simple commands so the remote limit is rarely the single point of failure; and log which fallback chain runs so cooldown tuning isn't guesswork. [PARTIAL - reasoning; routing existed in part elsewhere]
