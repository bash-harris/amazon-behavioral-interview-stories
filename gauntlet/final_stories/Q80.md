## Core Answer

The question trails off at "vs," so I'll say what I'm answering: the customer's short-term need against the engineering need to keep a safety check that protects them later. That's the balance I hit in the BOM reconciliation tool I built. (BOM = "bill of materials," the official parts list for a circuit board; "reconciliation" means checking the board's design against that list.)

The tool compared a part's pin count against the pads in its layout footprint, literally. In plain words: pins are a part's little electrical legs, pads are the solder points the design file draws where those legs land, and the footprint is the drawing of one part's position on the board - so the rule asked whether the part had exactly as many legs as the drawing had solder points. On real boards most of those flags were false alarms - warnings pointing at nothing actually wrong - because footprints are drawn with extra pads for package variants (the same part sold in slightly different physical versions) or no-connects (pads drawn on purpose but deliberately left unconnected). And they had a cost: the manufacturing engineers - my customers - had started re-verifying every pin-count flag by hand, which undid the whole point of automating it. (The customer here is internal: the engineers who run boards through the tool every day.) Their need was real - as it reached me, they needed a report they could trust.

The competing need was quieter. The tempting fix - instant satisfaction, almost free for me - was to remove pin count from the comparison. But pin count is what catches a physically wrong part: if the BOM calls for more pins than the footprint has pads, that part can't fit. Deleting the check trades a daily annoyance for a rare, expensive escape - a bad board leaving the line.

I didn't pick a side by instinct. I pulled 20 BOMs that had produced pin-count mismatches and sorted every case: footprint larger than the part, smaller, or exact. (The 20 = how many real parts lists that had triggered the warning I could pull and sort by hand; it counts flagged files, my own categorization - a small sample, not a measurement program.) The pattern was clean - larger is almost always a legitimate variant, smaller is almost always genuine. So I kept the check and made it discriminate: more pads than pins, flag for review; fewer, definite mismatch. Plus a component-type check and a ratio threshold, defaulted from the data at 2:1. (The type check compares what kind of part the list calls for against what the drawing shows; the ratio threshold is the widest pad-to-pin ratio the tool tolerates - 2:1 means up to twice as many solder points as the part has legs is treated as a legitimate variant, not an alarm.)

I also owned the new hole that opened: a mismatch that happens to satisfy the rules can slip through. I documented that risk and left the threshold and an override in the engineer's hands. (Override = a manual switch so the engineer can second-guess the rule on a given part.)

False alarms dropped from about 12% to under 2% on mixed-component boards, and the hand re-verifying stopped. (That number is the share of the tool's warnings that pointed at nothing wrong, counted from the tool's own output on the board files I had and checked by me - a direction of travel on those files, not an instrumented production figure; "mixed-component boards" means boards packed with many different part types.) Both sides got served - the report became trustworthy and the safety check stayed. What I learned: when what the customer wants today and what's best for them later disagree, the answer isn't to pick the popular side. It's to get the data and design something that serves both.

## Memory Anchors
- Problem: literal pin-count-vs-pad-count rule (legs vs solder points) flagged legitimate package variants; engineers hand-re-verifying every flag - automation's value erased
- Customer: internal manufacturing engineers; no verbatim request or named person in the record, none invented; their workaround was the signal
- Insight: the two needs aren't the same customer's wants at different time horizons - daily trust vs protection from a physically wrong part; direction of the discrepancy (larger vs smaller footprint) separates them
- Action (mine): pulled and bucketed 20 mismatched BOMs (20 real flagged parts lists, hand-sorted into three buckets); kept the check, made it directional (more pads -> review, fewer -> mismatch); added component-type check with configurable 2:1 default; documented residual false-negative risk; left threshold + override with the engineer
- Result: ~12% -> under 2% false alarms on mixed-component boards (files I had, regression-checked - not an instrumented production rate); manual re-verification stopped; safety check retained
- Learning: balance != pick a side; get data, design the rule that serves both horizons; admit the original rule (mine) was wrong

5. What happens when the heuristic is wrong the other way? - False negatives: a mismatch satisfying the type-and-ratio rules slips through. (A false negative = a real problem the tool stays quiet about.) I didn't pretend the tool was infallible - documented the risk, exposed the raw comparison and an override in a debug view (a built-in extra screen showing the underlying comparison plus the manual switch), kept the threshold configurable. Judgment stays with the person accountable for the board. [SAFE]

I also re-ran the full 15-BOM regression suite (a fixed test set of known boards - 15 files - that I re-run after any change to make sure nothing else broke): the 5 problem boards came out correct, the other 10 unchanged, no previously-correct match flipped.
