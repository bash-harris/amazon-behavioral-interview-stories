## Core Answer

I'll start with the honest part: I don't have a documented case of picking up work that plainly wasn't mine. The story I keep reaching for is the transparency layer on the BOM reconciliation tool, and I want to tell it accurately. (BOM = bill of materials, the master parts list for a circuit board; the reconciliation tool is a program that cross-checks that list against the board's design drawing and the real board, then prints a match-or-mismatch verdict for every part.)

The problem was real. I built the reconciliation engine - the program that does that three-way comparison and prints a verdict per component. The engineers used it but didn't believe it. When it flagged a mismatch they couldn't see why, so for anything that mattered they re-checked by hand. I treated that distrust as mine to fix, not theirs to live with. So I built a debug view behind `?debug=true` - a hidden page you open by adding a short piece of text to the tool's web address, which shows the tool's inner workings exactly as it saw them: raw BOM text (the parts list as it was written), the reference after each parsing step (what the program understood at each stage), the layout match (which design item it paired with each part), the rules that fired, and a step-by-step decision tree (the chain of checks that produced the verdict). Plus a reconciliation dashboard with side-wise counters (mismatch tallies for each of the things being compared), status chips (small colored labels showing pass/fail), and severity ranking - ordering problems from worst to least - so clicking a row jumps to the component. In the first week the engineers used it to find two edge cases where my heuristic (rule-of-thumb matching logic) was wrong - both unusual naming conventions, and quick to diagnose because the view exposed the intermediate data. Those two are real events written up in the project record, not a measured rate. After that they stopped double-checking and relied on it for production verification.

That's genuine ownership of the adoption risk - the chance that people simply wouldn't trust or use the tool. But here's why I won't dress it as the answer to your question: my own project notes list the debug endpoint as part of the assigned task. The reflection calls it an afterthought - late, not out-of-scope. So the strongest true claim is "I owned more than the code," not "I took on something outside my responsibilities." **[GAP]** - the real outside-my-job example has to come from my team experience, and I'm bringing one rather than stretching this one.
