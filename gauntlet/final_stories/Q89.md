# Q89 - tell me about a time where you interacted with a customer with negative feedback directly

**LP:** Customer Obsession (primary); Earn Trust; Invent and Simplify (touch)   **Story:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite - STORY 8 GPT-4o "AI eye": the engineers' direct in-product feedback - their incorrect-marks showing the AI analysis was confidently wrong - and the reframe from verdict to suggestion. This replaces the earlier STORY 2 re-frame draft in `answers\Q89.md`, which duplicated Q86; distinct from Q45/Q78/Q86 (alignment false alarms + BOM trust loops) and from Q1/Q40 (same feature, Invent & Simplify / Are Right A Lot spines - there the feedback beat is a closing lesson, here it IS the story).

## Core Answer

The negative feedback I remember best was about the AI feature in my inspection tool: the engineers marked it wrong, over and over, and they were right.

Context: my PCB comparison tool - a PCB is the green circuit board inside every electronic device - takes a photo of a finished board, compares it to its design drawing (the "layout"), and draws a red region where the two don't match, but it doesn't say what the mismatch means. Wrong part, rotated part, just a reflection - the manufacturing engineer had to look and decide, about thirty seconds a region, and that thirty seconds is the engineers' own estimate of how long one check takes, not a stopwatch study. So I added an AI analysis: crop the flagged region from the layout and the photo, send both to GPT-4o (an off-the-shelf AI model that can look at pictures and answer questions about them) with a structured prompt (a strict instruction that demands the answer back in one fixed, machine-readable form), get back component type, mismatch type, and a confidence (the AI's own score for how sure it says it is). A read became a five-second confirm - the "five seconds" is how long the online AI service takes to answer, computer time, not the engineer's time - and it was right about 85% of the time, meaning how often its answer matched what the engineer saw, from my rough tally of their feedback, not a measured rate.

The problem was the other 15% - the share of answers that were wrong in that same rough tally - because a wrong AI can be wrong with a straight face. A confident "false alarm" (the AI saying a red mark means nothing) on a QA tool (a quality-check tool) is the worst failure mode: it ships a bad board - a defective product gets waved through as fine. The pushback came through the channel I built for it: the mark-as-incorrect button (one click by an engineer saying "this answer is wrong," so every disagreement was recorded inside the tool, case by case). I expected occasional use; they used it until it was a signal, on the specific analyses they didn't trust. That was their direct feedback - no quote, nothing to invent.

I could have defended the demo. Instead I treated their incorrect-marks as data. Sorted, they weren't random: one systematic error showed up. I'd assumed the model could read the value printed on small surface-mount parts - the tiny components soldered straight onto the board. The print is too small and too blurry; it could do component type and orientation, not exact values. My assumption was the bug - and "bug" here means my wrong belief about what the AI could do, not broken code.

Then a decision: chase accuracy, or change the feature's job. I chose the second. I relabeled the output a suggestion with a confidence number, never a verdict - the engineer kept the call. I stopped asking it to commit to values. And the feature was optional by design: server build only (the version that ran on the company network; the separate offline build ran no AI at all); no API key (a password that lets software use a paid online AI service), no analysis, the red regions still work.

What they got: a triage aid - something that helps decide which red marks to open first - they trusted because it never pretended to be the answer - they said it changed which mismatches they opened first on dense boards. What I kept: an AI feature will be wrong some fixed share of the time no matter how good the model gets, so customer obsession is deciding who is trusted for what. Tool proposes, engineer confirms. And their incorrect-marks became the best bug reports I ever had - each one a precise, real example of the AI failing, filed by the person closest to the work.

## Memory Anchors

- Problem: the AI mismatch analysis could be confidently wrong; on a QA tool, a false "false alarm" (a "nothing here" that isn't nothing) ships bad boards - the engineers' incorrect-marks were the direct signal
- Customer: the manufacturing engineers using the comparison tool; their negative feedback was the incorrect-marks themselves, in-product, on the analyses they didn't trust (no verbal quotes exist - never invent any)
- Insight: the incorrect-marks weren't noise - they clustered on one systematic error: my assumption that a VLM (a vision-language model - an AI that looks at pictures and answers questions about them) can read value print on small SMD (surface-mount device - the tiny parts soldered straight onto the board) parts
- Action (mine): sorted their marks, found the systematic error, relabeled the output as a suggestion with confidence (never a verdict), dropped the value-reading ask, kept the feature an optional enhancement (server build only)
- Result: engineers accepted it as triage - reported it changed which mismatches they opened first on dense boards; ~30 s read -> ~5 s confirm; ~85% right by my rough check (30 s = their own estimate; 5 s = the AI service's answer time; 85% = my rough tally, not a measured test)
- Learning: with AI, design for the wrong share - work backwards from the engineer's trust, not the demo; the feedback path is part of the feature, and tool proposes, engineer confirms

## Follow-Up Bank

1. Was that really a direct interaction, or just button-clicks? - The button-clicks were the direct interaction: each incorrect-mark was an engineer rejecting a specific analysis, in the tool, at the moment they disagreed. Dense, per-case evidence - I don't reconstruct a conversation or quote anyone, because the feedback lives in the marks. [PARTIAL - channel honest; feedback is the mechanism + their reported prioritization help, no verbal scene exists]
2. What specifically was it getting wrong? - Component values on small surface-mount parts: the printed text is too small and too blurry to read reliably from a crop. Type and orientation it could do; exact values it couldn't. [SAFE - STORY 8 Reflection]
3. How did you know it was systematic, not random? - I sorted the incorrect-marks instead of fixing them one by one; they clustered on value-reading. Random misses don't do that. [SAFE - follow-up: marks "help me identify systematic errors and improve the prompt"]
4. Why not retrain or improve the accuracy instead of relabeling? - You can't OCR (machine text-reading - software transcribing words from a picture) text that isn't resolved in the image, meaning the letters simply aren't visible in the pixels; no prompt fixes missing pixels, and a bespoke classifier (a custom-trained AI detector) wouldn't read the print either. I changed the promise, not the number. [PARTIAL - defensible inference from the reflection]
5. Did you change the prompt at all? - I'd already iterated it once: the first "describe what you see" prompt was verbose and wandering; forcing strict JSON (a fixed, machine-readable answer format) with per-mismatch-type examples is what made it consistent. After the feedback I stopped asking it to commit to exact values. [SAFE - prompt iteration documented; the values-narrowing is my account of acting on the marks]
6. Why keep a feature that's 15% wrong? - The engineers reported it helped them prioritize which mismatches to open first, especially on dense boards. As triage with the human holding the call, 85% saves most of the 30-second read; as a verdict, no percentage is safe. [SAFE - Story 8 Result]
7. Was the ~85% measured? - No - rough self-assessment over the cases I looked at, no held-out set (a held-out set is fresh examples kept back, never shown to the AI, for scoring accuracy fairly - I never built one, so 85% is my judgment, not a test figure). I say that out loud before anyone asks. [PARTIAL - honest label]
8. And the 30 s -> 5 s? - ~5 s is the API response time - how long the online AI service takes to send the answer back, computer time, not anyone's working minute - an estimate for a typical region; the real saving is most of the engineer's ~30 s zoom-and-decide, which is their own account, not a timed study. Boards ran 5-20 flagged regions - the number of red marks one board could carry, per the engineers' reports. [PARTIAL - reported/typical, not instrumented]
9. What if the API is down or there's no key? - The analysis disables itself with a clear message; the red regions still work. It was designed as an enhancement, never a dependency - and the offline standalone build (the single self-contained file that runs on a machine with no server and no installs) runs no VLM at all - no picture-AI whatsoever, exactly the pre-feature tool. [SAFE]
10. Cost - did anyone push back on that? - About a cent an analysis (the AI service's charge for one answer, a rough figure), so ~a dime for a ten-mismatch board - the full AI bill for one board with ten red marks. Only flagged regions went to the model, so cost scaled with mismatches, not components. [SAFE]
11. Any disagreement with the users? - Not with them. The assumption I had to give up was mine: that a strong VLM means reliable value-reading. It doesn't - context beats OCR, and blurry pixels beat both (better reasoning and better instructions still can't read letters that aren't visible). [PARTIAL - no documented user conflict]
12. Scale / who did this? - I built the suite solo. Across the full suite it was about 15 engineers across 2 sites - the people using the suite overall, not just this AI feature - and they told me it saved roughly 20 hours a week: what they said they were saving, their recollection rather than anything a system counted - engineer-reported, not instrumented. [PARTIAL - reported]
13. What would you do differently? - Ship the confidence label, the human-verifies framing, and the incorrect-mark path in the first version, not as the response to the complaint. And start from a simple prompt - my fancy first one was the slow way around. [SAFE]
