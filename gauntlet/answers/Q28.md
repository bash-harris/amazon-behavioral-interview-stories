## Answer (core, ~470 words)

The complex problem was the BOM reconciliation parser in the PCB inspection suite, and the part I could not solve on my own was not in the data — it was in the manufacturing conventions underneath it.

The tool reconciled a Bill of Materials exported from SAP against the PCB layout exported from Altium and reported components missing, extra, or mismatched. I wrote the obvious parser first: read the Excel with XLSX.js, pull each reference out with something like `/^[A-Z]+\d+$/`. That worked for roughly 70% of real BOMs. Then production files arrived and it broke, one edge case at a time. The failures that were pure pattern problems — an `MP_` process-code prefix, or SAP cells exported as hyperlinks so XLSX.js handed back an object instead of a string — I could diagnose myself by logging raw cell values and auditing the data.

But the hardest one was not a pattern problem. `R1||R2` and `R1/R2` were coming through, and my parser was treating them as invalid and silently dropping them. I did not know whether that was right. I could see the string; I could not see the intent. So I went to the person who did: a manufacturing engineer on the floor who used the BOM tool and knew how the boards were actually built.

He explained it plainly. Those notations mean one physical position can accept either part depending on which board variant you are building. So when the BOM lists `R1||R2` and the layout shows a single position, the system should treat R1 and R2 each as a valid match for that position — not as an error to discard. That was the piece I could not get from the file. What looked like malformed data was a legitimate manufacturing instruction.

I rewrote the variant splitter around exactly what he told me: normalize both `||` and `/`, extract the individual references, and treat each as a valid match target. Then I built a test matrix — `R1||R2`, `R1/R2`, `R1||R2||R3`, and malformed cases like `||R1` or `R1/` — and verified each behaved correctly after the change.

The result: across 15 representative production BOMs the parser reached zero false positives and zero false negatives, and a 2,000-component BOM parsed in under 200 milliseconds. The engineers could upload their two files and get a reconciliation report in seconds instead of spending 20 to 30 minutes cross-referencing by hand. In the first week it caught three real mismatches manual review had missed.

The lesson changed how I approach detail. Diving deep is not only reading the data harder — sometimes the detail lives in a person who holds the intent, and you have to go ask. I had been treating `R1||R2` as a parsing bug when it was a question about manufacturing. My honest reflection is that I found these reactively, one production failure at a time; I should have sat with the engineers and collected representative BOMs before writing the parser, not after it broke.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Who exactly did you talk to, and why that person?**
**A:** A manufacturing engineer who used the BOM tool and did the board cross-referencing by hand before it existed. He was the one who actually knew how variants are built, so he held the intent behind the notation that I could only see as text.

**Q: Why couldn't you work this out from the data alone?**
**A:** The data gave me the string `R1||R2`, not its meaning. I had assumed it was malformed and dropped it. The fact that it represented one physical position accepting either part depending on variant is a manufacturing convention — it is not inferable from the BOM file by pattern matching, which is exactly why I needed a person.

**Q: How did you verify the change matched what he told you?**
**A:** A test matrix of the variants and malformed cases — `R1||R2`, `R1/R2`, `R1||R2||R3`, `||R1`, `R1/`. For each I checked the parser extracted the right individual references and treated each as a valid match target for that position.

**Q: How did you decide what to solve yourself versus what to ask about?**
**A:** Pattern problems — the `MP_` prefix, hyperlink cell objects — I could audit in the data and fix myself. Semantic intent — what a variant reference or a DNP entry means on the line — only the engineers could tell me. I made that split deliberately and went and asked for the second kind.

**Q: What if no engineer had been available?**
**A:** By default the parser skips anything that does not match the standard reference pattern, so it degrades instead of crashing — but that would have meant silently dropping valid components, which is a worse failure than an error. That is a big part of why I would collect samples and interview users before writing the parser next time.

**Q: What did this teach you about diving deep?**
**A:** That detail is not only in the artifact. Some of it lives in the people who hold the context, and the fastest way to it is to ask an explicit, narrow question rather than guess and ship a fix.
