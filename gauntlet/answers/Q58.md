# Q58 — a time when you had to make a difficult decision to meet a tight deadline

**Mapped LP(s):** Bias for Action; Deliver Results; Are Right, A Lot.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the five-day prototype scope decision (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 8, "Bias for Action — First Working Prototype"). Near-duplicate of Q19; this is a deliberate re-frame around **the decision itself** — the options on the table, the criteria, and the trade-off — rather than Q19's tight-deadline framing or Q36's quality framing. One telling only: STORY 8's numbers (five-day prototype, three issues found within an hour, production-ready in two weeks = 5 days + 9 days). The prototype is described consistently as having **no export** — the Good Board export fallback (STORY 1) is not attributed to it. ## Answer (core, ~430 words)

The difficult decision wasn't "work faster." It was *which version to build first*, and I had to make it before I had the information to make it cleanly. The manufacturing engineers wanted an automated PCB comparison tool, and the request had sat on the backlog for six months because everyone kept trying to scope it properly. I decided the real bottleneck wasn't capability — it was definition.

So I put the options on the table honestly.

Option one was to keep scoping. That was the status quo, and six months of it had produced nothing.

Option two was to build the version I'd be comfortable putting my name on: upload, image alignment, illumination handling, and export. That was the version that eventually took about two weeks.

Option three was to build the smallest end-to-end slice that answered one question — *can the tool see a difference at all?* — in five days, and defer everything that made the answer trustworthy.

I chose three, and the reasoning was that it was a reversible bet. A prototype can be thrown away, so buying information with it was worth more than spending two weeks on a guess about what they needed. That's the decision: when the constraint is definition, the fastest path is a working artifact in front of the users, not more analysis.

The trade-off was real and I named it before the demo. Option three meant boards had to be photographed at the exact same angle under the same light, because alignment and illumination handling were the deferred half. That's a tool that works on my desk and fails on a real line, and I knew it. So I drew one line for myself: cut features, not correctness. The prototype had to be correct under the conditions it claimed to support. I wrote the limitation down instead of hiding it.

Day five I demoed to the engineering lead, and he shared it with the team immediately. Within an hour they gave me three concrete problems: pixel comparison failed under lighting changes, there was no alignment so photos had to match exactly, and there was no export. Those three issues became the roadmap. I ordered them by impact — alignment first, then illumination, then export — and the tool went from that prototype to production-ready in two weeks: five days plus nine.

That's why I'd still defend the call. The five-day prototype generated more useful direction than six months of requirements gathering, and it met the date. What I'd do differently is collect a couple of real board photos before the demo, so the team's first hour went to next steps instead of rediscovering a limitation I already knew about. The decision wasn't speed over quality — it was choosing what to buy with the time I could actually verify.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Was there an external deadline, or did you set it?**
**A:** I set it, and I'd say so plainly. There was no imposed date. What existed was a six-month backlog and a scoping stalemate. I chose five days to force a decision instead of another week of analysis. The "tight" part was that I chose to compress — not that someone imposed it.

**Q: Why was shipping the smallest slice the right call rather than building the trustworthy version?**
**A:** Because the binding constraint was definition, not capability. Nobody could tell me what to build, so a rough artifact in front of real users bought information that more scoping wouldn't. And the decision was reversible — a prototype can be discarded, so the downside was bounded.

**Q: What criterion separated what you kept from what you cut?**
**A:** Whether it changed *whether* the concept worked or only *whether the result was trustworthy*. Upload, comparison, and a visual result answer "can it see a difference." Alignment, illumination, and export are what make the answer trustworthy. The trustworthy half was the deferrable half.

**Q: Isn't knowingly shipping something that fails on a real line a bad decision?**
**A:** The cut was "works under real manufacturing conditions," not "works correctly." It was correct under the conditions it claimed to support, and I named the limitation before the demo rather than letting the team discover it. A named limitation I can defend is a different thing from a hidden defect.

**Q: What if the team had rejected it outright?**
**A:** I should be straight: there wasn't a fallback artifact, because export was one of the three gaps, so nothing could be saved out. The only route was the manual comparison the engineers already did. The risk I was actually managing wasn't adoption — it was building the wrong features, and shipping early is what removed that risk.

**Q: How do you know the decision was right rather than lucky?**
**A:** The outcome was specific and directional. The team surfaced three concrete issues inside an hour, those became the priority order, and the tool reached production-ready two weeks later. That's the feedback loop working, not a feeling.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Collect a couple of real board photos before the demo, so the team's first hour went to next steps instead of rediscovering the lighting and angle limitations I already knew about.
