## Answer (core, ~430 words)

I have to be straight with you: I don't have a documented disagreement with a senior engineer that I can stand behind. Have Backbone is the thinnest part of my story bank — the banks rate it Weak, and the one episode that touches it is a disagreement with a tool's measurement rule, not a person. So I'll give you the pattern I'd actually bring into that room, labeled as a framework rather than claimed as an event.

The closest real episode is the pin-count rule in the BOM reconciliation tool. The tool compared the pin count in the BOM against the number of pads in the layout footprint, literally, and called any difference a mismatch. I didn't agree with that measurement. Instead of saying "pin count is unreliable" — which is just a preference — I pulled 20 BOMs with pin-count mismatches and categorized every one into three buckets: footprint larger than the component, footprint smaller, and exact match. Footprint-larger was almost always a legitimate package variation; footprint-smaller was a genuine mismatch because the part physically can't fit; exact match was fine. That turned an opinion into a finding the team could act on.

Here's the framework I'd use with a senior engineer, because the difference from the tool rule is the counterpart, not the method.

First, assume they have context I don't. Seniority usually means they've seen failure modes I haven't, so I'd ask what I'm missing before assuming they're wrong. That isn't deference; it's benchmarking my view against better information — the same reflex that made me abandon affine alignment after a controlled test showed it was 15 pixels off at the board edge while homography was 2.

Second, disagree privately and directly, with evidence. Not in a group thread, not sideways to the team. Bring the specific data and the specific risk — a correctness gap, a customer impact, a cost — because "I disagree" isn't a disagreement, but "here's the board population where this rule fails and what it costs us" is.

Third, separate the technical point from authority. I'd argue the finding, not the org chart, and I'd say up front what evidence would change my mind — then genuinely update if it arrives. That happened when a manufacturing engineer corrected my parser: "R1||R2" should count as a valid match for either reference. He knew the floor better than I did, and I changed the code.

Fourth, commit. If the senior engineer still decides against me, I implement it as if I'd made the call, and I don't relitigate it in side conversations. If executing it surfaces a new material fact, I raise it once, with evidence, then respect the call. Half-committing is the worst outcome.

One line I'd hold: if it isn't a reversible business call but a compliance, safety, or customer-trust obligation, committing isn't the whole answer — I'd raise it through the chain, documented. I haven't had to do that, so I won't pretend I have. And I won't pretend the senior-engineer scene happened either. What's real is the data-first reflex and the commit discipline.
