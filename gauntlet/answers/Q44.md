# Q44 — tell me about a project where you had to learn a new skill and eventually excelled at it

**Mapped LP(s):** Learn and Be Curious (primary); Deliver Results (primary evidence of excelling).

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the EfficientNetV2S defect-classifier pipeline (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 3, "Building a Machine Learning Pipeline from Scratch", with the training-strategy detail and the final numbers from STORY 14, "Progressive Unfreezing"). ## Answer (core, ~470 words)

Nothing in my record said I could train a production model. I had ML theory and I had never shipped a model anyone depended on. The project was the defect classifier inside the PCB inspection suite — take a cropped image of a component and classify it as good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing — and the bar was explicit: above 90% on held-out data, under 100ms per image, and retrainable as new defect types appeared. I had to learn the whole thing end to end to clear it.

I learned it in the order the problem demanded. First the architecture: I read across ResNet, EfficientNet, and Vision Transformers and reasoned from the data I actually had. ViTs want large datasets and serious compute; ResNet is well understood but surpassed; EfficientNetV2S offered the best trade of training efficiency, ImageNet strength, and scaling to a custom set. I checked the detail that decides it — the small variant is about 21 million parameters, and the accuracy gap between it and the large variant on my data was under two points, which didn't justify three times the training cost. Second, the data: I built a Tkinter labeler with keyboard shortcuts and CSV output, and applied CLAHE to normalize lighting across fluorescent, LED, and shadowed shots, so the model learned component features instead of illumination. Third, the method: transfer learning, not training from scratch, because ImageNet already encodes the edges, textures, and shapes a PCB crop needs.

Then the results started teaching me. My first fine-tune hit 99% training accuracy and 72% validation — memorizing, not learning. I didn't paper over it with a bigger model. I learned the mechanism: the randomly initialized classification head produces large gradients that overwrite the pre-trained features. So I used two-phase progressive unfreezing — freeze the base and train the head for 10 epochs at 0.001, then unfreeze the last 30 layers at 0.0001 so the base adapts to PCB textures without being rewritten. I logged every run in Weights & Biases and tested the one choice that mattered: 20 layers underfit, 50 overfit on the small set, 30 was the measured sweet spot. I also handled the 5:1 good-to-defect imbalance by weighting defects 5x and reading precision-recall instead of raw accuracy.

That's where "excelled" stops being a claim. On a held-out set of 500 component images the model reached 94% — above the 90% bar — with only a three-point training-to-validation gap, so it was generalizing rather than memorizing. Inference was 45ms per image on CPU, under the 100ms target. Recall was 92% for wrong-value, 96% for wrong-orientation, and 98% for missing components. And the pipeline retrained on new data in about 30 minutes on a single GPU, so the model could improve without stalling the inspection line.

What I'd change: collect more than the ~2,000 images I had, and use a learning-rate schedule instead of a fixed rate in phase two. The skill that transferred wasn't TensorFlow — it was knowing how to find the next failure and close it.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Why EfficientNetV2S and not something bigger, or a Vision Transformer?**
**A:** I matched the model to the data and the input. The crops are 224×224 component images; the model needs local features — color, shape, text — not complex scene semantics. ViTs want large datasets and significant compute, which I didn't have. Within EfficientNetV2, S is about 21 million parameters and trains roughly 3x faster than L, and the S-vs-L accuracy gap on my data was under two points — so S was the right point on the curve, not a compromise.

**Q: How did you actually get good enough at TensorFlow and transfer learning to ship this?**
**A:** I learned in the order the pipeline demanded and validated each piece. Build the labeling tool, preprocess with CLAHE, then load a pre-trained EfficientNetV2S and fine-tune. The hard part wasn't the API — it was the training dynamics. My first run overfit, and learning *why* (a random head corrupting pre-trained features) is what made the second design work. I also exported the model to TensorFlow.js for client-side inference, where the preprocessing has to match training exactly or the outputs are nonsense.

**Q: How do you know 94% is real excellence and not a lucky split?**
**A:** Three things. It's measured on a held-out set of 500 images, not training data; the training-to-validation gap is only three points, which is the signature of generalizing rather than memorizing; and I report per-class recall — 92/96/98 — instead of one aggregate number that a majority class could inflate. I also split at the component level so the same component couldn't leak across train and test.

**Q: What was the hardest single thing to learn?**
**A:** The training strategy, specifically catastrophic forgetting. It's counterintuitive that unfreezing *more* of a pre-trained network can make it worse, but on a small dataset it does — 50 layers overfit where 30 generalized. The second-hardest was the data pipeline: making the browser preprocessing match the training preprocessing exactly, because a silent mismatch produces confident wrong answers.

**Q: Where did the 2,000 labeled images come from?**
**A:** I built the labeling tool myself — a Tkinter app with keyboard shortcuts and CSV output that cut labeling from about 30 seconds to about 3 seconds per image, so the full set took roughly 100 minutes instead of an estimated 10 hours. I later re-labeled the whole set a second time to check label quality, which is a different answer's story, but the point here is that the "learn a new skill" project included building the data supply, not just the model.

**Q: What would you do differently, and what does that say about how you learn?**
**A:** Collect more than ~2,000 images, and use a learning-rate schedule like cosine annealing instead of a fixed rate in phase two. I'd also assert the input tensor shape before inference so a preprocessing mismatch fails loudly instead of silently. The pattern across all of it: I learn by building the smallest real version, measuring it honestly, and fixing the binding failure — not by studying until I feel ready.
