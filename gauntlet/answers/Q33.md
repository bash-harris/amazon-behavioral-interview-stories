## Answer (core, ~500 words)

I'd point to the stretch where I stopped trying to make my PCB defect classifier better and spent the time figuring out what was capping it.

The classifier sat at 94% accuracy on about 2,000 labeled component images. The obvious next move was more model work — a different architecture, learning-rate schedules, more epochs. I held off, because I didn't know what the limit was. The question I dug into was narrow: is 94% a model-capacity ceiling or a data ceiling? Those call for completely different work.

To answer it I measured two things.

First, I plotted learning curves during training — validation performance as the amount of training data changed. That curve is the diagnostic: when it flattens, adding model complexity buys less than adding data, and it puts a number on the payoff. Ours had flattened, and reading it gave me an estimate: doubling from 2,000 to 4,000 images would move accuracy to roughly 96%, and past 4,000 the improvement would be marginal. That's a concrete marginal value for the next labeling push, not a feeling.

Second — the part I'd have skipped — I audited the labels themselves. I had labeled the whole set, so it was tempting to trust it. Instead I labeled the entire dataset a second time, a week apart, and compared the passes. My agreement with myself was 92% — about one label in twelve was one I couldn't reproduce. That matters: if I can't produce the same label twice, the model can't be expected to learn that distinction, so part of the flattening wasn't the model's capacity, it was noise in the data. I went back through the 8% disagreements and resolved each to the label that best described the primary defect, so the set that trained the model was one I'd re-audited.

That combination is how I knew I was focusing on the right thing. The curve gave me the direction and size of the payoff — more data helps, up to about 4,000, then it doesn't. The self-agreement check told me part of the ceiling was label quality, a different fix from more labels or a bigger model. With both numbers on the table I could point effort at the binding constraint instead of tuning a model that was already near the data's limit.

The outcome was a data strategy I could defend with numbers: 2,000 images was enough to reach 94%, roughly 4,000 buys about two more points, and beyond that the 3 seconds per image is the cheap part — finding and cropping new images takes far longer. I stopped treating model tuning as the bottleneck.

What I'd do differently: add batch labeling for near-identical images. Many crops were the same component at a slightly different angle; a "label all similar" function would have cut labeling time and exposed how much of the 2,000 was near-duplicate content. Near-duplicates add images, not information — exactly the padding that makes a learning curve look rosier than the data deserves.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: What did the learning curves actually show you?**
**A:** That we were in the flattening part of the curve — the marginal value of each additional image was shrinking. Reading the curve gave me the estimate directly: 2,000 images → 94%, doubling to 4,000 → about 96%, and beyond 4,000 the gain was marginal. That turned "should we collect more data?" into a number I could act on: the return was real up to ~4,000 and flat after, so spending beyond that wasn't justified.

**Q: A 92% agreement with yourself sounds low — how did you use that number?**
**A:** As a ceiling, not just trivia. If roughly one label in twelve changes when I re-label the same image a week later, then that fraction is genuinely ambiguous, and no model can learn a distinction I can't make consistently. So part of the plateau was data noise, not model capacity — which meant the next move was label quality, not architecture. I re-examined every one of the 8% disagreements and resolved each to the label that best described the primary defect.

**Q: How do you know the model wasn't just under-trained — that the ceiling really was the data?**
**A:** Because I measured both sides instead of assuming. On the model side, the learning curve had flattened, which is the signature that more data matters more than more capacity. On the data side, the self-agreement audit showed the labels themselves carried noise. Two independent measurements pointing at the data, not the architecture, is why I redirected effort there rather than running another tuning sweep.

**Q: Why stop at ~4,000 images instead of just collecting more?**
**A:** Return versus cost. The curve said beyond 4,000 the accuracy gain is marginal. The cost side isn't just labeling — each image is about 3 seconds to label, but finding and cropping new board images takes far longer. So the honest answer was to stop when the curve flattened, not when the count looked impressive.

**Q: How is this different from the training work where you improved the model's accuracy?**
**A:** That work was model-side — changing what the network adapts and how. This was data-side — asking whether the data could support a better model at all, then measuring the labels and the curve to find the answer. They're complementary, but the decision here was where to spend effort, and it came out of the curve and the label audit, not a hyperparameter change.

**Q: Where did the labels come from, and weren't they just your own opinion?**
**A:** They were my own labels, which is exactly why I tested their reproducibility rather than trusting them. Labeling the full set twice a week apart and comparing passes is a direct check on whether the labels are stable. 92% agreed; the 8% that didn't were the genuinely ambiguous ones, and I resolved those individually instead of leaving a coin-flip label in the training set.
