# Q92 — tell me about a time you used data to solve a problem

**Mapped LP(s):** Dive Deep; Are Right, A Lot; Deliver Results.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the **learning-curve analysis** behind the EfficientNetV2S defect classifier: deciding how much more training data was worth collecting, and when to stop and ship. Single telling: the labeling economics and the learning-curve estimate in `AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 9 ("Building the Training Data Collector" — Result line 885, follow-up line 911) plus STORY 3's measured result. **Distinct re-frame** from Q63 (an engine make-vs-buy recommendation), Q27 (the 4-signal comparison pipeline), and Q80/Q4/Q88 (the pin-count fix): here the delivered skill is *measuring the return on data itself* — using data to decide where effort should stop.

## Answer (core, ~450 words)

Data answered a question for me that wasn't really about the model: how much more training data was worth collecting, and when to stop and ship. I'll give you the measured version, because that's the honest one.

I was the sole builder of a PCB defect classifier — a cropped component image in, a label out: good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing. I had a working pipeline and about 2,000 labeled images, and the open question was whether to keep labeling. More data almost always helps in the abstract, but the labels were mine to make, so every extra image was real time. I needed to know where the return flattened, not whether more data was good.

So I used the data I already had. I plotted learning curves during training — accuracy against dataset size — and the shape gave me the answer: doubling the set from about 2,000 to 4,000 images would move accuracy from 94% to roughly 96%, and past that the curve was close to flat. That's a measured trade: several more hours of labeling for about two points. I also measured the labeling economics. The keyboard-driven tool I'd built cut labeling from about 30 seconds to about 3 seconds per image, so the 2,000 images took roughly 100 minutes instead of an estimated 10 hours. Cheap per image — but the curve said those hours were buying less and less.

I checked the quality of the data, not just its quantity. I labeled the whole set twice, a week apart, and compared: 92% agreement with myself, and the 8% disagreements were the borderline defects, which I re-examined and resolved. That told me the labels were trustworthy and where the ambiguity lived.

One trap the data saved me from: with a 5:1 good-to-defective imbalance, a model could hit about 83% accuracy by always predicting "good." So I weighted defective examples 5x and read precision-recall instead of raw accuracy, and I split train and test at the component level so the same component couldn't leak across sets.

The outcome was the model shipping at 94% on a held-out set of 500 images, 45ms per inference on CPU, with per-class recall of 92%, 96%, and 98% for wrong-value, wrong-orientation, and missing components. The data-driven decision was *not* to chase the last two points. My honest reflection is that more data would have helped generalization, and I say so — but the curve told me where the effort stopped being worth it, and that's what let me ship on schedule instead of grinding for a number the tool didn't need.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: What exactly did the learning curve tell you?**
**A:** That I was already near the flat part. Going from about 2,000 to 4,000 labeled images projected to about 94% → ~96%, and beyond that the gain was marginal. That's a measured marginal return, which is what made the stop decision defensible rather than a guess.

**Q: How did you get the labeling economics?**
**A:** From the labeling tool I built. It cut labeling from about 30 seconds per image (manual CSV entry) to about 3 seconds with keyboard shortcuts, so 2,000 images took roughly 100 minutes instead of an estimated 10 hours. That per-image cost is what I multiplied against the curve's marginal accuracy to decide whether more labeling was worth it.

**Q: Why did you double-label the whole set?**
**A:** Because a 94% number is only as good as its labels. I labeled the set twice, a week apart, and compared — 92% agreement. The 8% disagreements were the borderline cases, and resolving them by re-examining gave me both a quality check and a map of where the data was genuinely ambiguous.

**Q: With a 5:1 class imbalance, why not just report accuracy?**
**A:** Because a model could have hit ~83% accuracy by always predicting "good," which would have looked respectable and been useless. I weighted defective examples 5x in the loss and used precision-recall as the primary metric, so the number reflected detecting the rare class that actually matters on a production line.

**Q: How do you know the 94% wasn't leakage or a lucky split?**
**A:** Three things. It's on a held-out set of 500 images, not training data; the split was stratified and done at the component level so the same component couldn't land in both train and test; and I report per-class recall — 92/96/98 — rather than one aggregate that a majority class could inflate.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Collect more than the ~2,000 images, and use a learning-rate schedule rather than a fixed rate in phase two. But I'd still make the stopping decision the same way — with the curve, not with a feeling about whether more data "feels" better.
