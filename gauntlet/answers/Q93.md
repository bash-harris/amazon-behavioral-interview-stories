**Mapped LP(s):** Learn and Be Curious.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — integrating a hosted vision-language model (GPT-4o) as an interpretation engine inside a quality tool, an unfamiliar external AI surface with no training data and no guarantee of correctness (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 8, "Adding an AI Eye to the Inspection"). ## Answer (core, ~460 words)

Outside my comfort zone isn't always a new library. The stretch I'd point to is putting an unfamiliar, external AI model inside a tool people depend on — in the loop of a quality decision I couldn't fully verify myself.

The tool was the PCB inspector. It could flag where a photographed board didn't match its design, but it couldn't tell an engineer what the difference *meant*, so someone had to zoom into every flagged region and interpret it — about 30 seconds each, and five to twenty regions per board. I wanted to automate that interpretation, and I made a bet I had never made before: use a hosted vision-language model, GPT-4o, as the eye that reads the mismatch.

That was uncomfortable for concrete reasons. I had no training data for this task and no guarantee the model would be accurate. It was a cloud dependency on a restricted factory network, it cost money per call, and its answers weren't reproducible the way a versioned model's are. A confident wrong answer could mislead an inspector.

So I managed it instead of trusting it. Before committing, I benchmarked the options — GPT-4o, GPT-4V, Claude 3, and Gemini Pro Vision — and chose on measured axes: GPT-4o was fastest at roughly 3 seconds per call against about 8 for GPT-4V, and Gemini was weaker on small surface-mount parts. I iterated the prompt from a loose "describe what you see" into a structured one that forced a JSON answer — component type, mismatch type, confidence — because the loose version produced verbose, inconsistent output. And I bounded the blast radius: the model only sees a small set of flagged regions, not the whole board; its output is labelled an AI suggestion with a confidence, not a diagnosis; there's a correct/incorrect feedback loop for the engineers; and the tool degrades gracefully when the API is unreachable. Before any board image went out, I confirmed with the security team that the images weren't sensitive.

The honest part: it landed at about 85% on component and mismatch type, and the 15% failures were mostly images too poor or parts too small to read. I also learned a wrong assumption — I thought the VLM could read the values on small surface-mount parts; it's better at type and orientation than exact values. What made it worth shipping was human verification: a 30-second manual interpretation became about a 5-second first pass that an engineer confirms.

I had never built anything whose correctness leaned on an external model I don't control. That's the discomfort — and the discipline it taught me, benchmark before betting, constrain the context, label the uncertainty, keep a working fallback, is one I've reused since.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Why was integrating a VLM "outside your comfort zone" rather than just another API call?**
**A:** Because it put an external, non-deterministic model inside a correctness-critical step I couldn't verify analytically. I had no labeled data for the interpretation task, the answers weren't reproducible like a versioned classifier's, and a confident wrong answer could mislead an inspector. That's a different kind of risk from calling a deterministic service.

**Q: What made you comfortable enough to ship it at ~85%?**
**A:** Human verification and bounded scope. It only interprets a small set of already-flagged regions, its output is a confidence-labelled suggestion the engineer confirms, and the deterministic comparison stays the core. At 85% as a first pass against 30 seconds of manual interpretation per region, it was worth shipping with a person in the loop.

**Q: How did you choose GPT-4o over the alternatives?**
**A:** Benchmarking on measured axes, not reputation. GPT-4o was about 3 seconds per call versus about 8 for GPT-4V; Claude 3's API was less mature at the time; Gemini Pro Vision was weaker on small surface-mount detection, which is the whole point for SMD parts. Speed and small-object accuracy decided it.

**Q: What were the risks, and how did you bound them?**
**A:** Latency, cost, availability, and data privacy. I bounded them by cropping the model's job to a small region of interest, using about a cent per analysis, keeping the four-signal comparison as an offline fallback with graceful degradation, and getting security clearance because board images carry no trade secrets. The VLM is an enhancement, not a dependency.

**Q: Where did you go wrong?**
**A:** Two places. My first prompt was too loose and produced inconsistent output, so I converged on a structured JSON prompt with examples. And I assumed the model would read component values from photographs; the text on small SMD parts is often too small or blurry, so it's reliable on type and orientation and weak on exact value.

**Q: What would you do differently now?**
**A:** Explore a smaller self-hosted VLM — LLaVA-class — instead of a hosted API, for cost and control, provided the hardware has the VRAM. On the factory machines it doesn't, which is why the API was the right call at the time, but I'd re-evaluate it as local models get cheaper.
