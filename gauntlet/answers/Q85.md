## Answer (core, ~440 words)

The deep dive I'd point to is one where the numbers looked fine and the metric was quietly wrong. I was building the component comparison in the PCB inspection tool — crop a component from the board photo and the layout, score how different they are, flag the ones that differ. The pipeline produced plausible scores. I didn't trust the pass rates, so I went into the scoring code to see exactly how the number was made.

The first thing I found was a normalization step doing more than it admitted. To compare crops of different shapes, I letterboxed each crop to a square 128×128 tile, padding with a constant gray `(114,114,114)` to preserve aspect ratio. Then SSIM — structural similarity — was computed over the entire 128×128 tile, including the padding, and reduced to a single number by taking the mean of the similarity map.

That's where the dive paid off. The padding is identical in both images by construction — the same gray, the same rectangle — so local SSIM in the padding region is essentially 1.0, a perfect match. Averaging over the whole tile therefore drags the score toward "pass," and it does so in proportion to how much of the crop is padding. A component with a 2:1 aspect ratio is about half padding, so roughly half its similarity score is a guaranteed "identical." The detector was systematically most blind on exactly the parts I cared most about — tall connectors and wide ICs, the components whose absence matters most. A normalization step had injected a statistical bias into the metric.

I also saw the warts in the same function: a first intermediate value computed and then recomputed, with a comment admitting the first was a hack, and an allocation that was never used and deleted at the end. Small, but they told me this path had never been scrutinized.

The fix is identified and scoped, but I have not landed it, and I want to be precise about that. The change is small: restrict the mean to the valid region using the crop bounds the letterbox step already computed — the offset and the true width and height — so the SSIM map is reduced over that rectangle instead of the padded square, or the mean takes a validity mask. That code is still on my to-build list; I did not make the change and I did not re-measure it. I also didn't have a labeled ground-truth set, so I couldn't quote a recall delta on connectors, and I wasn't going to invent one. Applying the fix is a couple of hours; the honest before-and-after needs a labeled set and a precision-recall curve, and that loop wasn't closed.

The lesson I keep: a metric is only as good as the region it's averaged over, and a normalization step can inject a bias that never crashes anything. It just makes the numbers plausible and wrong. The other lesson is about what I can claim — a diagnosis is something I did; a fix and its delta are things I'd have to show.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: How did you find it — did something crash?**
**A:** No. That's what made it worth the dive: nothing crashed. The clue was behavioral — the score moved with a component's aspect ratio in a way it had no business doing. I read the scoring path instead of the output, and the padding was the only step correlated with shape.

**Q: Walk me through the mechanism again, mathematically.**
**A:** The crop is padded to a 128×128 square with constant gray. SSIM is computed pairwise over the whole tile and reduced with a mean. In the padded region the two images are identical by construction, so local SSIM is essentially 1. The mean is therefore pulled toward 1 in proportion to the padded fraction, which rises with aspect ratio. The valid content's similarity is diluted by guaranteed-perfect padding.

**Q: Have you fixed it?**
**A:** No, and I want to be exact. I identified the fix — reduce the SSIM map over the `offX/offY/newW/newH` rectangle that the letterbox step already computes, or pass a validity mask — but the code still averages the full padded tile, and the prep doc lists the fix and its measurement as a to-build item. I won't present designed work as built. Landing the one-line change is a couple of hours; proving it needs labels I didn't have.

**Q: What would measuring it take?**
**A:** A labeled set. I'd label roughly two hundred components — including a good number of non-square connectors and ICs — run the pipeline before and after the ROI restriction, and plot the precision-recall curve so the fix has a before and an after instead of just a mechanism. My own training-data collector already does that labeling, which is the tool I'd reuse.

**Q: What else did you find in that function?**
**A:** Two smaller warts — an intermediate value computed and then recomputed, with a comment admitting the first pass was a hack, and an unused allocation deleted at the end. They didn't affect results, but they were a signal that the code path had never been reviewed closely.

**Q: Why did a bug like that survive?**
**A:** Because the scoring functions were pure but trapped in files that touched the DOM at load time, so nothing could import them and no headless test could run. If I could have tested SSIM in isolation, an aspect-ratio case would have exposed the bias immediately. Making the core importable is the real fix behind the fix.

**Q: What did this teach you about metrics?**
**A:** Two things. A metric averaged over a region only means something if the region is meaningful — a mean over padding is partly a mean over nothing. And normalization can inject statistical bias: the step that made the crops comparable also quietly made non-square components look more similar.
