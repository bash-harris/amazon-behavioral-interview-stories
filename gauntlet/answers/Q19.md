# Q19 — tell me about a time when you had to make a difficult decision to meet a tight deadline

**Mapped LP(s):** Bias for Action (primary); Deliver Results; Are Right, A Lot.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the first working prototype (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 8, "Bias for Action — First Working Prototype"). The decision is the scope cut: keep only the smallest end-to-end slice and ship it on a self-imposed five-day demo date instead of building the trustworthy version. One telling only — STORY 8's numbers (five-day prototype, three issues found within an hour, production-ready in two weeks). The 4000+ line / 21-module refactor figures from `AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 7 and the "500 lines" line count from STORY 8's own follow-up are both deliberately not cited. The prototype is described consistently as having **no export** — the export fallback from STORY 1's Good Board tool is not used. Angle differs from Q13, which used the same prototype for the quality trade-off line; here the subject is the deadline decision and the trade-off weighed.

## Answer (core, ~420 words)

Tight deadline, and a decision I had to make before I had enough information — which is the honest version of this question. The manufacturing engineers wanted an automated PCB comparison tool. It had sat on the backlog for six months because everyone was still trying to scope it. I decided the bottleneck wasn't capability, it was definition, so I gave myself a five-day demo.

The hard part was what to cut. A version I'd be comfortable showing would have had image alignment, illumination normalization, and export — the version that ended up taking two weeks, five days to the prototype plus nine days of refinement. I had five days. So the decision was to build the smallest end-to-end slice that still answered the real question — *can this tool see a mismatch?* — and defer everything that made the result trustworthy. I kept upload, a pixel-level comparison, and a visual result. I cut alignment, illumination handling, and export. That meant boards had to be photographed at the exact same angle under the same light, which almost never happens on a real line. I knew that, and I shipped it anyway, because the alternative was another stretch of polished guessing about what they needed.

The trade-off I weighed was speed of feedback against accuracy of the first impression. I judged that a rough tool that provoked a reaction was worth more than a careful tool nobody had validated. I drew one line: I'd cut features, not correctness. The prototype worked under the conditions it claimed to support. I wrote the limitation down, and I said it out loud in the demo.

I demoed on day five. The lead shared it with the team immediately, and within an hour they gave me three critical issues: pixel comparison failed under lighting changes, there was no alignment so photos had to match exactly, and there was no export. That was exactly the information I'd bought. I prioritized by impact and frequency of complaint — alignment first because nothing worked without it, illumination second, export third — and took it from prototype to production-ready in two weeks: five days to the prototype plus nine days of refinement.

The result: a five-day imperfect build generated more useful direction than six months of requirements gathering, and it hit the date. What I'd do differently is collect a couple of real board photos before the demo, so the team's hour went to next steps instead of rediscovering what I already knew. The decision I'd still defend was cutting scope to a named limit to hit the date — not cutting the standard I could actually verify.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Was there a real deadline, or was five days self-imposed?**
**A:** Self-imposed, and I'd say so plainly. There was no external date forcing five days. What existed was a six-month backlog and a scoping stalemate. I set the date to force a decision instead of another week of analysis. The "tight" part was real in the sense that I chose to compress, not that someone imposed it.

**Q: How did you decide which three features to keep versus cut?**
**A:** I asked what question the demo had to answer. It had to show the tool could detect a mismatch at all. Upload, comparison, and a visual result answer that. Alignment, illumination handling, and export make the result *trustworthy* — they don't change whether the concept works. So the trustworthy half was the deferrable half.

**Q: Isn't shipping a knowingly inaccurate tool a bad decision?**
**A:** The cut was "works under real manufacturing conditions," not "works correctly." It was correct under the conditions it claimed to support, and I documented the limitation before the demo rather than hiding it. Shipping a named limitation to learn what to build is a different act from shipping something broken.

**Q: What if the team had rejected the prototype outright?**
**A:** I should be straight about that — there wasn't a fallback artifact, because export was one of the three gaps they'd found, so nothing could be saved out of it. The only fallback was the manual route they already used, comparing the two board images by eye, and the prototype at least put that comparison on screen. The risk I was actually managing wasn't adoption; it was building the wrong features, and shipping early is what removed that risk.

**Q: How do you know the decision was right, rather than just lucky?**
**A:** Because the outcome was specific and directional. The team found three concrete issues within an hour, those issues became the priority order, and the tool reached production-ready in two weeks. That's the feedback loop working, not a vague "it felt right." I'm not claiming the tool was good on day five — only that the decision to cut scope to a date is what produced the roadmap.

**Q: Did the speed create debt you're still paying?**
**A:** Yes, in two places. I refactored the prototype's monolith into modules after the first two weeks so the next feature wasn't a rewrite. And later I shipped a second tool by copying the first because I needed it working that week — that bought a deadline and then cost months of divergence, because the copies drifted. The lesson isn't "don't copy"; it's set a deadline on the copy.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Collect a couple of real board photos before the demo, so the team's hour went to next steps instead of rediscovering the lighting and angle problems I already knew about.
