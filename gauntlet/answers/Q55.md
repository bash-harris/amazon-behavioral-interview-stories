# Q55 — tell me about a time you had to motivate a team after a demoralizing event
**Mapped LP(s):** Earn Trust (primary); Think Big.

## Answer (framework, ~420 words)

First, the honest part: I built the PCB inspection suite solo, so I don't have a team I had to motivate, and I won't borrow the gesture-controlled device story from the question - that's the poster's, not mine. What I can give you is how I'd run that situation, grounded in the closest thing the record actually has: getting the manufacturing engineers to trust a tool when they had good reason not to.

When morale drops, my first move isn't a speech - it's an honest read of the facts. The two documented times the work looked bad, I named it plainly instead of spinning it. The parser worked on about 70% of real BOMs and then broke in production; I said so and listed the edge cases. The pin-count rule produced false alarms about 12% of the time; I didn't defend it, I pulled 20 BOMs and categorized every failure. A team can't re-anchor on a picture that's prettier than reality.

Second, I'd re-point the group at a direction worth rallying to. Think Big isn't optimism - it's finding the defensible move. When the factory-floor constraint killed the server approach, the direction was to ship the whole suite as one self-contained HTML file that needs no internet, no installs, and no IT ticket. That's the thing that had been demoralizing - deployment friction - turned into the thing worth building.

Third, make the next step small, concrete, and visible, and take the hardest part myself. I sequenced the suite tool by tool, learning OpenCV.js for the second and TensorFlow.js for the third, so each finished piece funded the next. On the pin-count issue I did the analysis, the rule rewrite, the debug view, and the regression run myself.

Fourth, build credibility with verifiable action, not reassurance. The debug view let the engineers audit every decision; in the first week they used it to surface two real edge cases, and I fixed both. Then I kept the commitment - the suite shipped, was adopted by 15 engineers across two sites, and saved roughly 20 hours a week.

Last, I'd make the recovery structural, not motivational. Shared infrastructure meant one fix improved all three tools, so the next setback wouldn't compound. That's the honest framework: candor, a defensible direction, a small visible next win, verifiable follow-through, and a structure that makes the improvement stick.
