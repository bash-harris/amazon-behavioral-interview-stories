## Answer

**(a) A conflict with a team member.**
I'll be straight about the conflict, because I'd rather label the gap than invent a scene: the record doesn't contain a blow-up with a teammate, and I won't claim one. It does contain two real disagreements. The first wasn't a fight — it was a correction. I had built the BOM parser to treat variant references like `R1||R2` and `R1/R2` as invalid and drop them; a manufacturing engineer pushed back and explained that they mean one physical position can accept either part depending on the board variant, so the system should match either. He was right about the floor, I was wrong about the data, and I rewrote the parser. The second was a data-first pushback on how the tool measured a match — the literal pin-count rule was generating false alarms on legitimate footprints — so I pulled 20 BOMs and reframed the problem into three evidence-backed buckets instead of an opinion. My framework for a real peer conflict is: raise it once, directly, with the data and the customer/correctness risk attached; argue the finding, not the person; ask what evidence would change the outcome; and once it's decided, commit fully and don't relitigate it sideways. I haven't had the documented blow-up, and I won't pretend otherwise.

**(b) Stepping out of your comfort zone.**
The stretch that was genuinely outside my comfort zone wasn't a new library — it was **giving up control of my own tool**. The manufacturing engineers used the BOM reconciliation tool but didn't trust it: when it flagged a mismatch, the report showed the verdict and hid the reasoning, so for anything that mattered they re-verified by hand, which defeated the point of automating it. The comfortable move would have been to fix the bugs privately and tell them the tool was better now. Instead I did the uncomfortable thing: I exposed the tool's reasoning and handed them override authority. I built a `?debug=true` view that shows, per component, the raw BOM entry, the parsed reference after every preprocessing step, the layout reference it was matched against, the result, and the specific rule that fired — a step-by-step decision tree. The principle was that if an engineer asks "why did it call this a mismatch?", the view has to answer completely, because any missing step just sends them back to me. That's the discomfort: making your own heuristic auditable, in public, by the people who depend on it. It is a different kind of stretch from learning a new technology — it's about being willing to be shown wrong by the customer.

**(c) A project you recently worked on.**
The most recent project is Tab Scroller, a Manifest V3 Chrome extension I built as an AI research workspace — a hover-activated tab ribbon with `Ctrl+K` search, an `Alt+Shift+T` command bar that turns natural language into browser actions on cloud Gemini or local Ollama, and a token-saving engine where simple commands are resolved by local rules and never hit the API. The project I'd want probed deepest, though, is the Bosch PCB Inspector Suite: three browser-based inspection tools — BOM reconciliation, PCB-vs-layout, and good-board-vs-new-board — that I delivered solo in eight weeks and that were adopted by 15 engineers across two sites. That's where the technical depth is: the BOM parser, the homography-plus-TPS alignment pipeline, and the EfficientNetV2S defect-classifier training pipeline.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: You didn't really have a conflict, did you?**
**A:** Honest answer: no documented peer conflict exists. The variant correction and the pin-count pushback are the closest real evidence; the rest is a labeled framework, not a claimed scene. I'd rather say that than perform a conflict I didn't have.

**Q: How would you handle a peer who won't accept your data?**
**A:** Escalate the evidence, not the emotion: put the finding in writing, ask what evidence would change their mind, bring in the person who holds context I'm missing, and once it's decided, commit and stop relitigating it in side conversations.

**Q: Why is transparency "outside your comfort zone" rather than just a feature you built?**
**A:** Because it's a different axis of discomfort from learning a new library. The tool was mine; making its reasoning, including its mistakes, visible to the engineers who depend on it meant inviting them to find where my heuristic was wrong and to overrule it. That's about giving up control, not about unfamiliar technology — and it's the part I'd have skipped if I'd taken the comfortable route.

**Q: What's Tab Scroller's biggest weakness?**
**A:** The AI action layer shipped without the guardrails I'd want — a confidence gate, transactional undo, and telemetry. The failure mode matters because a wrong model call can close a user's tabs, which is exactly why those guardrails belong with the feature, not after it. That design is on paper, not in the build, and I say so plainly.

**Q: Which project would you want probed deepest?**
**A:** The PCB suite — that's where the technical depth is (the BOM parser, the alignment pipeline, the four-signal comparison, the training pipeline). Tab Scroller is where the most recent learning is.

**Q: What ties the three parts together?**
**A:** Two habits: get the real evidence in front of the decision, and be explicit about what's documented versus what's designed.
