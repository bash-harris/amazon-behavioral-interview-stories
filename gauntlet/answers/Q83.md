## Answer (core, ~470 words)

The hardest decision I made on the PCB inspection suite wasn't a feature — it was which model to bet the defect classifier on, and I had to make it before I had a labeled dataset or any experience training a production model.

The problem: take a cropped image of a component and classify it as good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing. The bar was fixed before I started — above 90% accuracy, under 100 ms per inference, and retrainable as new defect types showed up — and I had to hit it without stalling the inspection tools that were already in use.

I put three options on the table honestly. Train a convolutional model from scratch — ResNet was well-understood but already surpassed, and from scratch needs data and compute I didn't have. Use a Vision Transformer — powerful, but it wants large datasets and significant compute, which described exactly what I lacked. Or transfer-learn an efficient backbone, reusing ImageNet features and fine-tuning on my small set.

I scored each option against accuracy on a small custom dataset, training cost, and inference speed, and I chose EfficientNetV2S with transfer learning. The reasoning was that ImageNet pre-training already encodes edges, textures, and shapes — and component inspection is a local-feature task, not scene understanding, so those features transfer directly. Fine-tuning would be faster and need far less data than training from scratch.

Then the part that was genuinely a decision rather than a lookup: within the EfficientNetV2 family, S is about 21 million parameters, M about 54 million, and L about 119 million. S was enough for 224-by-224 crops of local features, it trains roughly three times faster than L, and the accuracy gap on my data was under two percent. I chose the smallest model that cleared the bar, not the strongest one available.

The second half was harder to commit to. I trained in two phases — freeze the base and train only the classification head for 10 epochs at 0.001, then unfreeze the last 30 of roughly 100 layers for 20 more epochs at 0.0001. The reason is that a randomly initialized head produces large, noisy gradients early that would rewrite the pre-trained features the whole bet depends on; stabilizing the head first keeps them. I didn't assume the layer count — I tested unfreezing the last 20, 30, and 50. Twenty underfit, fifty overfit on ~2,000 images, thirty was the measured middle.

What made the decision difficult was that the first run failed: 99% training accuracy, 72% validation. The instinct that failure invites is to add capacity; the data said to stabilize and regularize instead. I held the decision.

It held. The model reached 94% on a held-out set of 500 component images, with 45 ms inference on CPU against the 100 ms target — recall of 92% for wrong value, 96% for wrong orientation, 98% for missing — and it retrained on new data in about 30 minutes on a single GPU.

What I'd decide differently isn't the model — it's the data and one assumption. I started with about 2,000 images, which is low for fine-tuning; augmentation compensated, but more data would generalize better. And I assumed CLAHE preprocessing would fully normalize the factory lighting; extreme cases defeated it, and I'd try to control capture instead. The selection call — small, transfer-learned, progressively unfrozen — I'd make again.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Why not just train a model from scratch — isn't that more "real" ML?**
**A:** It's the wrong tool for a small-data problem. PCB component classification is 224-by-224 local features with roughly 2,000 labeled examples; from scratch would overfit or demand far more labels and compute. ImageNet pre-training already carries edges, textures, and shapes, so transfer learning was both faster and safer. Knowing which problem you actually have is the decision.

**Q: Why EfficientNetV2S and not M, L, or a Vision Transformer?**
**A:** V2S/M/L are about 21M / 54M / 119M parameters. The task is local features on small crops, not scene understanding, so S was sufficient; it trains roughly three times faster than L and the accuracy gap on my data was under two percent. The ViT path needed more data and compute than I had. I picked the smallest model that clears the bar, not the strongest one.

**Q: Why progressive unfreezing instead of fine-tuning the whole network at once?**
**A:** The classification head starts random, so early in training it emits large, noisy gradients. Unfreezing the whole base immediately would let those gradients overwrite the pre-trained features the entire bet relies on. Phase one freezes the base and stabilizes the head; phase two adapts the base at a 10x lower rate. Skipping phase one is exactly what produced 99% training against 72% validation.

**Q: How did you decide on 30 layers, and how do you know it wasn't luck?**
**A:** I tested it. Unfreezing the last 20 underfit — the frozen early layers couldn't produce PCB-specific features. Fifty overfit on ~2,000 images. Thirty was the measured middle: enough trainable parameters to learn the domain without memorizing it. I tracked the runs and compared them objectively rather than trusting a memory of which felt better.

**Q: What was the hardest part of the decision?**
**A:** Committing before I could prove it. I had no production-model track record, the first run failed, and the failure's obvious lesson — "add capacity" — was the wrong one. The decision was to trust the mechanism: stabilize, then adapt, and regularize the small dataset instead of making the model bigger.

**Q: When is a "difficult decision" one you should slow down on?**
**A:** When it's irreversible or when the missing information is cheap to get. This one was reversible at the architecture level — I could change backbone or training strategy and retrain in about 30 minutes — so committing early was rational. The part I'd slow down is the data: get representative labeled samples before betting, because that's the input the whole decision rests on.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Collect more than ~2,000 images before training, and control the capture lighting instead of assuming CLAHE would normalize extreme variation. The model selection I'd defend; the data groundwork I'd redo.
