# Q81 — tell me about a time when your project deadline was very near, but you still took time to verify or test the data/code before submission

**Mapped LP(s):** Insist on the Highest Standards; Deliver Results.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the image-alignment validation before shipping the PCB-vs-layout tool (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 2, "When Affine Alignment Wasn't Enough"), inside the fixed eight-week delivery window (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 12, where PCB vs Layout is the weeks-3–4 milestone). ## Answer (core, ~460 words)

The deadline I have in mind is the PCB-vs-layout tool, one milestone inside a fixed eight-week delivery window. It was the piece with the steepest learning curve — browser-based computer vision — and the natural pressure at that point was to ship once the aligned overlay looked right and move on to the next tool.

What stopped me was the arithmetic downstream. The comparison engine runs on a ten-pixel mismatch threshold, so if alignment is off by more than about three pixels, every result it produces is noise. "Looks aligned" is not evidence at that tolerance. So instead of shipping on the overlay, I built a verification step that measured the thing that mattered: a set of 20 board images with ground-truth markers placed at known positions, and I measured the pixel offset after alignment. The average offset came out at 2.1 pixels with a standard deviation of 0.8 — which told me the pipeline was inside the 2–3 pixel requirement, not just plausibly close.

The number alone wasn't enough, so I added a second, qualitative check. I had the manufacturing engineers overlay the aligned photo on the layout and visually confirm that component pads lined up. That mattered because it catches a failure mode the average hides: a case where the aggregate metric looks fine but the warp is subtly wrong in a local region. Two independent checks, because a single metric can pass while the product is still wrong.

Crucially, I treated verification as a condition of submission, not an optional task to run if time permitted. That is what the deadline pressure was pushing me to skip, and it was exactly the wrong thing to skip: shipping the overlay-looking version would have pushed alignment error into the comparison as false alarms, and the engineers would have lost trust in a tool that had not earned it yet.

The result justified the time. Average misalignment dropped from 15–20 pixels with homography alone to 2–3 pixels with the full pipeline, and comparison accuracy went from roughly 85% to over 98% for boards with moderate warping. The pipeline handled photos up to 30 degrees off-perpendicular and up to about 3mm of board warp, which is what made the ten-pixel comparison threshold reliable instead of noisy.

Two honest notes. The validation has a limit: severely warped boards above roughly 5mm still defeat the approach, and I said so rather than implying it was solved. And the change I'd make is process — I'd start simpler and add complexity incrementally; I spent time on full feature matching before finding that simpler board-outline detection covered most boards. What I would not change is verifying before submission. That discipline is the reason the result was trustworthy.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Why wasn't the aligned overlay good enough to ship?**
**A:** Because the comparison runs on a ten-pixel mismatch threshold, so alignment error above about three pixels becomes false alarms downstream. An overlay that looks right to the eye can hide a couple of pixels of local error, and that is enough to change the comparison output. I needed a measurement, not an impression. (STORY 2 Task, line 188.)

**Q: What exactly did the verification consist of?**
**A:** Two checks. Quantitative: 20 board images with ground-truth markers at known positions, measuring the pixel offset after alignment — average 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8. Qualitative: the manufacturing engineers overlaid the aligned photo on the layout and visually confirmed pads lined up, which caught cases where the number looked good but the warp was subtly wrong. (STORY 2 follow-up, line 260.)

**Q: How close to the deadline was this, and did it delay you?**
**A:** The whole suite was delivered inside an eight-week window and the PCB-vs-layout tool was the weeks-3–4 milestone, so every stage was on the clock. The validation was a bounded, defined step — not open-ended polish — and it was cheaper than shipping an alignment that silently corrupted the comparison and had to be re-diagnosed later. (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 12, lines 466, 473.)

**Q: What did verifying under deadline actually change in the outcome?**
**A:** It moved the tool from "built" to "trustworthy." Average misalignment went from 15–20 pixels with homography alone to 2–3 pixels, and comparison accuracy from roughly 85% to over 98% for moderately warped boards. Without that verification I would not have known whether the ten-pixel threshold was reliable. (STORY 2 Result, line 208.)

**Q: What was the honest limit you found?**
**A:** Severe warping — above roughly 5mm — still defeats the approach, because the control points can't capture extreme deformation; that would need a physically based model, though it's rare in production. I stated that limit rather than implying the alignment was solved for every board. (STORY 2 Reflection, line 216.)

**Q: Is there anything you'd do differently about the verification itself?**
**A:** I'd start simpler and add complexity incrementally, and I'd lean harder on the qualitative overlay check earlier — it caught things the aggregate number hid. The measurement set itself I'd keep; ground-truth markers and engineering spot-checks are the right two lenses. (STORY 2 Reflection, line 214; follow-up line 260.)

**Q: Isn't this the same verification story as your standalone-build harness?**
**A:** Different subject. The standalone harness verified that a compiled artifact matched the server version — output parity on 15 BOMs and 10 images. This is about verifying the alignment pipeline's accuracy against ground truth, measured in pixels, before the comparison tool shipped. Both are the same discipline, applied to different risks.
