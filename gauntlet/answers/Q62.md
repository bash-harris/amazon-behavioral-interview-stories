## Answer (core, ~450 words)

The clearest feedback-from-above moment I have came through my engineering lead, and the channel matters, so I'll be precise about it before the lesson.

An automated board-comparison tool had sat in a six-month backlog because it was "too complex" to scope. I stopped scoping and built a working prototype in five days — a single monolithic HTML file, inline JavaScript, a basic pixel-level comparison. It worked under ideal conditions. I demoed it to my lead on day five, and he immediately put it in front of the team. Within an hour the team came back with three problems: the pixel comparison broke under illumination changes, there was no image alignment so boards had to be shot at exactly the same angle, and there was no export. It reached me through my lead and a team review, not as a personal one-on-one, and I won't inflate it past that.

The one that actually changed how I work was the alignment point. Not because it was the hardest fix, but because of what it said: the prototype only held under conditions I controlled — same lighting, same angle — and real factory photos were neither. That meant my validation was the flaw, not just one function.

So the specific change wasn't "build a better prototype." The record shows I already believed a rough prototype was the right call, and the result bore that out. The change was *when and how I validate*. Before that, I judged work by whether it looked right in front of me. After, I stopped trusting a demo and started collecting the real inputs — actual factory photos and BOMs — and testing against those before I claimed anything worked. On that tool it meant alignment first, illumination normalization second, export third, and production-ready in two weeks. The durable part is the process: gather representative real-world samples before building, and test under the conditions users actually have rather than the ones I can arrange.

A second change came out of the same review: I build observability in from the start now. The debug view on the later BOM tool was an afterthought, and building it earlier would have let a user show me exactly which rule fired instead of me guessing what went wrong. I also carried the collect-the-failures-and-analyze-the-patterns discipline onto the next tool's illumination, rotation, and scaling problems instead of fixing symptoms one at a time.

Reflection: the review cost me an hour of pride and bought me a two-week roadmap that was actually right. The feedback wasn't really about the prototype — it was about my bar for what "working" was allowed to mean.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Was that really negative feedback from a higher up, or a team critique?**
**A:** It was a critique delivered through my lead. I demoed to him, he put it in front of the team immediately, and the team found the three issues within an hour. No verbatim critique from him is documented and I won't invent one. The "higher up" element is real in that a senior reviewer exposed it to real users and it didn't hold up — not that he sat me down and told me I'd failed.

**Q: What exactly changed in how you work because of it?**
**A:** The validation point. I stopped judging work by whether it looked right in front of me and started collecting real inputs — factory photos, real BOMs — and testing under the conditions the users actually had. That is the change that carried onto the next tool, not the three bug fixes.

**Q: Why single out the alignment issue when all three were the team's list?**
**A:** Because alignment was the one that couldn't be blamed on a function — it was a condition mismatch. The tool only worked at the same angle and lighting I'd used to build it. That's a flaw in how I validate, and it generalizes; the pixel and export issues were surface fixes.

**Q: How did you keep the change from being a one-off?**
**A:** I applied the same discipline to the Good Board tool's illumination, rotation, and scale problems — collect the failing cases, analyze the patterns, fix root causes, add them to the regression set — instead of patching the first example I saw. And I started building observability in from the start so users could show me the failing case cheaply.

**Q: How did you stay non-defensive?**
**A:** I'd documented the prototype's limitations before the demo, so the feedback confirmed known gaps rather than exposing hidden ones. The point of the prototype was to learn what to build; defending it would have meant defending the wrong thing.

**Q: What would you still do differently?**
**A:** Collect the representative real inputs before I write the first version, not after the review. The record already names this as the lesson on both the BOM parser and the comparison tool — I discovered the conditions reactively instead of gathering them up front, and I've since front-loaded that step.
