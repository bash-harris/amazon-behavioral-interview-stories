## Answer (core, ~440 words)

The closest I have to negative feedback from above came through my engineering lead. I'll give you the documented sequence.

Manufacturing engineers had wanted an automated PCB comparison tool for six months, and it kept stalling because it was "too complex" to scope properly. I stopped scoping and built something working instead — a five-day prototype. It was a single monolithic HTML file with inline JavaScript: no build system, no modules, no tests. It loaded two images, ran a basic pixel-level comparison, and displayed a result. It worked under ideal conditions and was rough everywhere else, and I knew that when I walked in.

I demoed it to my engineering lead on day five. He shared it with the team immediately, and within an hour the team came back with three critical issues. The pixel comparison failed under illumination changes. There was no image alignment, so boards had to be photographed at the exact same angle. And there was no export, so engineers couldn't save results. That was the feedback, and it was genuinely negative — the prototype wasn't usable on real boards yet. It came through my lead in a team review rather than as a formal one-on-one, and I won't inflate it past that.

I didn't defend it. I'd documented the limitation going in, so the feedback confirmed known gaps rather than exposing hidden ones. I accepted all three as real and asked the team what order mattered.

That turned the recovery from "fix everything" into a roadmap. I prioritized by impact and frequency of complaint: alignment first, because without it nothing else could work; illumination normalization second, because it was the most frequent complaint; export third, because it was valuable but not blocking. Then I delivered — the tool went from rough prototype to production-ready in two weeks, five days to the prototype plus nine days of refinement.

The lesson wasn't "build better prototypes." I'd optimized for speed of feedback and underinvested in realism. Two changes stuck. First, I started building observability in from the start instead of adding it after trust eroded — the debug view on the BOM tool was an afterthought, and building it from the beginning would have caught edge cases earlier. Second, I applied the same collect-the-failures, analyze-the-patterns discipline to the Good Board tool's illumination, rotation, and scale problems. The review cost me an hour of pride and bought me a two-week roadmap that was actually right.

If a manager had instead told me the whole approach was wrong, I'd ask for the specific failing case, reproduce it on the same inputs, and test the alternative against the cases that broke mine — that framework is in the follow-ups.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Was that really negative feedback "from a higher up," or a team critique?**
**A:** It was a team critique delivered through my lead. I demoed to him, he put it in front of the team immediately, and the team identified the three issues within an hour. No verbatim critique from him is documented, and I won't put words in his mouth. The higher-up element is real in that a senior reviewer put it in front of real users and it didn't hold up — but I won't call it a one-on-one performance conversation when it wasn't.

**Q: How did you stay non-defensive?**
**A:** I'd documented the prototype's limitations before the demo, so the feedback confirmed known gaps instead of exposing hidden ones. The whole point of the prototype was to learn what to build. Defending it would have meant defending the wrong thing.

**Q: What specifically did you change?**
**A:** Short term, the tool: alignment first, illumination second, export third, production-ready in two weeks (5 prototype + 9 refinement). Long term, how I work: build observability in from the start rather than as a post-incident addition, and apply the collect-failures-and-analyze-patterns discipline to every tool — which is what I did on the Good Board tool's illumination, rotation, and scaling edge cases.

**Q: How did you decide the priority order?**
**A:** Impact and frequency. Alignment gated everything — without it, comparisons on real photos couldn't be trusted. Illumination was the most frequent complaint. Export was valuable but didn't block inspection, so it went last.

**Q: What if the lead had said the entire approach was wrong? (FRAMEWORK)**
**A:** I'd want the specific failing case, not a general verdict — a concrete case is reproducible and a verdict isn't. I'd reproduce it on the same inputs, test the alternative against the cases that broke mine, and commit to whichever survives the evidence. That's a framework; the record shows the three specific issues, not a wholesale rejection.

**Q: What did this change about how you take feedback now?**
**A:** I ask for the specific failing case rather than a general verdict, and I try to reproduce it in the user's environment rather than mine. I also try to make feedback cheap to give — the debug view exists so a user can show me exactly which rule fired instead of just telling me the answer looks wrong.
