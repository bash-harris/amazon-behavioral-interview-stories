## Answer (core, ~380 words)

The customer interaction I'd point to was with the manufacturing engineers who used the PCB-vs-layout comparison tool — and it was negative, delivered directly, and they were right.

The context: the tool compares a photograph of a physical board against its layout and highlights where components are missing or misplaced. The comparison runs on a 10-pixel mismatch threshold, so alignment has to hold to about 2–3 pixels or every result downstream is noise. My alignment was global — a homography — and by my metric it looked good. Then the engineers began reporting false alarms: the tool was flagging mismatches on warped boards that were actually fine. That's the worst kind of feedback for a QA tool, because every false alarm sends an engineer to hand-verify a part that was never wrong.

I could have pulled up my numbers. Instead I asked them to show me. I had them overlay the aligned photo directly on the layout and check the pads — and that's where the feedback got specific. The aggregate number looked good, but on the overlay the pads in some regions clearly didn't line up. My metric and their eyes disagreed, and their eyes were right. The board had flexed in the reflow oven, and the warping was non-uniform, so a single global transform structurally cannot fix a bow in the middle of the board.

What made it customer obsession rather than just bug-fixing is that I worked backwards from their experience. The constraint wasn't "make my metric better" — it was "the engineer has to trust this result on a real, warped board." So I treated their overlay as data I didn't have rather than an opinion to argue with, asked them to mark the failing cases, and reproduced them on the same inputs. That reproduces the physics: I added a second, non-rigid stage — a thin-plate-spline warp on top of the homography — to correct local deformation by fitting a smooth warp through control points at corners, fiducials, and pads.

Average misalignment dropped from 15–20 pixels with homography alone to 2–3 with the non-rigid stage, and comparison accuracy went from roughly 85% to over 98% for boards with moderate warping. The false alarms stopped and the engineers went back to trusting the tool. And I kept their check: the overlay spot-check became a standing gate in my validation, alongside the quantitative check on 20 ground-truth images — average offset 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8 — because it caught what the metric could not.

Earning trust back wasn't the apology; it was the delivered result and keeping their check in the loop. The lesson I still carry: when a customer tells me my result is wrong, the number is my hypothesis and their observation is the data.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Was that really direct negative feedback, or a QA finding?**
**A:** Both, and I'll be precise. The engineers reported false alarms — that's user feedback. Then, in the overlay check, the alignment that passed my metric visibly failed on the pads. That second part was direct and corrective, with the user driving it. I don't have a verbatim quote, so I won't invent one.

**Q: How did you keep from getting defensive?**
**A:** I separated the two kinds of evidence. My aggregate looked good; their overlay showed pads that didn't line up. Rather than argue the average, I asked them to show me the failing cases and I reproduced them. The number was my hypothesis; their observation was data I didn't have.

**Q: What specifically did you change?**
**A:** I added a non-rigid thin-plate-spline stage after the homography, with control points at corners, fiducials, and pads, rejecting any control-point match below a template-similarity threshold because one bad point bends the whole warp. Average misalignment went from 15–20 pixels to 2–3; comparison accuracy from roughly 85% to over 98% on moderate warping.

**Q: How did you make sure it stayed fixed?**
**A:** I kept the engineers' overlay spot-check as a standing validation gate alongside the quantitative check on 20 ground-truth images — average offset 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8 — and I re-ran a fixed regression set so a fix for one case couldn't break another.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Bring the customer into validation earlier. I validated against a number before I validated against their eyes, and their eyes found the gap the number hid. If the overlay had been in the loop from the start, I'd have caught the warping sooner.

**Q: Why is this "customer obsession" and not just debugging?**
**A:** Because the thing I optimized changed. I stopped optimizing an alignment score and started optimizing the engineer's ability to trust the tool on a real board. That reframed the fix from "improve the metric" to "make the tool trustworthy under the conditions they actually work in" — and it made their overlay a permanent part of how the tool is validated.
