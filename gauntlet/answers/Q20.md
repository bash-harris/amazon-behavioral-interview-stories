## Answer (core, ~500 words)

The deep dive I'd point to is the image alignment in the PCB-vs-layout tool — the stage that decides whether a photograph of a board can be compared to its CAD layout at all. The comparison runs on a 10-pixel mismatch threshold, so if alignment is off by more than about three pixels, every result downstream is noise. I learned that the hard way.

I started with the conventional answer: feature matching into a transform, and I first tried an affine. Affine felt right — six parameters, and it handles rotation, scaling, translation, and shear. It worked on flat boards photographed straight-on and failed on real ones. The surface symptom was "alignment is bad." The actual cause was in the camera model. An affine transform preserves parallel lines. But photograph a board at an angle and its parallel edges converge toward a vanishing point — perspective distortion breaks parallelism, so no affine matrix can undo it. So I switched to `findHomography` with RANSAC, which models perspective projection, needs four point pairs because eight unknowns take eight constraints, and rejects wrong feature matches so a few bad correspondences can't corrupt the matrix. I ran RANSAC at its default 2000 iterations — conservative against the ~370 the theory needs at a 50% outlier rate, adding maybe 50 ms for a large accuracy benefit.

That fixed the global case, but real photos still averaged 15–20 pixels of misalignment with the homography alone. The detail that unlocked it was physical: PCBs go through a reflow oven, and the heat warps them non-uniformly — the center bows differently than the edges. A homography is a single global transform, so it structurally cannot fix a bow in the middle of a board. I needed a non-rigid stage. I compared three options — elastic deformation, B-splines, and thin-plate splines — and chose TPS because it minimizes bending energy, is deterministic given the control points with no iterative tuning, needs no physical material parameters like Young's modulus that have no clear meaning for a PCB, and is mature in OpenCV. I wired it after the homography, selected control points by template matching known features — board corners, fiducials, component pads — and rejected any match whose template similarity fell below a threshold, because a bad control point pulls the whole warp the wrong way.

I validated instead of eyeballing it. On 20 board images with ground-truth markers the average offset after alignment was 2.1 pixels with a 0.8 standard deviation, and the manufacturing engineers did visual spot-checks overlaying the aligned photo on the layout — that caught cases where a good number hid a subtly wrong warp. The result: average misalignment dropped from 15–20 pixels to 2–3, and comparison accuracy went from roughly 85% to over 98% for boards with moderate warping. The pipeline handles photos up to 30 degrees off-perpendicular and up to 3 mm of warp.

What I'd do differently: start simpler and add complexity incrementally. I spent time implementing full SURF feature matching before finding that simple board-outline contour detection was sufficient for most boards. And an honest limit: very severely warped boards — above about 5 mm — still defeat TPS, because control points can't capture extreme deformation; that would need a physically based model, and it's rare in production.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Explain affine versus perspective transform mathematically.**
**A:** An affine transform maps (x,y) to (ax+by+e, cx+dy+f) — a linear transform plus a translation, six parameters — and it preserves parallel lines. A perspective transform maps (x,y) to ((ax+by+e)/(gx+hy+1), (cx+dy+f)/(gx+hy+1)): it divides by a linear function of the coordinates, has eight parameters, and does not preserve parallelism. Parallel lines can converge to a vanishing point, which is exactly what an angled photograph does.

**Q: Walk me through RANSAC in the homography.**
**A:** It randomly selects 4 point pairs, computes a homography from them, and counts how many other pairs agree within a pixel tolerance (inliers), repeating 2000 times and returning the model with the most inliers. It's robust to up to about 50% outlier matches. Four points is the minimum because a homography has 8 unknowns and each pair gives 2 constraints; 3 points is underdetermined, more is overdetermined.

**Q: Why TPS over elastic deformation or B-splines?**
**A:** TPS minimizes bending energy and has a clean, deterministic solution given the control points; it needs no material-property tuning like Young's modulus or Poisson's ratio, which have no clear meaning for a PCB; and OpenCV's implementation is mature. B-splines require choosing a control-grid resolution as a hyperparameter, and elasticity requires physical constants I don't have in a manufacturing environment.

**Q: How many control points, and what if one is badly matched?**
**A:** TPS requires at least 4 control points for a 2D warp, but with exactly 4 there's no redundancy to average out noise. For a well-constrained warp I want them spread across the board; in practice I typically find 15–30. A wrong control point would pull the warp field toward the wrong position, so I use template matching with a similarity threshold and reject weak matches.

**Q: How did you validate the alignment accuracy?**
**A:** A test set of 20 board images with ground-truth markers — I placed markers at known positions and measured the pixel offset after alignment. The average offset was 2.1 pixels with a standard deviation of 0.8. I also had the manufacturing engineers do visual spot-checks, overlaying the aligned photo on the layout to confirm pads lined up, which caught cases where the quantitative metric looked good but the alignment was subtly wrong.

**Q: Is there a bug in how you approximate local scale from the homography?**
**A:** There is a known weakness: the code uses a single element of the homography matrix as a proxy for local scale, which is only valid under pure translation and uniform scale — it's wrong under rotation or shear, which is exactly what a homography exists to model. The correct local scale is the square root of the determinant of the Jacobian of the projective map at that point. I found that by auditing my own code, not by inspection of the output.
