# Q48 — tell me about a time when you had a blocker in your work and how did you resolve that blocker?

**Mapped LP(s):** Bias for Action; Deliver Results.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the first training run of the EfficientNetV2S defect classifier producing an unusable model (99% training accuracy / 72% validation accuracy), resolved with a two-phase progressive-unfreezing strategy (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 14, "Progressive Unfreezing — Teaching a Model What to Forget", supported by STORY 3, "Building a Machine Learning Pipeline from Scratch"). This is a deliberately different blocker from Q29 (the OpenCV.js/WebAssembly loading blocker in the standalone build) — here the blocker is the deliverable's result being unusable, not the deployment pipeline. One telling only: the PREPARATION.md numbers (99%/72% → 94% validation on a held-out 500 images, 3-point gap, 45ms inference, ~2,000 images, 10 epochs at 0.001 then last-30-layer unfreeze for 20 epochs at 0.0001). The conflicting `AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 6 telling (85% → 93%, 500 boards) is not used.

## Answer (core, ~450 words)

The blocker I'd point to is the first version of the ML defect classifier — not a bug in infrastructure, but the model itself coming out unusable, which blocked the whole inspection deliverable it was meant to power.

The classifier's job was to take a cropped image of a component and label it good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing, and the bar to ship was validation accuracy above 90% with inference under 100 ms. My first training run came back at 99% training accuracy and 72% validation accuracy. That gap is the tell — the model had memorized the training set instead of learning. On paper I had a model; in practice I had one I could not put in front of a manufacturing engineer, because a false negative on a PCB can mean a defective board ships.

So the blocker was real and it stopped the deliverable. What I did not do was throw a bigger model at it or start guessing at hyperparameters. I root-caused it. I had fine-tuned by letting gradients flow into the whole pre-trained network, and the problem was catastrophic forgetting: the classification head starts with random weights, so early in training it produces large, noisy gradients that overwrite the general features — edges, textures, shapes — that ImageNet pre-training had already learned. I was destroying the thing I was trying to reuse.

The resolution was a two-phase progressive-unfreezing strategy. Phase one: freeze the entire EfficientNetV2S base and train only the classification head for 10 epochs at a learning rate of 0.001, so the head stabilizes and the gradients are controlled before anything touches the pre-trained layers. Phase two: unfreeze the last 30 layers and continue for 20 epochs at 0.0001 — a 10x lower rate — so the base adapts to PCB textures without being rewritten.

I also tested the one assumption that mattered instead of trusting it. I compared unfreezing 20, 30, and 50 layers, tracking runs in Weights & Biases so the comparison was objective: 20 underfit and 50 overfit on my roughly 2,000 images, and 30 was the measured sweet spot. I added early stopping with a patience of five epochs so training stopped when validation loss flattened.

The unblock: validation accuracy went from 72% to 94% on a held-out set of 500 component images, with a training-to-validation gap of only three points, so it was genuinely generalizing. Inference ran at 45 ms per image, under the 100 ms target, and the model retrained on new data in about 30 minutes on a single GPU, so new defect types wouldn't stall the pipeline.

What I'd do differently: I assumed more unfrozen layers would always help and 50 proved that wrong; I'd also use a learning-rate schedule like cosine annealing instead of a fixed rate in phase two, and collect more than ~2,000 labeled images. The lesson I'd carry is that when the blocker is the result itself, acting fast is not the same as guessing fast — I moved quickly, but I moved on the cause.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: What exactly was the blocker?**
**A:** The model was unusable, not the code. First run: 99% training accuracy, 72% validation accuracy. The ship bar was >90% validation. A model that memorizes the training set can't be trusted to classify boards it hasn't seen, so the classifier could not go into the inspection tools in that state.

**Q: How did you diagnose it rather than just retrain with different settings?**
**A:** The train/validation gap was the clue — it points at memorization, not underfitting. That led me to the cause of the memorization: I had unfrozen the whole pre-trained network against a small dataset, so the randomly initialized head's large early gradients overwrote the general ImageNet features. Naming that as catastrophic forgetting is what made the fix obvious, instead of blindly tuning.

**Q: Why two phases? Couldn't you just lower the learning rate?**
**A:** Lowering the rate alone doesn't fix the ordering problem. Phase one trains only the head with the base frozen, which stabilizes the gradients before anything touches the pre-trained layers. If you skip that and unfreeze immediately, the noisy head gradients corrupt the base — which is exactly what produced the 99%/72% run. Phase two then adapts the base with a 10x lower rate.

**Q: How did you pick 30 layers?**
**A:** I tested 20, 30, and 50 and compared validation accuracy in Weights & Biases. Twenty underfit — the frozen early layers couldn't produce PCB-specific features. Fifty overfit on roughly 2,000 images. Thirty gave enough trainable parameters to learn domain patterns while the frozen layers preserved general features, and the final 3-point train/validation gap is the evidence it wasn't luck.

**Q: How did you know the blocker was actually resolved and not just the numbers you wanted?**
**A:** The 94% is on a held-out set of 500 component images, not the training set, with a training-to-validation gap of only three points. The per-class recalls were also balanced — 92% wrong-value, 96% wrong-orientation, 98% missing — and inference stayed under the 100 ms target. Generalization and per-class behavior are the test, not a single accuracy figure.

**Q: When is a blocker yours to solve versus something to escalate?**
**A:** This one was inside my own pipeline, I had a concrete path to it, and escalating would only have added delay. If it had required data or access I didn't have — say, labeled boards I couldn't get — that's when I'd raise it with the people who control that input.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Use a learning-rate schedule like cosine annealing instead of a fixed rate in phase two, and collect more than ~2,000 labeled images — more data would improve generalization and would have made the layer-count choice less delicate. I also assumed more unfrozen layers always help, and the 50-layer overfit disproved that.
