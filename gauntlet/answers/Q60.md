## Answer (core, ~440 words)

The bottleneck showed up in the first working version of the PCB-vs-layout comparison, and it was the version-killing kind. The prototype was good at exactly the wrong thing: on my clean, flat test images it aligned a photograph to the CAD layout well enough to compare. On the first real factory-floor photos it didn't.

Why that's a bottleneck and not just a bug: the comparison runs on a 10-pixel mismatch threshold. If the photograph and the layout are off by more than about three pixels, every flagged mismatch downstream is noise. My first approach — feature matching into a global transform — left the average misalignment at 15–20 pixels on real boards. The whole premise of the tool is comparing positions, and the positions didn't line up.

What I didn't do was start tweaking the detector. I localized the bottleneck first, and it turned out to live in two layers. The global transform was affine, and an affine preserves parallel lines — but photograph a board at an angle and its parallel edges converge toward a vanishing point, so no affine can undo that. I switched to a homography with RANSAC, which models perspective projection and rejects bad feature matches. That fixed the global case and still left 15–20 pixels, which told me the remaining error was somewhere else.

That turned out to be physical. PCBs go through a reflow oven, and the heat warps them non-uniformly — the center bows differently than the edges. A single global transform structurally cannot correct a local bow. So the bottleneck was that the prototype needed a third, non-rigid stage it didn't have. I added a Thin Plate Spline warp on top of the homography. TPS fits a smooth warp through control points, which I selected by template-matching known features — board corners, fiducials, component pads — and I rejected any match below a similarity threshold, because one bad control point pulls the whole warp field the wrong way.

I made the fix measurable instead of trusting the overlay. On 20 board images with ground-truth markers, the average offset after alignment was 2.1 pixels with a 0.8 standard deviation, and I had the manufacturing engineers overlay the aligned photo on the layout to spot-check it. Average misalignment dropped from 15–20 pixels to 2–3, and comparison accuracy went from roughly 85% to over 98% on moderately warped boards. The prototype could then handle photos up to 30 degrees off-perpendicular and up to 3mm of warp.

Two honest limits: very severe warping above about 5mm still defeats it, and I'd have started simpler — I spent time on full SURF feature matching before finding that board-outline detection was enough for most boards. The lesson I keep is to find which layer the error lives in before changing anything.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: What exactly was the bottleneck?**
**A:** The prototype aligned on flat, clean test images and produced 15–20 pixels of misalignment on real photos. With a 10-pixel comparison threshold, that made every mismatch flag unreliable. The tool couldn't be trusted until alignment was inside about three pixels.

**Q: How did you diagnose it instead of just trying transforms?**
**A:** I isolated which layer the error lived in. First layer: the global transform — affine can't represent converging parallels, so I moved to a homography. That removed the global error but left 15–20 pixels, which ruled out the transform model and pointed at something local. The second layer was physical board warping from the reflow oven — non-uniform, so no global transform can fix it. That's what made a non-rigid stage the answer rather than a different global one.

**Q: Why Thin Plate Splines and not another non-rigid method?**
**A:** TPS minimizes bending energy, is deterministic given the control points with no iterative tuning, and needs no physical material parameters like stiffness or Poisson's ratio, which don't have a clear meaning for a PCB. Elastic deformation needs those constants; B-splines need a control-grid resolution, which is another hyperparameter. TPS was the method with the fewest unsupported knobs.

**Q: How did you know the unblock actually worked and wasn't just a nicer-looking overlay?**
**A:** Two ways. Quantitatively, 20 board images with ground-truth markers: average offset 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8. Qualitatively, the engineers overlaid the aligned photo on the layout and confirmed pads lined up — that caught cases where the number looked fine but the warp was subtly wrong. And the downstream comparison accuracy moved from roughly 85% to over 98%.

**Q: When does this still fail?**
**A:** Very severely warped boards — above about 5mm — still fail, because control points can't capture extreme deformation; that would need a physically based model, and it's rare in production. Steep photo angles past about 45 degrees break feature matching outright; the practical limit is about 30–40 degrees, which covers most real manufacturing photos.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Start simpler and add complexity incrementally. I spent time implementing full SURF feature matching before discovering that simple board-outline contour detection was sufficient for most boards. Feature matching is more general, but it's also more computationally expensive and harder to debug. And I'd question the assumption that the warp was the final answer — severely warped boards still defeat it.
