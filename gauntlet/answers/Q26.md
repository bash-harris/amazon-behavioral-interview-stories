## Answer (core, ~450 words)

The deadline I'd point to is an eight-week window I had to deliver the entire PCB inspection suite — three separate tools — on my own.

The manufacturing team needed three verification steps covered: Schematic vs EBOM, which checks whether the bill of materials matches the schematic; PCB vs Layout, which checks whether the physical board matches its design; and Good Board vs New Board, which checks a newly made board against a known-good reference. Each had different inputs, different algorithms, and different failure modes. The requirement was to deliver all three within eight weeks, and the information I had at the start was incomplete — I'd never parsed Gerber files, and I hadn't used OpenCV.js or TensorFlow.js.

What let me hit the date was deciding what mattered first and bounding each tool instead of trying to design one perfect unified engine upfront. I sequenced by dependency and impact. Schematic vs EBOM went first because it was the highest-priority pain point and had no dependencies — I shipped the BOM parser with its edge-case handling, debug endpoint, and reconciliation dashboard in the first two weeks. PCB vs Layout went second, because that's where the steepest unknown lived: I had to learn OpenCV.js and build the CAD alignment pipeline. Good Board vs New Board went third, because by then it could stand on the infrastructure the first two tools had paid for; that one was the most algorithmically complex, adding the multi-signal comparison pipeline and the GPT-4o component analysis. Shipping each as a complete, usable slice is what kept the deadline real — a half-built unified engine would have delivered nothing at week eight.

I also kept the UX identical across all three tools — same drag-and-drop, same color scheme, same export — so engineers learned the workflow once and I wasn't rebuilding the interaction layer three times.

The biggest threat to the deadline was the CAD alignment pipeline, because Gerber parsing was the genuine unknown. I attacked that risk early rather than late: instead of writing a Gerber parser from scratch — the specification is 200-plus pages — I went looking for an existing open-source parser, found `gerber-parser`, and integrated it. That decision saved two to three weeks, and it's the Bias for Action call I'd defend most: I didn't need to prove I could write a Gerber parser, I needed the tools shipped.

All three tools were delivered within the eight weeks. They were adopted by 15 manufacturing engineers across two sites, and the combined time savings was about 20 hours a week across the team.

What I'd do differently is the same lesson I learned on the parser: get the real artifacts — the Gerber files and sample BOMs — in front of me before I estimate, because my early risk read was guesswork until I saw the actual format. And if I had to do it in four weeks, I'd ship the two highest-impact tools and defer PCB vs Layout, which would have delivered most of the value in half the time.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Eight weeks for three tools on your own — was the deadline aggressive or padded?**
**A:** Aggressive relative to the unknown. I'd never parsed Gerber files, and I had to learn two browser libraries from scratch. The reason it landed at eight weeks is the sequencing plus reusing an existing Gerber parser — the plan assumed I'd write that parser, which is what made the timeline look impossible.

**Q: How did you decide the order?**
**A:** Dependency and impact. BOM verification had no dependencies and was the top pain point, so it went first. PCB vs Layout needed OpenCV.js and CAD parsing, which is where the learning curve was. Good Board needed both libraries and could build on the infrastructure the first two produced.

**Q: What would you have cut if you were going to miss?**
**A:** PCB vs Layout. It was the most complex and least frequently used. Shipping the other two would have delivered most of the value in roughly half the time, and I'd have phased the third.

**Q: Tell me about a moment the deadline was actually at risk.**
**A:** Gerber parsing was the risk from day one. I didn't defer it to the end — I went looking for an open-source parser early, found `gerber-parser`, and integrated it. Waiting until week six to discover I couldn't parse the format would have been the failure mode.

**Q: How did you know the tools were actually done and not just demo-ready?**
**A:** Adoption and outcome are the evidence: 15 engineers across two sites used them, and the combined time savings was about 20 hours a week. A demo that nobody adopts isn't delivery.

**Q: What did you learn about estimating?**
**A:** Get the real artifacts first. My early risk read was guesswork until I saw an actual Gerber file and real BOMs. That's why I now try to collect diverse samples from the floor before committing to a plan.
