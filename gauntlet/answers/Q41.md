## Answer (core, ~400 words)

I want to be straight with you: I don't have a documented time someone handed me one job and I deliberately did a different one that was outside my role. On this project I was the sole builder and architect of the inspection suite, so the charter was already wide, and the closest adjacent example — the training-data collector — is recorded as my responsibility, not as something outside my job. So let me give you the framework I actually use instead of inventing a story.

It has three steps, and each is visible in what I did build.

**One: separate the role from the outcome.** My job was to build inspection tools. The outcome was engineers who could verify a board and trust the result. When a gap sits between my task and that outcome, I treat the gap as mine to raise. That instinct shows up twice in the record. The defect model needed a labeled dataset that didn't exist, so I built the labeling tool to unblock it rather than reporting a blocker (STORY 9). And after the BOM reconciliation engine shipped, the engineers didn't trust its verdicts, so I built the debug view and reconciliation dashboard to make the reasoning verifiable — adoption work, not the coding task (STORY 11).

**Two: check whether I'm closest to it, and whether it's reversible.** If I'm closest and the cost of a wrong attempt is bounded, I take it. If I'm not closest, or the cost is large, I flag it to whoever owns it and hand over what I've learned instead of quietly absorbing it. When I moved from Python to browser-only computer vision, I spent one day on a minimal prototype — load two images, run one operation — specifically to make the unknown cheap before committing weeks to it (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 5).

**Three: make the scope visible and close the loop.** I say what I'm taking on and why, rather than expanding quietly and surprising people. The debug endpoint exists so a user can see exactly which rule fired instead of taking my word for it (STORY 11); the standalone build made the deployment constraint visible to everyone, and I distributed it by email and USB so it reached the people who needed it (STORY 4).

The honest boundary: I don't have a documented "that's not my job" moment, so I won't claim one. What I can defend is the pattern — I default to owning the unowned dependency that stands between the team and the outcome, provided I can bound the risk and keep it in the open.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Wasn't the training-data collector outside your role?**
**A:** No, and I won't claim it was. STORY 9's own Task line names building the labeling tool as my responsibility. I built it because the model was useless without labels and I was closest to the problem — that's ownership of a dependency, but it isn't "outside my job description," so I keep it in the framework as evidence of the instinct, not as the answer to this question.

**Q: So give me a concrete decision where you had to judge whether work was yours.**
**A:** The transparency layer. The assigned deliverable was the reconciliation engine; the engine worked. What I judged to be mine was whether anyone would trust it — engineers were falling back to manual checks because the tool showed a verdict and hid the reasoning. That was an adoption gap, not a feature in the brief, so I built the debug view and dashboard. I'd call that ownership of the outcome, but I'd still frame it as scope I raised, not scope someone assigned me.

**Q: How do you avoid stepping on someone else's toes or duplicating work?**
**A:** Step two. I ask whether I'm actually closest to the problem. If I am, I take it and say so. If someone else owns it, I give them what I've learned and offer to help rather than doing it behind them. Quietly absorbing work someone else owns is how you get two half-solutions and a confused team.

**Q: What if it's genuinely someone else's job and they're not doing it?**
**A:** Then I escalate with a specific proposal and a timeframe, not a complaint — here's the gap, here's the impact, here's what I can do and by when, who should own it. If it's blocking the users and I have a real path, I don't let the org chart stall them while the escalation resolves.

**Q: Where do you draw the line and say no?**
**A:** When it's not bounded and I'm not the right person. If a wrong attempt is expensive and irreversible, or the work needs expertise I don't have, taking it quietly is worse than flagging it. The reversibility test is what keeps "never say that's not my job" from turning into overreach.

**Q: How is this different from just doing your job?**
**A:** The distinction is task versus outcome. A role is defined by tasks; ownership is defined by the outcome and by whether the unowned dependency sitting between the team and that outcome gets picked up. I try to be honest about which of my examples are assigned tasks and which are scope I raised — and on this project, the record doesn't contain a clean out-of-role event, which is why this answer is a framework.
