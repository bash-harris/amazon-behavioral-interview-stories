# Q11 — tell me about a time you dove deep and optimized something

**Mapped LP(s):** Dive Deep (primary); touches Invent and Simplify and Deliver Results.

## Answer (core, ~470 words)

I optimized a model by refusing to trust a number that looked great on the surface. I built the defect classifier for the PCB inspection suite — take a cropped image of a component and classify it as good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing. I had ML theory but I had never trained a production model from scratch, and I was doing it while the inspection tools were already in use.

The first training run was the honest interesting failure: 99% training accuracy, 72% validation accuracy. That gap is a model memorizing, not learning. If I'd stopped at the training number, I'd have shipped something that looked excellent and failed on the floor.

So I went after the cause instead of the symptom. I had fine-tuned by letting gradients flow into the whole pre-trained network. The problem is catastrophic forgetting: the classification head starts with random weights, so early in training it produces large, noisy gradients that overwrite the general features — edges, textures, shapes — that ImageNet pre-training had already learned. I was destroying the thing I was trying to reuse.

The fix was a two-phase progressive-unfreezing strategy. Phase one: freeze the entire EfficientNetV2S base and train only the classification head for 10 epochs at a learning rate of 0.001, so the head stabilizes and the gradients are controlled before anything touches the pre-trained layers. Phase two: unfreeze the last 30 layers and continue for 20 epochs at 0.0001 — a 10x lower rate — so the base adapts to PCB textures without being rewritten. I did the data work too: CLAHE preprocessing to normalize the very different lighting on the floor — fluorescent, LED, shadowed — so the model learned component features instead of illumination, and I weighted the defective class higher because "good" outnumbered defects about 5:1.

What made it a dive rather than a guess was the auditing. I tracked every run in Weights & Biases so I could compare configurations objectively instead of relying on memory, and I tested the one assumption that mattered: how many layers to unfreeze. Unfreezing the last 20 underfit — the frozen early layers couldn't produce PCB-specific features. Unfreezing 50 overfit on the small dataset. Thirty was the measured sweet spot. I also used early stopping with a patience of five epochs, so training stopped when validation loss flattened.

The result: validation accuracy went from 72% to 94% on a held-out set of 500 component images, with a training-to-validation gap of only 3% — so it was genuinely generalizing, not memorizing. Inference was 45ms per image on CPU, under the 100ms target, with recall of 92% for wrong-value, 96% for wrong-orientation, and 98% for missing components. The pipeline retrained on new data in about 30 minutes on a single GPU, so new defect types didn't stall the inspection workflow.

What I'd change: I assumed more unfrozen layers would always help, and 50 proved that wrong. I'd also use a learning-rate schedule like cosine annealing instead of a fixed rate in phase two, and I'd collect more than ~2,000 labeled images — more data would improve generalization further.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Walk me through catastrophic forgetting. Why does the order of the phases matter?**
**A:** Early layers learn general features — edges, textures, shapes — and later layers compose them into complex ones. On ImageNet the whole network has useful weights. If you unfreeze everything immediately, the randomly initialized head produces large, noisy gradients that shift the pre-trained layers and destroy those general features; the model then memorizes the new data. Phase one stabilizes the head with the base frozen, so when you unfreeze the base the gradients are smaller and consistent. Skipping phase one is exactly what produced 99% training / 72% validation.

**Q: Why unfreeze exactly 30 layers, and how do you know it wasn't luck?**
**A:** I tested 20, 30, and 50 and compared validation accuracy in W&B. Twenty underfit — the frozen early layers couldn't yield domain-specific features. Fifty overfit on ~2,000 images. Thirty was the measured sweet spot: enough trainable parameters to learn PCB-specific patterns while the frozen layers preserve general features. The train/val gap of 3% at the end is evidence it wasn't luck.

**Q: Why 0.001 for the head and 0.0001 for the unfrozen base?**
**A:** The head starts random and needs to move quickly, so phase one uses the higher rate. The base already has good weights and only needs fine adjustment, so phase two uses a 10x lower rate to avoid large updates. That 10x ratio is the conventional starting point; I verified it by trying 0.0005 and 0.0002 for the base and keeping 0.0001, which gave the best validation accuracy.

**Q: What is CLAHE and why did it matter here?**
**A:** Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization divides the image into tiles and equalizes each tile independently with a contrast clip limit, so it improves local contrast without blowing out bright regions or amplifying noise. Standard equalization operates on the whole image and can't handle mixed lighting. Without it, the model learned to classify lighting rather than component features, because the factory photos came from fluorescent lights, LED panels, and shadowed setups.

**Q: How did you handle class imbalance?**
**A:** "Good" components outnumbered defects roughly 5:1, so a model could score 83% by always predicting "good." I weighted defective examples 5x in the loss and used precision-recall rather than raw accuracy as the primary metric. The per-class recalls — 92% wrong-value, 96% wrong-orientation, 98% missing — are what I reported.

**Q: Your code documentation lists different training hyperparameters. Which is right?**
**A:** I'm citing the story-bank telling of the training run — 10 then 20 epochs, 0.001 then 0.0001, last 30 layers — and `CODEBASE_DOCUMENTATION.md` records a differently-specified set (15 epochs at 1e-4, last 50 layers at 1e-6). Rather than blend two conflicting tellings, I've used the STORY 14/STORY 3 telling throughout and flagged the discrepancy here.
