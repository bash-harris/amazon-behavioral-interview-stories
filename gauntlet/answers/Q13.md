# Q13 — tell me about a time you compromised quality to deliver quickly

**Mapped LP(s):** Bias for Action (primary); Deliver Results; Insist on the Highest Standards.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the first working prototype (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 8, "Bias for Action — First Working Prototype"). This is a deliberate speed-vs-quality trade: a five-day monolithic prototype with documented limits, repaid to production-ready in two weeks. Angle differs from Q10, which used the same prototype for influence, not for the quality trade-off. No conflicting telling is blended; the 4000+ line / 21-module refactor figure in `AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 7 is not used. The prototype is described consistently as having **no export** — the Good Board tool's export fallback (STORY 1) is not attributed to it.

## Answer (core, ~430 words)

Honestly, yes — and I'll give you the real one, not a story where I never compromise quality. The manufacturing engineers needed a PCB comparison tool, and the request had sat on the backlog for six months because it was "too complex to scope properly." I decided the fastest way to learn what they actually needed was to put something working in front of them, so I built a first prototype in five days. It was a single monolithic HTML file with inline JavaScript — no build system, no modules, no tests. It loaded two images, ran a basic pixel-level comparison, and showed the result. It worked correctly under ideal conditions and was inaccurate under real lighting and angles, and the UX was rough. I knew all of that when I shipped it.

Let me be precise about the compromise. The quality I cut was "works under real manufacturing conditions." The quality I did not cut was correctness under the conditions it claimed to support. I wrote the limitation down instead of hiding it, and I defined "bad enough to ship" as inaccurate, not broken. That's the line: a limitation I can name, not a defect I can't.

Then I contained it. I showed the prototype to the engineering lead on day five, stated the known limits, and let the team react. They found three concrete problems within an hour — pixel comparison failed under illumination changes, there was no image alignment, and there was no export. Those became the roadmap, and I prioritized by impact: alignment first, illumination normalization second, export third.

And I closed it. The tool went from ugly prototype to production-ready in two weeks — five days to the prototype plus nine days of refinement — because the feedback came from real users instead of my guesses. Then I refactored the monolith into modules so the next feature wouldn't be a rewrite.

So five days of an imperfect prototype generated more useful direction than six months of requirements gathering, and the quality debt was repaid on a schedule rather than left open. The reason I'd defend the call is not that speed beats quality — it's that a named, bounded cut with a repayment plan is a different thing from an unmeasured one.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Isn't shipping an inaccurate tool just a quality cut you should be embarrassed by?**
**A:** I'm not embarrassed by it, but I'm precise about it. The prototype was correct under the conditions it claimed to support; the cut was that it didn't yet support real lighting and angles. That's a documented limitation, not a hidden defect. Shipping a named limitation to learn what to build next is a different act from shipping something broken and hoping.

**Q: How do you decide when an ugly build is acceptable to ship?**
**A:** I used three criteria: (1) it works correctly under expected conditions, (2) the limitations are documented, and (3) the feedback from shipping is worth more than the cost of the limitations. All three were true here. If any one had been false, I'd have kept it internal.

**Q: What if the prototype had been so rough the team dismissed it?**
**A:** I should be straight about that — there wasn't an export fallback to fall back on, because export was one of the three gaps they found, so the prototype couldn't hand them a saved file. The only fallback was the manual route they already used, comparing the two board images by eye, and the prototype at least put that comparison on screen. The risk I was managing wasn't adoption; it was building the wrong features, which is exactly what shipping early removed.

**Q: Have you ever compromised quality without a good reason?**
**A:** Yes, and the honest example is a threshold I tuned by watching the UI until false positives stopped annoying me. Every threshold moved in the loosening direction — a resistor class from 0.40 to 0.55, an IC class from 0.25 to 0.40 — which trades recall for precision. In PCB QA that's backwards: a missed defect can ship, while a false alarm costs an operator about thirty seconds. There was no held-out set, so I didn't know the cost. The fix I'd run is to label ~200 components, plot a precision-recall curve, and pick the operating point by minimizing the weighted false-negative and false-positive costs the process engineers give me. That one isn't fully repaid yet, and I say so.

**Q: You said speed was right — what was the actual downside of the trade?**
**A:** The obvious one is that the first users hit rough edges in their own environment instead of mine. The subtler one is debt that outlives a deadline. I shipped a second tool by copying the first because I needed it working that week; that bought the deadline and cost about six months of divergence, including two different anomaly formulas between the tools. The lesson isn't "don't copy" — it's set a deadline on the copy, so the shortcut gets collapsed instead of compounding.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** State the prototype's known limitations even more sharply before the demo, so the team's hour went to next steps rather than rediscovering what I already knew. And for the threshold tuning, I'd define the measurement before touching the knob.
