## Answer (core, ~470 words)

The complex problem was making board-to-board comparison actually usable on a manufacturing floor, and the valuable information wasn't in the images — it was in how the engineers worked.

The obvious approach is pixel or template matching: compare the board against a reference. That fails in the real world, because the engineers photograph boards under different lighting, at slight angles, and at varying distances, and they do it to answer a human question — "is this component right?" — not to produce a difference number. If I built a mathematically clean comparator that didn't fit their workflow, it wouldn't get used. I could see that the problem had two layers: robustness to illumination, rotation, and scale, and compatibility with how a person actually verifies a board.

So I went to the people who did the job every day. Before writing any code, I sat in on three of their screen-sharing sessions. I watched them toggle between a good-board image and a new-board image side by side, and — the detail that mattered most — I saw them using Zoom's annotation tool to circle areas of concern by hand. That told me what they actually needed: visual highlighting of *where* and *which* region, not a single difference score. A score tells you something is wrong; a circled region tells you what to look at next.

I used that in two ways. First, the interaction: I built the tool to replicate their workflow exactly — drag-and-drop, one click, and a visual diff with color-coded regions, so the output replaced their manual circling rather than adding a step. Second, the comparison itself. Instead of one signal I combined four: Edge XOR for structural differences (~8 ms), a histogram comparison for illumination and color (~5 ms), SSIM for structural similarity (~15 ms), and Hu Moments for shape differences that are invariant to rotation and scale (~12 ms). I combined them with confidence weighting so the engineer could see which signal flagged a region, not just a verdict. The ensemble ran in about 40 ms and hit 92% accuracy in testing, against a single pixel-level method that was weaker and far less robust.

The result was adoption, not just accuracy. Comparison time went from 30–60 minutes per board to under two minutes, and the color-coded diff output became the shared reference the engineers used in their discussions — it replaced the Zoom annotation workflow. Browser-based delivery mattered too: no install, no IT ticket, so they could start using it immediately.

What I learned changed how I approach detail. Diving deep isn't only understanding the algorithm; it's understanding the person on the other end of it. I had been ready to treat this as an image-processing problem when the binding constraint was the verification workflow. My honest reflection is that I would observe even more systematically up front — three sessions was enough to find the annotation behavior, but a structured study of their whole process would have surfaced the export need sooner.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Who exactly did you talk to, and why them?**
**A:** The manufacturing engineers who did the board verification by hand. I talked with them directly, but the more useful input was watching them work — I sat in on three of their screen-sharing sessions before writing any code. They were the ones doing the task, so they held the answer to what "a good comparison" looked like.

**Q: Why couldn't you work this out from the images or the algorithm alone?**
**A:** The images told me that two boards differed. They didn't tell me which differences the engineers cared about or how they wanted to consume the answer. Watching them toggle images and circle regions with Zoom's annotation told me they needed visual highlighting, not a difference score — that's a human-workflow fact, not an image fact.

**Q: How did you validate the four-signal approach rather than just trusting it?**
**A:** I started with equal weights and ran the pipeline on 50 test boards, analyzing which signals correlated with actual defects. Edge XOR was most correlated (0.35 weight), then SSIM (0.30), Histogram (0.20), and Hu Moments (0.15). I then validated those weights on 50 additional boards to avoid overfitting. No single signal was as good as the combination.

**Q: Why four signals instead of one, or a custom-trained model?**
**A:** Each kept signal catches a class the others miss: Edge XOR for structure, histogram for illumination/color, SSIM for structural similarity, Hu Moments for shape under rotation and scale. A "good enough" ensemble outperformed any single "perfect" method under real-world conditions. A custom model would have needed labeled data I didn't have, and the ensemble is more inspectable — the engineer can see which signal fired.

**Q: How did you make sure the tool fit their workflow instead of the other way around?**
**A:** I matched what they were already doing: drag-and-drop, single-click comparison, and color-coded diff regions that replaced the manual annotation. I also kept it browser-based so there was no install or IT approval. Workflow compatibility turned out to matter more than feature completeness — if it required them to change their process, adoption would have been slower.

**Q: What did this teach you about diving deep?**
**A:** That detail isn't only in the technical artifact. Some of it lives in the person doing the job, and the fastest way to it is to watch them work rather than assume. I would structure that observation more systematically next time — three sessions found the key behavior, but a fuller study of their process would have surfaced other needs sooner.
