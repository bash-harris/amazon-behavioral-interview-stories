## Answer (core, ~440 words)

I want to be straight with you first: the record I have doesn't include a disagreement with a manager that I can stand behind, and I'm not going to invent one. Have Backbone is honestly the thinnest part of my story bank. What the record does show is the pattern I'd bring into that situation, so I'll give you that, labeled as a framework rather than claimed as an event.

The documented case closest to a disagreement is the pin-count rule in the BOM reconciliation tool. The tool compared the pin count in the BOM against the number of pads in the layout, literally, and called any difference a mismatch. I didn't agree with that measurement, so I pushed back with evidence, not opinion: I pulled 20 BOMs with pin-count mismatches and sorted them into three buckets. Footprint-larger-than-component was almost always a legitimate package variation; footprint-smaller was a genuine mismatch, because the part physically can't fit; exact match was fine. That turned a preference into a finding the team could act on. The only difference between that and a manager disagreement is the counterpart. The method is the same.

So if a manager hands me a direction I disagree with, this is the framework:

1. **Disagree privately, once, with data.** Make the counter-argument directly to the manager, not in a group thread and not sideways to the team. Anchor it to specific data and a specific risk: customer impact, cost, or a correctness gap. "I think this is wrong" isn't a disagreement; "here is the board population where this rule fails and what it costs us" is.

2. **Separate the decision from the person.** The goal is the best outcome for the customer and the business, not winning. If I'm missing context the manager has, I ask for it and update. That's what happened with the variant references: the manufacturing engineer's floor knowledge corrected my parser, and I changed it, because the data was better than my assumption.

3. **Make the call explicit.** If the manager still decides against me, I ask what evidence would change their mind, so the disagreement becomes a documented trigger rather than a feeling.

4. **Commit fully.** Once the decision is final, I implement it as if I had made it, and I don't relitigate it in side conversations. If executing it surfaces a new material fact that wasn't visible when the call was made, I raise that once, with evidence, and then respect the call. Half-committing is the worst outcome.

That's the honest answer. I have the data-first reflex and the commit discipline demonstrated in the record; I have not had the documented manager blow-up, and I won't claim one.
