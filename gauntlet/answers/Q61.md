# Q61 — a time when you had a conflict with a team-mate or someone

**Mapped LP(s):** Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit (primary); Earn Trust (touch).

**Story used:** `AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 1 ("The BOM Parser That Refused to Break") — the `R1||R2` variant-semantics disagreement with the manufacturing engineer, framed as a stakeholder disagreement, plus a labeled framework for the peer case. ## Answer (core, ~440 words)

I'll be straight about the shape of this: I built the PCB inspection suite solo, so there was no teammate on the other side of a conflict, and I won't invent one. Have Backbone is honestly the thinnest LP in my record. What I can give you is the one documented disagreement I actually had — with a manufacturing engineer, a stakeholder, not a teammate — and then the framework I'd use for a peer, clearly labeled as a framework.

The disagreement was about data semantics. My BOM parser extracted component references with a simple pattern and treated anything shaped like `R1||R2` or `R1/R2` as invalid, so it silently dropped those entries. A manufacturing engineer told me they weren't malformed — they mean the same physical position can accept either part depending on the board variant. I pushed back first, on principle: I didn't want to normalize data I didn't understand, because a guess inside a reconciliation tool becomes a silent false match. He explained the manufacturing intent, and we went to the actual board files together. He was right; my parser was losing real references. I rebuilt the variant splitter to normalize both separators and treat either reference as a valid match, and I committed to it fully — I didn't keep a private fallback to my original handling.

The backbone there was two-sided. I held firm on the principle long enough to get a real explanation instead of just complying: I don't normalize without understanding the semantics. But when the domain data contradicted my assumption, I changed the code rather than defend it. The customer in that case was the engineer trying to verify boards correctly, and that mattered more than my parser looking tidy.

For a peer conflict, which I haven't had, this is the framework I'd bring:

1. **Argue the finding, not the person.** Get the shared goal explicit first — usually the customer outcome, not who's right.
2. **Bring evidence, not preference.** "I think this is wrong" isn't a disagreement; a failing case with a number is. The variant episode is exactly that lesson.
3. **Separate reversible calls from values calls.** On a reversible business call, disagree once, directly and privately, then commit fully. On a safety, compliance, or trust line, escalate with documentation rather than pretend to commit.
4. **Commit visibly.** Once the call is made, implement it as if I'd made it, and don't relitigate it in side channels.

Honest answer: I have the data-first reflex and the commit discipline in my record; I have not had the documented teammate conflict, and I won't claim one.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: The question asks about a team-mate — was there really no teammate?**
**A:** No. I was the only engineer on the suite. The closest real counterpart was the manufacturing engineer, so I've answered with the actual disagreement dynamic — a stakeholder — rather than inventing a peer. The teammate half is a labeled framework, not a scene.

**Q: You say you pushed back, then changed. Was that backbone or just backing down?**
**A:** Backbone was holding the principle long enough to make him show me the manufacturing intent. Backing down would have been complying to avoid friction before I understood the semantics. I did neither — I asked for the evidence, he provided it, and the evidence beat my assumption. I'd do the same either way.

**Q: How do you disagree with someone senior or a domain expert without being the person who's always arguing?**
**A:** Once, privately, anchored to data and a customer or correctness risk, and then I commit. I argue the finding, not the person. If I'm wrong, I say so and change the code — a disagreement you can lose gracefully is worth more than one you "win" and quietly ignore.

**Q: What if the disagreement had been a values issue, not a reversible business call?**
**A:** That's where commit isn't the whole answer. I'd raise it through the chain with documentation rather than pretend to commit, because disagree-and-commit is for reversible business calls, not for overriding a safety, compliance, or trust obligation. I haven't had to do that; I'd describe the principle rather than a story I don't have.

**Q: How would you handle a peer who wouldn't engage with the data?**
**A:** I'd make the disagreement cheap to test: agree on the specific case where our positions differ, then reproduce it. If they still won't look, that's a signal to raise it once with the shared goal and a written counter-argument, then let the decision be made and commit. Escalation is a last resort, not the first move.

**Q: What does your framework leave out that a real peer conflict would test?**
**A:** The interpersonal part — reading whether the other person feels heard, and repairing the relationship after a disagreement that got heated. I've handled the technical-disagreement mechanics repeatedly; I've had less practice on the emotional repair, and I'd rather say that than pretend the framework is complete.
