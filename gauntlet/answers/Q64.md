## Answer (core, ~455 words)

First, a boundary. The way this question is circulating, it arrives glued to someone else's setup — an internship, an ETL pipeline being integrated with a legacy backend, a disagreement with a manager about how to do it. That is the poster's situation, not mine. I did not do that internship and I am not going to adopt their scene as if it were my history. What I also won't do is invent a manager disagreement I never had. My record does not contain one. Have Backbone is honestly the thinnest part of my story bank, and the honest answer is a clearly labeled framework built from the data-first behavior I do have on record.

The documented event closest to a disagreement is in the alignment work. I had built the PCB-to-layout alignment on an affine transform, because affine was the simple, standard approach and it worked on flat boards photographed straight on. The evidence said otherwise: at a 30-degree camera angle the affine-aligned image was 15 pixels off at the far edge of the board. I did not argue from preference. I ran the two transforms against a rectangular board at 30 degrees and measured them side by side — homography came in at 2 pixels. The test settled it and I switched. The same reflex shows in the BOM work, where I challenged a literal pin-count rule by pulling 20 real BOMs and sorting every mismatch into three buckets before I said a word.

So if a manager hands me a direction I disagree with, this is the framework I would run:

1. **Try to disconfirm myself first.** Before I take a disagreement to a manager, I check whether I'm the one missing context — the way the manufacturing engineer's floor knowledge corrected my variant handling and I changed the parser instead of defending it. Half of disagreements should die here, and correctly.

2. **Bring the smallest decisive evidence, not a volume.** One controlled test or a categorized sample beats an opinion in a meeting. The affine-versus-homography experiment is the model: pick the case that discriminates, measure it, show it.

3. **Disagree once, in the right channel, privately.** Directly to the manager — not in a group thread and not sideways to the team. Framed as risk to the customer or the business, not as who is right.

4. **If overruled, commit fully and convert the disagreement into a trigger.** I ask what evidence would change the decision, then implement it as if I had chosen it, with no side-channel relitigating. On the pin-count rule I kept the signal in and made it discriminating rather than half-implementing it to prove a point.

5. **Escalate only on values.** Disagree-and-commit is for reversible business calls, not for compliance, safety, or a broken customer promise. That is the one line where committing is not the whole answer. I have not had to do that, so I describe the principle, not a scene.

That is the honest version: the data reflex and the commit discipline are documented; the manager half is a framework, and I am labeling it as one.
