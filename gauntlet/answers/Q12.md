## Answer (core, ~500 words)

**The mechanism (PREPARED FRAMEWORK — how I will communicate a deadline risk; not a recorded event).**

I flag a deadline risk as soon as I can name a specific blocker and its consequence — not when the date passes. The message has five parts, in order: the blocker in one sentence; the impact (what slips and by roughly how much, or what it costs to avoid); two or three options with their trade-offs; my recommendation; and the date by which I'll know more. Then I commit to whatever is decided and keep status honest even when the news is uncomfortable. I would rather raise it early and be wrong than raise it late and be right. I flag on a named blocker and its consequence, not on vague uncertainty.

**Documented risk events (DOCUMENTED — the events are real; the conversation around them is not recorded).**

Deployment is the clearest. The suite originally ran on a Flask backend for BOM parsing and image processing, and the factory-floor machines had restricted network access — no internet, no software installs, limited IT. The plan required installing Python, managing dependencies, and configuring the network on each machine. That is a named blocker with a consequence: every deployment becomes a support ticket the manufacturing team can't absorb, which puts the rollout date at risk. The documented resolution was the standalone build — OpenCV.js about 8MB, TensorFlow.js about 3MB, PDF.js about 1MB, XLSX.js about 500KB, roughly 12.5MB of libraries embedded into one file of about 25MB that loads in about 5 seconds on a factory machine versus about 2 seconds on the server. Engineers double-click one file: no Python, no network, no IT ticket. Catching that blocker early and taking the standalone path is what turned the risk into something that shipped.

The size constraint is the same move in miniature: the about 25MB artifact collides with the about 20MB attachment limit some email systems use, and I documented that gzip brings it to about 15MB. That is a trade-off named up front — distribution versus file size — not found after the fact.

The training pipeline is the documented rework case. My first run hit 99% training accuracy but only 72% validation accuracy — overfitting, not shippable. I rebuilt it with two-phase progressive unfreezing — freeze the base, then unfreeze the last 30 layers at a lower learning rate — and reached 94% validation accuracy at 45ms inference, with retraining around 30 minutes. The first approach missed the bar; the framework move is to name the rework and its cost early instead of absorbing it silently and missing the date.

Two further documented schedule facts. The tools had to be browser-based because engineers couldn't install desktop software under IT restrictions — a constraint that eliminated a whole approach. And the biggest risk to an 8-week delivery was CAD/Gerber parsing; integrating an existing open-source parser instead of writing one saved 2–3 weeks.

The closest documented deadline trade-off is the copied fork. I shipped the second tool by copying the first because I needed it working that week; the documented cost is six months of divergence, including two different anomaly formulas between the two tools. That is the conversation I'd want early: here is the date, here is the debt the shortcut buys, here is my recommendation and its cost.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Has a date actually slipped, or is this hypothetical?**
**A:** The docs contain no recorded missed deadline. So the mechanism is a framework, and I have labeled it that way. What is documented is a set of real risks and trade-offs — the Python-install deployment blocker, the ~25MB/20MB size tension, the training rework, the fork deadline — and I use those to show how I reason about risk, not to claim a status meeting happened.

**Q: Who would you tell, and how early?**
**A:** (FRAMEWORK) As early as I can name a specific blocker and its consequence. I'd tell the people who own the outcome, state the blocker in one sentence, give the date by which I'll know more, and arrive with options rather than an open-ended problem. The deployment blocker is the documented example of naming it while scoping, before any date slipped.

**Q: What if the team insists on the original plan anyway?**
**A:** Then I commit to the decision and make it work, while documenting the trade-off we accepted so it is a conscious choice. On this project the constraint was hard — the factory machines could not install Python or reach the network — so the options were narrow and the standalone path was the one that cleared the blocker. The copied fork is the documented case where I took the shortcut and named its cost afterward.

**Q: How do you avoid crying wolf?**
**A:** (FRAMEWORK) I flag on a specific blocker and its consequence, not on mere uncertainty. A "maybe" stays on my own list until I can pair it with an option and a recommendation. If things change after I flag, I re-flag with a revised plan rather than going quiet.

**Q: Why not just compress the file instead of re-architecting?**
**A:** Compression alone wasn't enough — the file had to be a single artifact an engineer could double-click with no server. The documented reflection is that gzip could take ~25MB down to about 15MB, which matters because some email systems cap attachments around 20MB. Given the constraint, the single-file rearchitecture was the fix; compression is the stated follow-up.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Documented: compress embedded libraries from the start and set a deadline on the copy before the fork diverges. Framework: make the size/load trade-off explicit to the users earlier so they aren't surprised by a 25MB download.
