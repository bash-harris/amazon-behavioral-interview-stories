## Answer (core, ~470 words)

The expectation was a defect-detection model good enough for the manufacturing floor. That had a number attached: 90% accuracy, because the engineers were going to trust its output and a false negative could mean shipping a defective board. My first trained EfficientNetV2S came in at 85% on validation — under the bar, and easy to explain away as "close enough for a first pass."

I didn't take that. I treated 90% as the floor, not the goal, because the failure mode on the wrong side of it was a bad board leaving the line.

I started with the confusion matrix rather than the model architecture, because I wanted to know *how* it was wrong before changing anything. Two patterns stood out. It was flagging shadow edges as defects — false positives from illumination — and it was missing subtle component-level differences like wrong resistor values — false negatives. Different errors, different fixes.

That split the work into two phases. First, CLAHE preprocessing, which normalizes local contrast so the model stops keying on lighting; that cut false positives by 8%. Second, hard negative mining: I pulled the boards the model misclassified most often, corrected their labels, and folded them back into training; that lifted recall by 6%. I also tested learning-rate scheduling (cosine annealing), data augmentation, and focal loss for the rare defect classes.

The most useful result was the counterintuitive one. I had cranked augmentation up — random rotation to ±30 degrees and brightness jitter to ±50% — and accuracy *fell* to 80%. The reason is physical: PCB components have a specific orientation, so rotating a resistor 30 degrees changes what it looks like, and the model was learning from corrupted examples. I pulled rotation back to ±5 degrees and removed brightness jitter entirely, and accuracy climbed back to 88% on the way to the final number.

The final model hit 93% — above the 90% bar — with balanced precision and recall across the defect types, at inference under the 100ms budget. The two-phase approach (normalize the data, then mine the hard cases) became the standard pipeline for every later model iteration, so the improvement compounded instead of being a one-off.

Two honest notes. The training set was only 500 boards, which is why I stopped at 93% rather than chasing 95% — the marginal gain wasn't worth the overfitting risk on a set that small, so I documented the model's performance characteristics and moved on to deployment. And what I'd change is that I should have kept augmentation conservative from the start; I learned the PCB-orientation constraint by breaking the model, not by reasoning about it first.

The expectation was a working model. I held out for one that cleared the manufacturing threshold, and I refused to ship the 85% version just because it was good enough for a demo.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: What exactly was the expectation, and how did you exceed it?**
**A:** The expectation was a defect classifier usable on the floor; the stated requirement was 90% accuracy, and my first model was 85%. Exceeding it meant clearing the bar with margin — 93% — with balanced precision and recall, not an 89% that technically rounded to close enough.

**Q: Why wasn't 85% good enough?**
**A:** Because on a defect classifier the asymmetric error is a false negative — a bad board passes. I said plainly that I refused to ship a model with 85% accuracy just because it was good enough for a demo. A manufacturing engineer trusting the output is a different stakes level than a prototype.

**Q: How did you find the two error patterns?**
**A:** The confusion matrix. It showed the model was flagging illumination edges as defects and, separately, missing wrong-value and missing-component cases. I fixed the first with CLAHE preprocessing (about an 8% false-positive reduction) and the second with hard negative mining (about a 6% recall improvement) because they were different failure modes.

**Q: What was the most counterintuitive finding?**
**A:** That aggressive augmentation made the model worse. Random rotation to ±30 degrees and brightness jitter to ±50% dropped accuracy to 80%. PCB components have a defined orientation, so over-rotating them distorts the label. Reducing rotation to ±5 degrees and removing brightness jitter recovered accuracy to 88% on the way to 93%.

**Q: How did you decide when to stop at 93% instead of pushing for 95%?**
**A:** The training set was only 500 boards. At that size the risk of overfitting for the last couple of points outweighed the benefit, and a model that looks better on paper but generalizes worse is exactly the wrong trade for a manufacturing tool. I documented the performance characteristics and moved to deployment.

**Q: How is this different from the four-signal comparison story you use elsewhere?**
**A:** Different component, different metric. The four-signal work was the image-comparison pipeline measured in false positives and F1; this is the trained defect classifier measured in validation accuracy against a fixed 90% threshold. They live in the same suite but I keep the numbers separate.
