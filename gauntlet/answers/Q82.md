## Answer (core, ~430 words)

I'll be straight with you: I have never delegated a critical task to a junior engineer, and I don't have a documented case where a junior broke something and caused a delay. I built the PCB inspection suite solo — I was the sole builder and architect, with no direct reports and no teammate. Hire and Develop the Best is a manager leadership principle, and the rubric we're working from marks it out of scope for my level. So rather than invent a junior and a blow-up, let me give you the framework I'd actually run, drawn from things I did do.

Four moves, in order.

**One: delegate a slice with a checkable output, not a silent critical path.** My own instinct is to ship the smallest artifact that produces information — I built a five-day prototype and put it in front of users instead of polishing privately for two weeks, precisely because a small, reversible artifact buys feedback cheaply. Applied to a junior, I'd hand over a self-contained, gradable piece and define "done" as a specific, measurable artifact, so a gap shows up before the deadline, not at it.

**Two: put a checkpoint in before the deadline, measured against ground truth.** I don't grade by "it looks right." For alignment I measured twenty ground-truth images — average offset 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8 — and added the engineers' visual overlay as a second lens. For the standalone build I pushed fifteen BOM files and ten board images through both builds and required the reports to match exactly. A junior's work would get the same treatment: a measurable checkpoint early enough that a failure is still recoverable.

**Three: when it fails, own the outcome, not the blame.** Leaders are vocally self-critical. My record has me naming my own misses rather than hiding them: I assumed every factory machine ran a modern browser and shipped an IE11 compatibility check after being wrong; I let CLAHE stand in for controlled lighting; I shipped classifier thresholds I had tuned by feel with no held-out set. If a junior's failure reached the customer, I'd take the delay as mine in front of them and fix the mistake with them, not at them.

**Four: recover transparently, then develop.** When my prototype's three real issues landed, I prioritized by impact — alignment first, illumination second, export third — and went from prototype to production-ready in two weeks. I'd do the same to recover a junior's delay: cut scope to a named limit, never the correctness bar, and tell the customer early rather than at the deadline. Then the development step: give them the reasoning, not the answer — have them reproduce the failure and propose the fix, and review the fix against the evidence. That's the human version of the debug view I built so the manufacturing engineers could verify a decision without asking me.

The honest close: this is a framework because the record is a solo project, and the LP itself is above my level. I'd rather say that clearly than fabricate a junior engineer.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Have you ever managed or delegated to a junior engineer?**
**A:** No. I was the sole builder and architect of the inspection suite, with no reports and no junior teammates, and Hire and Develop the Best is a manager LP the rubric marks out of scope for my level. I won't manufacture a junior or a delay to fit the question.

**Q: Then why should I believe you could handle that situation?**
**A:** Because the underlying discipline is documented, even if the delegation isn't. I've delegated capability rather than people: the debug view was built so the manufacturing engineers could verify the tool's reasoning without me, and it worked — they found two edge cases in the first week. Setting a person up to succeed without you is the same job.

**Q: If a junior broke the critical path tomorrow, what's the first thing you'd do?**
**A:** Separate the failure from the person. Assess the blast radius, then own the miss to the customer within the day rather than let it surface at the deadline. Then decide re-do versus re-scope. That's the early-flag pattern I'd already use for my own slip.

**Q: How would you keep it from happening again?**
**A:** A gradable slice plus a checkpoint measured against ground truth. That's the reason I used a 20-image ground-truth set for alignment and a 15-BOM / 10-image parity harness for the standalone build — a metric catches the failure while there's still time to recover, instead of a status report at the end.

**Q: Would you fix the failure yourself or let the junior fix it?**
**A:** Recover the timeline first, then use it as the development episode: have them reproduce the failure and propose the fix, and review against the evidence. If the failure shows they needed a different kind of support or assignment, that's a development conversation. I haven't had that conversation, so I won't pretend to a scene.

**Q: Isn't giving feedback after a failure just blame in slow motion?**
**A:** Only if it's about the person. It's about the work: what the checkpoint missed, what the fix is, and what changes in how the task is structured next time. I've been on the receiving end of my own wrong assumptions — the IE11 miss and the uncalibrated thresholds — and the useful version names the mechanism, not the culprit.

**Q: What would you want a manager to know about you here?**
**A:** That I default to owning the outcome and making my work verifiable, and that I'll say "I don't have that experience" rather than claim it. The delegation record is genuinely absent; the ownership and verification habits are not.
