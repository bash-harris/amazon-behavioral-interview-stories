# Q14 — give me an example where you took a risk in a project and succeeded

**Mapped LP(s):** Bias for Action (primary); Are Right, A Lot; touches Think Big.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the alignment bet: adding a non-rigid Thin Plate Spline (TPS) warp on top of homography (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 2, "When Affine Alignment Wasn't Enough"). ## Answer (core, ~410 words)

The risk I took was betting that the alignment problem needed a third stage of math — a non-rigid warp — instead of the "good enough" global transform everyone reaches for. Let me set it up.

I was building the PCB-vs-layout comparison: align a photograph of a board to its CAD layout, then flag mismatches. I started with the conventional approach — feature matching into a homography, which corrects global perspective. On flat boards photographed straight-on it worked. On real factory-floor photos it didn't. Even after perspective correction the homography only got us to an average misalignment of 15–20 pixels across the board. Boards flex in the reflow oven, and the flex isn't uniform. A global transform can't correct that by definition.

The risk was whether adding a Thin Plate Spline warp on top would actually help, or whether I'd spend real time on new math — I'd never implemented TPS — and end up with something that corrected alignment on paper and broke on real photos. TPS fits a smooth warp through a set of control points, so one wrongly matched control point can pull the whole field the wrong way. Get that wrong and you ship a tool that looks precise and is silently worse.

Was that a gamble or a calculated risk? The downside was concrete: days spent on unfamiliar math, and a warp that could look precise and be silently wrong. What made me take it anyway — the physics were real and visible in the images; the requirement was concrete, the comparison used a 10-pixel mismatch threshold, so I needed 2–3 pixel accuracy for that threshold to mean anything; and the failure mode was measurable, not vague.

So I de-risked it before I trusted it. I ran homography first, so control-point matching happened on a roughly aligned image instead of from scratch. I selected control points by template matching known features — board corners, fiducials, component pads — and rejected any match where the template similarity fell below a threshold, since a bad control point would corrupt the warp. I validated on a test set of 20 board images with ground-truth markers — average offset 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8. And I pulled in the manufacturing engineers to overlay the aligned photo on the layout and visually verify that pads lined up. I didn't wait on their sign-off to proceed — the metric was my gate — but that check caught cases where a good number hid a subtly wrong warp.

It worked. Average misalignment dropped from 15–20 pixels to 2–3 pixels, and comparison accuracy went from roughly 85% to over 98% for boards with moderate warping. The pipeline handled photos up to 30 degrees off-perpendicular and up to 3mm of visible warp.

The lesson is that I'd assumed the global transform was the end state. The risk was admitting it wasn't and committing to the more complex method — after making the failure mode testable first.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Why not train a learned registration model instead?**
**A:** A learned approach like a U-Net that predicts a displacement field needs pairs of aligned and misaligned images with ground-truth correspondences. I didn't have that data, and collecting it would have been its own project. TPS is a classical method that works without training data. If I had thousands of labeled alignment examples, a learned approach might beat it under extreme conditions — that's a real alternative, not a hand-wave, and I'd revisit it.

**Q: Why TPS specifically, over elastic deformation or B-splines?**
**A:** Elastic deformation needs material properties — stiffness, Poisson's ratio — that don't have a clear physical meaning for a PCB. B-splines need a control grid resolution, which is another hyperparameter. TPS has a clean mathematical foundation — it minimizes bending energy — is deterministic given the control points, and OpenCV's implementation is mature. Those were the criteria I chose on.

**Q: How many control points, and what happens if one is wrong?**
**A:** TPS needs at least 4 points for a 2D warp, but with only 4 there's no redundancy to average out noise — I typically found 15–30 across the board. A wrong control point pulls the warp toward the wrong position, so I reject matches below the template-similarity threshold.

**Q: How did you know it worked and wasn't just a nicer-looking overlay?**
**A:** Two ways. Quantitatively, 20 board images with ground-truth markers: average offset 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8. Qualitatively, the manufacturing engineers overlaid the aligned photo on the layout and spot-checked that pads lined up — that caught cases where the metric looked fine but the alignment was subtly wrong.

**Q: When does this still fail?**
**A:** Very severely warped boards, above roughly 5mm, still fail — the control points can't capture extreme deformation, and a more physically based model would be needed. Very steep photo angles, past about 45 degrees, break feature matching outright; the practical limit is about 30–40 degrees, which covers most real manufacturing photos. I'd rather name the boundary than imply it's universal.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** I'd have started with the simpler approach and added complexity incrementally. I spent time implementing full SURF feature matching before discovering that board-outline detection — contour finding — was sufficient for most boards; feature matching is more general, but it's also more computationally expensive and harder to debug. And I'd have questioned my assumption that TPS was the final solution, since severely warped boards still defeat it and a more physically based model would be needed for those — instead of treating the warp as the end state.
