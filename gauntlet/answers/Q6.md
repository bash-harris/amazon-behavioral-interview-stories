# Q6 — a time when you needed to influence a peer who had a differing opinion about a shared goal

**Mapped LP(s):** Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit (primary); touches Earn Trust and Are Right, A Lot.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the manufacturing-engineer stakeholder on the BOM reconciliation tool (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 11 "Debug Endpoint for Manufacturing Transparency" + STORY 1 "The BOM Parser That Refused to Break"). The counterpart is a documented stakeholder group, not a peer — see the note in Fact provenance.

## Answer (core, ~390 words)

I'll be straight about the shape of this one. I built the PCB inspection suite solo — I was the only engineer on it — so there was no teammate on the other side of the table. The people whose opinion I had to move were the customers: the manufacturing engineers. So this is a stakeholder-influence story, not a peer-disagreement one, and I'd rather answer it accurately than reframe it as something it wasn't.

We shared one goal: boards verified correctly. We differed on two documented points. The first was a design call. I had decided that variant references like `R1||R2` were invalid, and my parser silently dropped them. A manufacturing engineer disagreed — he said those mean the same physical position can accept either part. He had the domain intent; I had a bad assumption. I didn't litigate it. I adopted his decision, rewrote the variant splitter to normalize `||` and `/`, and treated each reference as a valid match. Sometimes the backbone is knowing which disagreement isn't worth holding.

The second point was the one I had to move them on. The engineers didn't trust the reconciliation tool's output. When it flagged a mismatch they couldn't see the reasoning, so they re-verified by hand — which defeats an automation tool. Their position was, in effect, "we can't rely on this for production." My position was that the decisions were sound and the real gap was auditability. I didn't argue it abstractly. I made the counter-argument with evidence they could inspect: a `?debug=true` view exposing the raw BOM text, the parsed reference after each preprocessing step, the matched layout entry, the comparison result, and the exact rule that fired — plus a per-component decision tree and a dashboard of aggregate stats. If they thought a call was wrong, the view showed them where.

That changed their position. In the first week they used it to find two edge cases with unusual naming conventions and I fixed both. Once they could verify the decisions themselves, they were willing to rely on the results for production verification. That was the outcome I was advocating for — not "trust the black box," but "here's how you check it."

I dropped my original variant handling completely rather than defending a version of it. I'd do it the same way again, with one change: I'd build the debug view from the start, because the trust gap was predictable and I treated transparency as an afterthought.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: The question asks about a peer — was there really no teammate?**
**A:** No, it was a solo project; I was the only engineer assigned to it. The closest real counterpart was the manufacturing engineer, so I've answered with the actual influence dynamic rather than inventing a peer.

**Q: How did you influence people you had no authority over?**
**A:** With evidence they could inspect, not appeals. Their objection was that they couldn't verify the tool's decisions, so I removed the objection by exposing the decision path. Adoption followed from being able to check, not from being asked to trust.

**Q: Why not just make the comparison more accurate instead of adding a debug view?**
**A:** Accuracy wasn't the complaint — verifiability was. Even a more accurate tool they couldn't inspect would still get re-verified by hand. The debug view addressed the actual objection.

**Q: How did you know the adoption was real and not just politeness?**
**A:** They used the debug view to find and report two real edge cases in the first week, and they started relying on the results for production verification. That's a behavior change, not agreement in a meeting.

**Q: What did you learn about disagreeing with a domain expert?**
**A:** On the variant semantics I was the one who was wrong — the engineer had manufacturing intent I didn't have. The useful move wasn't to defend my parser; it was to recognize which disagreement I should stop holding and then commit to his decision completely.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Build the debug view from day one rather than after the skepticism. The trust gap was predictable, and I treated transparency as a follow-up instead of part of the product.
