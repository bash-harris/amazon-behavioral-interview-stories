## Answer (core, ~460 words)

The question trails off at "vs," so I'll name the assumption I'm answering under: I read it as balancing the customer's short-term needs against the engineering and quality need to keep a safety check — and making the call with data instead of picking a side. The clearest example is a decision in the BOM reconciliation tool.

The setting was this. The tool compared the pin count in the BOM against the number of pads in the layout footprint, literally. A BOM that called a part an 8-pin, against a 16-pad footprint, was reported as a mismatch. On real boards most of those were false alarms, and they had a cost: the manufacturing engineers — my customers — had started manually re-verifying every pin-count mismatch, which erased the value of automating the thing. Their need was real and I took it seriously: give them a report they can trust, or they'll stop using it.

The competing need was quieter but more important. The tempting fix was to remove pin count from the comparison entirely. That would have satisfied the customer's immediate ask and been almost free for me to build — but it would have deleted the check that catches a genuinely wrong part. If the BOM specifies a 16-pin IC and the layout shows an 8-pin footprint, the part physically can't fit. Removing the signal trades a daily annoyance for a rare, expensive escape: a bad board leaving the line. That's the balance — the customer's comfort today against the quality need that protects them later.

I took the decision to data rather than instinct, which is the "are right a lot" part of it. I pulled 20 BOMs with pin-count mismatches and sorted every one into three buckets: footprint larger than the component, footprint smaller, and exact match. The pattern was clean. A larger footprint is almost always a legitimate package variation — extra pads for package variants or no-connects. A smaller footprint is a genuine mismatch. So I kept the check and made it discriminating: more pads → flag for review, not a mismatch; fewer pads → definite mismatch; plus a component-type check and a configurable ratio threshold, defaulted from the data at 2:1.

I also recognized the heuristic could be wrong in the other direction — it can miss a mismatch that happens to satisfy the rules — so I didn't pretend the tool was infallible. I documented that false-negative risk and put the threshold and an override in the engineer's hands rather than making the judgment for them.

The result served both sides: false positives fell from about 12% to under 2% on boards with mixed component types, the engineers stopped re-verifying by hand, and the check that catches wrong parts stayed in the tool. The lesson for me is that when the customer's immediate ask and the customer's long-term interest point in different directions, the answer usually isn't to pick the popular side — it's to get the data and design something that serves both.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: What exactly was the "vs" you were balancing?**
**A:** The customer's short-term convenience against the quality need that protects them later. Removing pin count would have made their complaint disappear immediately, but it would also have removed the check that catches a physically wrong part. I balanced the two by keeping the check and making it discriminating rather than choosing one side.

**Q: Wasn't it obvious which side to pick?**
**A:** No — both were real. A tool that cries wolf gets abandoned, so the customer cost was genuine; a tool that misses a wrong part ships bad boards, so the quality cost was genuine. The decision only became clear once I had data on which mismatches were legitimate and which weren't. (STORY 10, lines 959–961.)

**Q: How did you get the data, and why 20 BOMs?**
**A:** I pulled 20 real BOMs that had produced pin-count mismatches and categorized every one into three types — footprint larger, footprint smaller, exact match. That turned "pin count is noisy" from an opinion into a finding: larger-footprint cases were almost always legitimate package variation, smaller-footprint cases were genuine. (STORY 10, lines 959–961.)

**Q: Where did the 2:1 threshold come from?**
**A:** From the same 20 BOMs. Legitimate footprint variations typically had a ratio of 2:1 or less — the footprint had at most twice as many pads as the component specified — so I set that as the default and made it configurable for other board designs. (STORY 10, follow-up line 995.)

**Q: What happens when the tool is wrong in the other direction?**
**A:** It can produce a false negative — a mismatch that happens to satisfy the type and ratio rules slips through. I don't hide that: I documented it, and the debug view lets the engineer see the raw comparison and override the tool. The configurable threshold keeps the judgment with the user rather than hard-coding one rule for every board. (STORY 10, follow-ups lines 999, 1015; debug view line 969.)

**Q: How did you know the balance actually worked?**
**A:** Two signals. False positives dropped from about 12% to under 2% on boards with mixed component types, and the engineers stopped manually re-verifying every pin-count mismatch — meaning they trusted the report again. And I ran the 15-BOM regression suite to confirm none of the previously-correct matches changed. (STORY 10, lines 973, 1011.)

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Collect more diverse board data up front. The pin-count issue only appeared with certain component types that weren't in my initial test set, so a broader sample would have surfaced it before it reached engineers. (STORY 10, Reflection line 977.)
