# Q36 — a time when you had to compromise quality over prescribed timeline

**Mapped LP(s):** Bias for Action; Deliver Results; Insist on the Highest Standards.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the five-day prototype and the documented two-week production hardening that repaid it (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 8, "Bias for Action — First Working Prototype"). One telling only: the 5-day monolithic prototype, the three gaps the team found in an hour, the alignment → illumination → export priority order, and production-ready in two weeks (5 days + 9 days). The 4,000-line / 21-module figures in `AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 7 are a different telling and are not used. ## Answer (core, ~440 words)

Honestly, yes — but the compromise I'd defend is one I bounded and then repaid, not one where I quietly moved the bar.

The manufacturing team had wanted a PCB comparison tool for six months, and it had never been scoped because the problem was "too complex." My deadline was self-imposed but real: get something working in front of the engineers that week so I'd learn what to build. So I built a first prototype in five days — a single monolithic HTML file, inline JavaScript, no build system, no modules, no tests. It loaded two images, ran a basic pixel-level comparison, and printed a result. It worked under ideal conditions, it was inaccurate under real lighting and camera angles, and the UX was rough. I knew all three of those things when I shipped it.

That's the compromise, stated precisely. The quality I cut was "works under real manufacturing conditions." The quality I did not cut was correctness under the conditions it claimed to support. I wrote that limitation down before the demo rather than letting the team discover it — a named limitation I can defend, not a hidden defect I can't.

Then I paid it back on a schedule. I showed it to the engineering lead on day five and stated the known limits, and within an hour the team had found three concrete gaps: pixel comparison broke under illumination change, there was no image alignment, and there was no export. Those became the plan, ordered by impact — alignment first, illumination normalization second, export third. Nine days after the prototype, the tool was production-ready. All three gaps closed, the same engine that had been inaccurate under real conditions was now reliable enough to use on the floor, and I refactored the monolith into modules so the next feature wasn't a rewrite.

So the trade was: users had a working tool from day five instead of week three, and the quality debt was bounded by a named limitation and closed inside two weeks. That's the standard I hold — speed is fine only when you know exactly what you cut and you have a date to bring it back. And the feedback from real users in those nine days gave me a better roadmap than the six months of scoping that had produced nothing.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Isn't a five-day, inaccurate prototype just a quality failure?**
**A:** The cut was narrow and named: correct under the conditions it claimed, inaccurate under conditions it didn't. I defined "bad enough to ship" as a limitation I could name, not a defect I couldn't — and it was repaid inside two weeks.

**Q: How do you know the standard was actually restored, not just promised?**
**A:** The team's three gaps were all closed before I called it production-ready — illumination-robust comparison, image alignment, and export — and I refactored the monolith into modules so the next change wasn't another rewrite. The proof is the tool moving from rough demo to floor use.

**Q: What's your rule for when an imperfect build is acceptable?**
**A:** Three criteria: it works correctly under expected conditions, its limitations are documented, and the feedback from shipping is worth more than the cost of the limitations. All three were true here.

**Q: Have you ever compromised quality without a good reason?**
**A:** Yes — I later copied the first tool to ship a second one in a week, and the copies diverged, including two different anomaly formulas, so the two tools could disagree about the same board. It bought the deadline and cost months of divergence. That one is still tracked debt; the collapse is designed — a golden-output harness and a shared headless scoring core, byte-identical before any behavior change — and I'm not claiming it's done. The lesson I applied after: treat a copy as debt with a due date, not a resting state.

**Q: What was the actual downside of the trade?**
**A:** The first users hit rough edges in their own environment before I'd hardened them. The subtler one is that a shortcut can outlive its deadline — which is what happened with the copy, and why I now put a date on the repayment.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** State the prototype's known limits even more sharply before the demo, so the team's first hour went to next steps instead of rediscovering what I already knew.
