## Answer (core, ~440 words)

The customer I pushed back on was internal — the manufacturing engineer who used the BOM reconciliation tool. The request, as it reached me, was to make the pin-count false alarms stop. The easy way to satisfy it was to drop pin count from the comparison entirely, and I pushed back on that, because it would have traded the engineer's short-term annoyance for a worse long-term failure: pin count is the signal that catches a genuinely wrong part, and without it a component that physically can't fit would sail through. To be precise, there's no external customer and no recorded quote in my notes; the request is the pain those engineers were expressing through their workaround — they had started manually re-checking every pin-count mismatch, which defeated the point of automating it.

I didn't push with an opinion. I pulled 20 BOMs that had pin-count mismatches and bucketed every one into three types: footprint larger than the component, footprint smaller, and exact match. The pattern was clean. A larger footprint is almost always a legitimate package variation — extra pads for package variants or no-connects. A smaller footprint is a genuine mismatch, because the part physically can't fit. Exact match is fine. So instead of deleting the signal, I proposed keeping it and making it discriminating: more pads → flag for review rather than call it a mismatch; fewer pads → definite mismatch.

That opened a new hole — a larger footprint on the wrong component type — so I pushed a second time for a component-type check with a configurable ratio threshold, defaulted from the data at 2:1. The result was false positives down from about 12% to under 2% on boards with mixed component types, and the engineers stopped manually re-verifying every mismatch. I confirmed with the full 15-BOM regression suite that none of the components that previously matched correctly had changed, because a fix that breaks the good cases isn't a fix.

I also kept the residual risk honest: the heuristic can still miss a mismatch that happens to satisfy the type and ratio rules. Rather than hide that, I documented it and put the threshold and an override in the engineer's hands through the debug view, so the judgment stayed with the person accountable for the board.

That's the shape of the pushback. I didn't refuse the request — I refused the shortcut it implied. Keeping the signal but making it smarter met the engineer's actual need, a report they could trust, without deleting the one check that protects them from a physically wrong part. And once the rule was set and the risk was documented, I committed to it fully and stopped pushing.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Who exactly was the customer, and was there a real request?**
**A:** The customer was the internal manufacturing engineer who used the tool — there is no external customer in my record. The request, in effect, was to make the pin-count false alarms stop, and the tempting answer was to drop the signal. Honest answer: the source documents the tool's measurement rule and the engineers' workaround (manually re-checking every mismatch), not a verbatim request or a named person. I'm answering with the pushback against that rule and requirement, not inventing a quote. (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 10, lines 949–951, 973.)

**Q: Why not just remove pin count if it caused so much noise?**
**A:** Because it's the signal that catches a wrong part. If the BOM specifies a 16-pin IC and the layout only has an 8-pin footprint, the part physically won't fit — that's a real defect. Deleting pin count would let those through. The right move was to make it smarter about when it matters, not to remove it. (STORY 10, follow-up line 991.)

**Q: How did you build the case before you spoke up?**
**A:** With data. Saying "pin count is unreliable" is a preference; showing that footprint-larger cases are almost always legitimate package variation — across 20 real BOMs, sorted into three buckets — is a finding the team could act on. (STORY 10, lines 959–961.)

**Q: What was the risk of your own proposal, and did you own it?**
**A:** Yes — the heuristic can produce false negatives, missing a mismatch that happens to satisfy the type and ratio rules. I stated that explicitly, made the threshold configurable so a 3:1 board can be handled, and added a debug view with an override, so the residual judgment stayed with the engineer rather than being hidden inside the tool. (STORY 10, follow-ups lines 999, 1015; debug view line 969; STORY 11, lines 1049–1051.)

**Q: How did you keep the pushback from becoming stubbornness?**
**A:** Push once, with evidence, on the finding and the customer risk — then commit to whatever the decision is and stop. The test for me is whether I have new data or just a preference. With pin count I had a finding; once the rule was set and the residual risk was documented with a configurable threshold and an override, there was nothing left to push. (STORY 10, follow-up line 1015.)

**Q: Did the customer actually get what they asked for?**
**A:** The need behind the request, yes: the false alarms went away (about 12% to under 2% on mixed-component boards) and they stopped re-verifying by hand. What they didn't get was the shortcut of removing the check — because that would have served them worse the first time a wrong part slipped through. (STORY 10, line 973.)

**Q: How did you verify the new rule didn't break genuine detection?**
**A:** I ran the full 15-BOM test suite, including the 5 boards with pin-count issues, and verified three things: the 5 problem boards now produced correct results, the other 10 still did, and no component that previously matched correctly changed output. The suite acted as the regression safety net. (STORY 10, follow-up line 1011.)
