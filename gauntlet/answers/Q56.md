## Answer (framework, ~380 words)

I don't have a documented event where I personally handed a colleague a piece of wrong information, so I won't invent one. This is the same gap as Q25 - treat the two as one prep answer, not two. What I do have is a repeated, documented pattern of my systems putting wrong information in front of the manufacturing engineers, and how I handled it; I'll offer that as the framework for the interpersonal case.

The clearest instance is the BOM reconciliation tool. It compared the BOM pin count to the layout pad count literally and flagged a mismatch whenever they differed. That was wrong information: on mixed-component boards, false-positive reports ran about 12% of the time. An engineer reading that report would believe a component was misplaced when it was a legitimate package variant. I didn't hide it or blame the BOM. I categorized 20 mismatches, found the asymmetry - more pads is usually a valid footprint, fewer pads is a real mismatch - rewrote the rule, added the component-type check and a configurable ratio, and brought false-positive reports under 2%, keeping the signal and owning the residual false-negative risk instead of deleting it to make the report look clean.

Two more documented pieces. When I built the debug view, it exposed the parser's raw output; when the parse was wrong - twice in the first week, on unusual naming conventions - the engineer could see exactly where the data diverged and I fixed it fast. And the GPT-4o analysis, right about 85% of the time, was never presented as a diagnosis: the UI labels it "AI Analysis" with a confidence percentage, tells the engineer to verify it with their own expertise, and gives a correct/incorrect button so systematic errors surface.

The framework I'd run if I gave a colleague wrong information:

1. **Surface it myself, immediately** - before they act on it; the cost of wrong information grows with time.
2. **Correct the record concretely** - the right fact and the impact, not a vague apology.
3. **Take responsibility** - no blaming the tool, the data, or the person who asked.
4. **Explain where the error came from**, so they can calibrate how far to trust the rest.
5. **Fix the process** so it can't recur, then follow up to confirm the correction landed.
6. **Invite them to flag it** if it happens again.

That's the honest answer: the record shows how I handle wrong information my tools put in front of people. It does not contain a personal wrong-information scene, and I won't claim one.
