## Answer (core, ~495 words)

The expectation for the PCB-vs-layout tool was a comparison that flagged where a photographed board didn't match its design. A single-signal comparison met that literally: convert both images to grayscale, subtract them, flag the differences. It caught the obvious cases — a missing part, a clearly wrong part. But when I measured it on real boards it was wrong about 15% of the time, almost all false alarms from lighting and sensor noise. That was the number I decided not to ship. A tool that flags roughly one component in seven as suspicious when it's fine is the kind of noise that erodes trust in an inspection tool.

So I set my own acceptance bar: cut the false positives hard without giving up real detections, and prove it on images where I knew the answer.

I rebuilt the comparison as four separate signals, each blind to something the others catch. The first is the raw grayscale difference — good for gross mismatches, weak under lighting change. The second applies CLAHE to both images first, which normalizes local contrast so illumination stops registering as a defect and subtle differences — a wrong-value resistor with slightly different markings — start to show. The third stretches the histogram for an aggressive contrast comparison that exaggerates near-identical parts. The fourth runs Canny edge detection on both images and compares the edge maps; edges are invariant to brightness and contrast, which makes that signal the most reliable one for structural differences like a part present in the layout and absent in the photo.

Each signal votes on whether a region matches, and the votes combine into a weighted score with a configurable threshold. I deliberately didn't hard-code the sensitivity, because the right operating point depends on the board and the lighting, and the engineer inspecting it knows that better than I do.

I validated it the way I'd validate any classifier: a ground-truth set of 100 board images with known defects — wrong component, missing component, extra component — starting from equal signal weights and grid-searching combinations against precision and recall.

The outcome was false positives down from about 15% to under 3%, with true positives still above 95%. At the best threshold, F1 was 0.94 — precision 0.97, recall 0.91 — meaning 97% of what it flagged was real and it still caught 91% of the real defects. It ran in about 500 ms per board in the browser, offline, inside the tool the manufacturing floor used.

Two honest corrections. I assumed more signals would always help; a fifth (frequency-domain via FFT) added computation and no measurable accuracy, so I removed it — signal quality beat signal quantity. And I'd have started from edge detection instead of raw comparison: edge detection was the most robust signal, and starting raw meant spending the early iterations fixing its false positives.

The ask was a comparison that worked. I held out for one the engineers could trust, and the false-positive rate is the evidence.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Why wasn't the single-signal comparison good enough?**
**A:** Because the number was 15% false positives on real boards — almost all lighting and noise, not real defects. On a tool whose whole job is telling an engineer what to look at, that false-alarm rate means they re-check by hand and stop trusting the report. I measured the problem instead of eyeballing it.

**Q: The bar here was yours, not someone else's. How is that "exceeding expectations"?**
**A:** The expectation was a comparison that worked, and a single signal did work on the easy cases. I set the acceptance bar at *trust*, not at existence, and then proved the higher bar with a ground-truth set. Exceeding it meant refusing the version that technically passed.

**Q: How did you choose the signal weights?**
**A:** I started from equal weights and grid-searched combinations against a validation set of 100 board images with known ground truth, keeping the total weight at 1.0. The final weights were raw 0.2, CLAHE 0.3, contrast 0.2, edge 0.3 — edge and CLAHE were higher because they were more reliable on that set. The weights live in a config object so they can be adjusted per board.

**Q: Why four signals and not three or five?**
**A:** Each signal I kept adds a category the others miss: raw catches gross differences, CLAHE handles lighting-robust differences, contrast catches subtle appearance differences, edges catch structural differences. Three would leave a category uncovered. I tested a fifth — frequency-domain via FFT — and it added no measurable value, so it came out.

**Q: How did you validate the pipeline's accuracy?**
**A:** A ground-truth dataset of 100 board images where I knew every defect location and type — wrong, missing, or extra component. I ran the pipeline across threshold settings and computed precision, recall, and F1. The optimal threshold gave F1 0.94, with precision 0.97 and recall 0.91.

**Q: Is this the same false-positive problem as the pin-count BOM issue across your other answers?**
**A:** No, and I keep them separate. The pin-count fix was a heuristic rule in the BOM reconciliation parser, about 12% to under 2%. This is the image comparison engine — a different component with a different metric, and the 100-image ground-truth validation here is specific to the comparison pipeline.
