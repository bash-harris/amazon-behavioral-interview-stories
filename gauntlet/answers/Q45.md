## Answer (core, ~470 words)

The most difficult thing I built was the non-rigid alignment stage of the PCB-vs-layout tool — and it's also where I got the sharpest corrective feedback, so I'll give you both.

The comparison runs on a 10-pixel mismatch threshold, so alignment had to hold to about 2–3 pixels or every result downstream was noise. I'd never implemented the math. I chose thin-plate spline warping — a non-parametric method that fits a smooth warp through control points — over elastic deformation and B-splines because it minimizes bending energy, is deterministic given the control points, needs no physical constants like Young's modulus that have no clear meaning for a PCB, and is mature in OpenCV. I implemented it from the original Bookstein 1989 paper because there was no JavaScript library. The failure mode was brutal: a single wrongly matched control point pulls the whole warp field the wrong way, so you ship something that looks precise and is silently worse.

The feedback came in two documented forms. First, the engineers reported the tool was flagging false alarms on warped boards. Second, when I brought them in to overlay the aligned photo on the layout, their spot-check caught cases where my metric looked good but the warp was subtly wrong.

I didn't defend the number. The metric said a 2.1-pixel average offset with 0.8 standard deviation across 20 ground-truth images, but the engineers were telling me something the metric couldn't see, so I treated their overlay as a validation gate rather than a courtesy. I reproduced the failures on the same inputs, kept the human check as a standing part of the test, and let it catch what the average hid.

I also went back to the physics instead of tuning around it. The residual error was reflow-oven warping, which is non-uniform, and a global transform structurally cannot fix a bow in the middle of a board. That's what justified adding the TPS stage and rejecting any control-point match below a template-similarity threshold, because one bad control point corrupts the whole field.

The part I'd call growth is the self-criticism the feedback forced. I'd over-built: I implemented full SURF feature matching before discovering board-outline contour finding was enough for most boards — more general, but more expensive and harder to debug, and I should have started simpler and added complexity only when the data demanded it. I also named the boundary honestly instead of implying the warp was universal: above roughly 5mm of deformation the control points can't capture it and a physically based model would be needed, which is rare in production.

The result: average misalignment dropped from 15–20 pixels with homography alone to 2–3 pixels once TPS was in, and comparison accuracy went from roughly 85% to over 98% for boards with moderate warping, handling photos up to 30 degrees off-perpendicular and 3mm of warp. The engineers reported the tool now caught mismatches on warped boards that previously triggered false alarms.

The lesson I still use: when someone tells me my result is wrong, the number is my hypothesis and their observation is the data. You deal with it by finding the failing case, not by defending the metric.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Was that really "negative feedback," or just a QA finding?**
**A:** Two documented pieces, and I'll be precise. The engineers reported the tool was producing false alarms on warped boards — that's negative feedback from users. And when they overlaid the aligned photo on the layout, their spot-check caught cases where my metric looked good but the warp was subtly wrong — that's a direct correction of my result. The only formal team-review negative feedback in my record is the five-day-prototype review, which I've used for other questions and am not re-telling here. I won't inflate the QA finding into a one-on-one I didn't have.

**Q: How did you keep from getting defensive?**
**A:** I treated the claim and my metric as different kinds of evidence. My average said 2.1 pixels; their eyes said something was still off. Rather than argue the average, I asked what they were seeing and reproduced it on the same inputs. The number was my hypothesis; their observation was data I didn't have.

**Q: How did you verify the fix landed?**
**A:** Quantitatively, 20 board images with ground-truth markers — average offset 2.1 pixels, standard deviation 0.8. Qualitatively, the engineers overlaid the aligned image on the layout and checked that component pads lined up, which is what caught cases the metric hid. I kept both, and I re-ran a fixed regression set so a fix for one issue couldn't break another.

**Q: What was actually hard about the task, independent of the feedback?**
**A:** Implementing TPS from the Bookstein 1989 paper with no JavaScript library, and doing it in a browser. The math is a radial-basis sum plus a linear term solving an (n+3)×(n+3) system, but the operational danger is that one bad control point bends the whole warp the wrong way — so correctness depends on rejecting weak control-point matches, not just on getting the formula right.

**Q: When does it still fail, and what would you do differently?**
**A:** Very severe warping above about 5mm still defeats the control points, and a more physically based model would be needed for those rare cases. If I did it again I'd start with the simpler approach — board-outline contour detection was sufficient for most boards — and add SURF and the non-rigid stage only where the data demanded it, instead of building the general solution first.

**Q: What did this change about how you take feedback on technical work?**
**A:** I now ask for the specific failing case rather than a general verdict, because a case is reproducible and a verdict isn't. And I keep a qualitative human check alongside the metric, because an average can be right while the thing it's summarizing is wrong. That's why the overlay check stayed in the pipeline.
