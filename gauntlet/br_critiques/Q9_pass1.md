## 7. Recommended structure (improved STAR outline)

- **S (~45s):** Manufacturing ran three verification steps by hand, on three different tools: schematic-vs-EBOM (document-to-document), PCB-vs-layout (image-to-document), good-board-vs-new-board (image-to-image). Different alignment needs, different failure modes, all of it 20–30 minutes of cross-referencing per board for BOM alone. I was the only engineer assigned to the suite.
- **T (~20s):** Build all three as one suite — reliable per tool, usable by non-programmers on locked-down machines, maintainable by one developer. Delivered within the eight-week window I worked to.
- **A (~2.5 min, first-person):**
  1. Ordered by impact: BOM first (the worst time sink), then the two image tools — each one standing on the previous one's infrastructure.
  2. Shared the skeleton once (upload/validation, preprocessing, auto-save, reports, UI); deliberately kept the three comparison engines separate — a universal engine would have added complexity without value; the cost was accepted duplication.
  3. Learned OpenCV.js while building the alignment-heavy second tool; browser inference via TensorFlow.js came in for the third.
  4. Identical UX across all three — drag-drop, green/red/blue — so the workflow was learned once.
  5. When engineers didn't trust the BOM verdicts, built the debug view (raw text → each parse step → rule applied → decision); in its first week they used it to catch two heuristics I'd gotten wrong; that auditability is what made them willing to rely on it in production.
- **R (~45s, only defensible outcomes):** Three tools inside the eight-week window. Adopted by engineers across both sites. On the BOM step, a 20–30-minute manual reconciliation became a report in seconds; the hardened tool caught three real mismatches in early production use that manual review had missed. Team estimate of the aggregate saving: roughly 20 hours a week across the floor — stated as an estimate, with the per-board baselines as its arithmetic, because I never instrumented it.
- **L (~40s):** Two named misses — retrofitted the shared layer instead of building it before tool one; discovered BOM formats reactively in production instead of collecting floor samples first. Two named debts — per-tool error-handling drift and tight coupling to SAP export formats. What changed in me: get the real artifacts and the real users in front of me before writing code, and measure what you intend to claim.

> **"What would still make me unconvinced as a Bar Raiser?"**
> That the pride in this story is priced in numbers you can't account for. Fifteen engineers, two sites, twenty hours a week — who counted, who timed, against what baseline, when? Your own bank documents measurement methods for your smaller claims (100-pair F1 sets, 20-image pixel ground truth) and none for the ones that matter most here. Add that your build order survives in three conflicting versions across the sources, that the TensorFlow.js timing you state is supported only by the line you told yourself to exclude, and that "first production week" sits uncomfortably next to your own 15%-false-negative deployment history — and the shape of the problem is clear: the architecture and the ownership are real and verifiable, but the outcome layer is a story bank grading itself. If your answer to "how did you measure" is a recollection you can't produce evidence for, say so plainly and lean on what survives attack — the solo build, the propagated fix, the debug view that let users falsify you — because right now the three sentences you are proudest of are the three you would lose under one follow-up each.
