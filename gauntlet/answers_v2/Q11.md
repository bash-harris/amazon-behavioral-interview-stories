## Answer (core, ~675 words)

I optimized a model by refusing to trust a number that looked great on the surface. I built the defect classifier for the PCB inspection suite — take a cropped image of a component and classify it as good, wrong value, wrong orientation, or missing. I had ML theory but had never trained a production model from scratch, and I was doing it while the inspection tools were already in use by manufacturing engineers. That set the bar: validation accuracy above 90%, no big training-to-validation gap, inference under 100ms.

The first training run was the honest, interesting failure: 99% training accuracy, 72% validation. That gap is a model memorizing, not learning. Had I stopped at the training number, I'd have shipped something that looked excellent and failed on the floor.

So I went after the cause instead of the symptom. I'd let gradients flow into the whole pre-trained network; the randomly initialized head produces large, noisy gradients early on that overwrite the general features — edges, textures, shapes — ImageNet pre-training had already learned. Catastrophic forgetting: I was destroying the thing I was trying to reuse. My first response was a regularization pass — fewer unfrozen layers, stronger augmentation, dropout on the head — and it took the second run to 91% validation. That was the honest intermediate: several changes moved the number together, and the lesson was that transfer learning doesn't prevent overfitting.

The strategy that actually worked was two-phase progressive unfreezing. Phase one: freeze the entire EfficientNetV2S base and train only the head for 10 epochs at 0.001, so the head stabilizes before anything touches pre-trained layers. Phase two: unfreeze the last 30 layers for 20 epochs at 0.0001 — a 10x lower rate, the conventional starting point, which I verified rather than assumed: I tried 0.0005 and 0.0002 for the base in logged W&B runs and kept 0.0001 because it validated best. Early stopping, patience five epochs.

The data work mattered as much as the schedule. CLAHE preprocessing for the mixed fluorescent/LED/shadowed lighting on the floor; 5x loss weighting on the defective classes because "good" outnumbered them about 5:1 — on that ratio a always-good model scores 83%, which is why precision-recall, not raw accuracy, was the metric I tuned on. And the split: 70/15/15, stratified, done at component level — two photos of the same component in different splits would have leaked memorization straight into my validation number. The 30 layers weren't folklore either: I tested 20 and 50 as well. Twenty underfit, 50 showed overfitting on the small dataset, 30 was the empirical middle. The logs record the directions; I won't quote per-config numbers I didn't record.

I want to be precise about which number sits on which set, because conflating them is exactly the failure I'm claiming to have caught. 94% validation accuracy — up from 72% — with a 3-point train–validation gap, on the ~300-image validation split. My notes separately record 94% on a held-out test set written as 500 images, with per-class recalls of 92/96/98; 500 doesn't reconcile with 15% of ~2,000, and at 5:1 every defect category is roughly two dozen images — one or two samples move a recall point by percent. So what I stand behind: accuracy and recall above 90% for every defect category. Inference benchmarked at about 45ms per image on CPU against the 100ms bar — single-image benchmark, hardware unrecorded, and the shipped path was TensorFlow.js in-browser, so that's a benchmark, not a floor number. Retrain took about 30 minutes on one GPU, so the pipeline could absorb new defect types without disrupting the workflow.

What I'd change: I assumed more unfrozen layers would always help — 50 proved that wrong; I assumed CLAHE would be enough — some extreme lighting survived it, and controlled lighting wasn't an option on the floor. I'd use a cosine schedule instead of a fixed phase-two rate, and I'd collect far more than ~2,000 labeled images.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: Walk me through catastrophic forgetting. Why does the order of the phases matter?**
**A:** Early layers learn general features — edges, textures, shapes — and later layers compose them. If you unfreeze everything immediately, the randomly initialized head produces large, noisy gradients that shift the pre-trained layers and destroy those general features; the model then memorizes the new data. Phase one stabilizes the head with the base frozen, so when the base unfreezes the gradients are smaller and consistent. Going straight for the whole network is what produced 99% training / 72% validation.

**Q: Why unfreeze exactly 30 layers, and how do you know it wasn't luck?**
**A:** I tested 20, 30, and 50 and compared runs in W&B. Twenty underfit — the frozen early layers couldn't yield domain-specific features. Fifty showed overfitting on ~2,000 images. Thirty was the empirical middle: enough trainable parameters to learn PCB-specific patterns while the frozen layers preserved general features. Honest caveat: the sweep evidence is directional in my records, and the 94% is the final run's validation number — I didn't compute run-to-run variance, so "was it luck" can only be answered by the mechanism (the train/val gap behavior), not by confidence intervals I never took.

**Q: Why 0.001 for the head and 0.0001 for the unfrozen base?**
**A:** The head starts random and needs to move quickly; the base already has good weights and needs fine adjustment — the conventional 10x ratio. I verified it rather than trusting it: I tried 0.0005 and 0.0002 for the base in logged W&B runs and kept 0.0001, which validated best.

**Q: What is CLAHE, and did it fully solve the lighting problem?**
**A:** Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization — tiles the image, equalizes each tile independently with a contrast clip limit, so local contrast improves without blowing out bright regions or amplifying noise. It mattered because the floor photos came from fluorescent, LED, and shadowed setups; without it the model classifies lighting, not components. No, it didn't fully solve it — some extremes it couldn't compensate, and controlled lighting wasn't feasible in the manufacturing environment. That was one of my two falsified assumptions.

**Q: How did you handle class imbalance, and what was your primary metric?**
**A:** "Good" outnumbered defects about 5:1, so a model could score 83% by always predicting "good." I weighted defective examples 5x in the loss and tuned on precision-recall, not raw accuracy. What I report from the held-out evaluation is recall above 90% for every defect category; my notes carry per-class figures (92/96/98), but at those set sizes each category is roughly two dozen images, so I treat the per-class decimals as beyond what the data can defend.

**Q: Your repo's training script unfreezes the last 50 layers at 1e-6 with a phase-2 fit running epochs 15 to 50 and gradient clipping — the exact config your sweep rejected. Which is real?**
**A:** Both, at different times, on different tasks — and the file says so. `pcb_training_v2_fixed.py` is a later hardened iteration of this pipeline: its own comments read "Increased from 10," "Much lower! Was 1e-5," "CLAHE applied BEFORE augmentation," and it documents the fixes made on top of the phase structure I just described. Its 50-layer figure is on a different Kaggle present/absent dataset whose path the file itself calls "bigger" — and the dataset-size-to-unfrozen-layers relationship I worked out (bigger dataset, more layers you can afford) is why 50 on that set doesn't contradict 30 being optimal on my ~2,000. What I won't do is blend the two run records into one tidy story.

**Q: Then which script produced your 94%?**
**A:** None checked in — that's a gap I'd rather name than hide. The shipped artifact's head is `Dense(1, sigmoid)`: a binary present/absent detector. The 4-class pipeline the story describes isn't in the repo I can open today. So the 94% is the story bank's documented run, not an artifact I can hand you; if a Bar Raiser opens that file, the mismatch is the first thing they'd find, which is why I lead with it.

**Q: How reliable were the results?**
**A:** Configurations were compared as logged W&B runs; the headline numbers are single final-run measurements — no repeated seeds, no variance, and the held-out-set-size discrepancy (500 written versus ~300 implied by the 70/15/15 split of ~2,000) is unresolved in my own notes. The qualitative conclusions — two-phase beats naive full unfreezing; 30 beats 20 and 50 — survived because they were directional across runs, not because I have confidence intervals.
