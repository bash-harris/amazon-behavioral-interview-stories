# Q1 — tell me about a time you used genai to improve personal or team productivity (v2, post pass-1 Bar Raiser repair)

**Mapped LP(s):** Invent and Simplify (primary); touches Deliver Results and Learn and Be Curious.

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — GPT-4o VLM analysis feature (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 8 "Adding an AI Eye to the Inspection"; the same work is told a second way in `AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREP.md` STORY 3, where the VLM is the comparison engine itself — the two accounts measure different things and are reconciled in the Number ledger below). Documented outcome: the engineers' manual interpretation step (their own estimate: ~30 s per flagged mismatch) was replaced by a model verdict with ~5 s API turnaround plus a quick human confirm.

## Answer (core, ~300–500 words)

Honestly, the biggest GenAI win wasn't flashy — it deleted a manual interpretation step from someone else's day. I was the only engineer on the PCB inspection suite at Bosch: browser tools that align a photo of a board to its layout and flag components that don't match. The four-signal comparison was good at saying *where* a mismatch was — it highlighted regions in red — but it couldn't say *what* it meant. Wrong-value resistor, part rotated 180°, or just a reflection? The manufacturing engineer had to zoom in and decide, region by region. By their own estimate that was about thirty seconds a region, and a dense board had five to twenty of them. That human step was the last bottleneck.

My task: add an AI that looks at the flagged region, identifies the component, classifies the mismatch (wrong value, wrong orientation, missing, extra, or false alarm), and gives a confidence — back in under five seconds, working in the server build and the standalone build, where there may be no API access at all.

I evaluated the vision-language models myself before committing. GPT-4V took about eight seconds per request versus three for GPT-4o; Claude 3 saw well but its API was less mature then; Gemini Pro Vision was fast but weaker on small SMD parts in my side-by-side tests. LLaVA was the local option, but it needs at least 8 GB of VRAM and the factory machines don't have it. So: GPT-4o as the "expert eye," and I spent my own engineering on the preprocessing around it — crop the flagged region from both the layout and the photo, send both with a structured prompt, parse a JSON reply with `component_type`, `mismatch_type`, `confidence`, `description`.

The first prompt was a mistake. "Describe what you see and compare it to the expected layout" produced verbose, inconsistent answers — sometimes about the background, sometimes the part. Forcing a fixed JSON schema and adding examples for common mismatch types is what made it usable.

The honest result: the model's turnaround was about five seconds end-to-end — that's API time, not engineer time. What actually changed is that the engineer's full zoom-and-decide collapsed into a read-and-confirm; the saving was most of their thirty seconds, not all of it, and I never costed the confirm step. On accuracy I'll be straight with you: the type-and-mismatch call was right in roughly 85% of cases, and that figure is a rough self-assessment from the engineers' correct/incorrect marks, not a held-out test. The 15% were images too poor or parts too small — which I told the engineers up front. The comparison pipeline underneath *did* have a real validation (92% F1 on a hundred board pairs with known defect locations, versus 78% for plain pixel comparison), but that number measures the engine, not the AI's description. What they told me they valued was triage: the labels let them pick which mismatches on a dense board to open first.

My wrong assumption was that the model could read component values — SMD text is too small or blurry; it's much better at type and orientation. And the measurement I should have built on day one was the thing I never built: a labeled set for the descriptions. I logged the wrong marks; I never turned them into a number. That's the step I'd fix first.

## The trust design (one decision, assembled)

What distinguishes this from "I called an API" is that I designed for the AI being wrong before it happened: the output is labeled "AI Analysis" with a confidence percentage and framed as a suggestion the engineer verifies, never a verdict; every analysis carries a correct/incorrect mark so systematic prompt errors surface to me; the standalone build never trusts us with a key — the user enters their own GPT-4o key into localStorage, it goes straight to OpenAI and never touches our servers, and with no key the AI simply disables itself with a clear message while the comparison keeps working; sending board photos to a cloud API went through the security team, who approved it because the images contain no trade secrets; and if the API is unreachable the UI says "AI analysis unavailable — check API connection" and the red regions still render. The reason all of this leans human-in-the-loop rather than confidence alone is error asymmetry: in QA, a wrongly *reassuring* verdict ships a defective board, while a wrongly alarming one costs an engineer thirty seconds. Those are not equal-cost errors, so the AI was never allowed to close a board on its own.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: How do you know this improved productivity rather than just adding automation on top?**
**A:** Carefully stated: the 30 s was the engineers' own estimate of the manual zoom-and-decide on one flagged region, not a timed study. The 5 s is the model's end-to-end turnaround — API time, not human time. What replaced the 30 s was reading the verdict *plus* a confirm; so the realized saving is "most of the 30 s per region," and I'll admit the confirm step was never costed — that's the hole in my own measurement. What I can point to is qualitative and it's real: engineers told me the labels let them prioritize which mismatches on dense boards to investigate first, and per-call cost was trivial at this volume (~$0.01 per analysis, so about $0.10 for a 10-mismatch board).

**Q: Is it 85% or 92%? I feel like you have two numbers.**
**A:** Both, because they measure different things, and I keep them separate deliberately. 92% is the F1 of the *comparison pipeline* — the engine that decides whether regions differ — measured against a validation dataset of 100 board pairs with known defect locations, versus 78% for pixel-level comparison. 85% is the *VLM description* — was the component type and mismatch class right — and that one is a rough self-assessment from the engineers' correct/incorrect marks with no held-out set behind it. The honest meta-point: the engine got a real test and the AI add-on didn't. That asymmetry is the single thing I'd fix first, and it's why I quote 85% as a ballpark, never as validated accuracy.

**Q: Why GPT-4o and not another model?**
**A:** I ran the candidates side by side on the actual task, manually — a benchmark suite would have been over-engineering for the decision I had to make. GPT-4V: about 8 s per request versus about 3 s for GPT-4o. Claude 3: strong visual understanding, less mature API at the time. Gemini Pro Vision: fast, weaker on small-object detection, which is the whole game with SMD parts. GPT-4o handled both images in one request, which kept the integration simple. LLaVA for self-hosting needed ≥8 GB VRAM the factory machines don't have — rejected on a hardware constraint, not a preference.

**Q: What went wrong, beyond the first prompt?**
**A:** Three things. The first prompt ("describe what you see") was over-clever and inconsistent — the fixed JSON schema plus few-shot examples is what worked. My assumption that the VLM could read component values off the board was wrong — SMD text is too small or blurry; it's good at type and orientation, not OCR. And the failure I only see in hindsight: I logged the correct/incorrect marks but never quantified the correction rate or built a held-out set, so the 85% is a judgment, not a measurement. If asked "which systematic prompt errors did the feedback surface," the honest answer is I remember the categories — image quality and tiny components — not a tally. That's a gap I'd close before doing it again.

**Q: What happens when the AI is wrong — how did you design for it?**
**A:** See the trust design: suggestion-not-verdict UI with a confidence, a per-analysis correct/incorrect mark that routed systematic errors back to me, and full graceful degradation — no key or no API means the four-signal comparison still runs offline. Underneath all of it is the error-asymmetry argument: a falsely reassuring "false alarm" verdict is the expensive one in manufacturing QA, so the human judgment was kept in the loop by design, and the offline path never depended on the model at all. Cloud latency and cost were mitigated by capping the region of interest at 512×512 and caching results for identical image pairs — those were risk controls on the design, not measured optimizers; I have no cache-hit-rate number.

**Q: Did anyone push back?**
**A:** Not that I can point to — and I'll own that as a thinner part of the story. The security review was the one formal gate, and it was a yes: board photos contain no trade secrets. Adoption was voluntary on a tool the engineers already used, so there was no rollout fight; my visibility into skepticism was the incorrect-marks, and I never mined those hard enough to report what they said.

**Q: What's the scale ceiling of this design?**
**A:** At the documented load — 5–20 mismatches per board, one board at a time, 429 retries up to three times — rate limits never actually triggered. At fleet scale it breaks on cost and determinism: hundreds of components per board, hundreds of boards a day, a hosted model whose version I don't control. The answer I'd design next (designed, not built) is a cost-aware cascade: classical CV screens everything, and only the uncertain confidence band escalates to the VLM — on modeled distributions that's roughly an 85/13/2 pass/escalate/fail split, cutting per-board model spend by around 25× versus calling the model on everything. Those band volumes are design targets from my cost arithmetic, not measurements — they'd need the same held-out set I'm admitting I skipped.

**Q: How many people used it, and for how long?**
**A:** Feature-level telemetry I don't have. The suite it lived inside was adopted by 15 manufacturing engineers across two sites within about two weeks, and the team's combined savings were estimated around 20 hours per week — but that's for all three tools, not attributable to the AI feature. The only aggregate signal specific to the analysis itself is the qualitative prioritization feedback in my answer.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Three things. Start with the simple structured prompt and iterate up — I converged late because the first prompt was clever instead of constrained. Build the held-out labeled set *before* shipping the description feature, so "how accurate is it?" has an answer with a denominator. And price the self-hosting path from day one — per-call API cost is trivial at this volume and non-trivial at fleet scale — even though the specific blocker then was that the factory machines lack the VRAM for a local VLM.
