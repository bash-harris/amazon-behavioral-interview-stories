# Q6 — a time when you needed to influence a peer who had a differing opinion about a shared goal

**Mapped LP(s):** Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit (claimed primary — only its *commit* half and *advocating for what's best for the customer* are evidenced; the *"disagrees with peers and supervisors using data"* strength is an **OPEN GAP**, no documented instance — see the note under Strong signals and in Fact provenance). **Earn Trust is the strongest-fitting LP** and is where I'd lead if you want the best-evidenced version; Are Right, A Lot (touch).

**Story used:** BGSW PCB Inspector Suite — the manufacturing-engineer stakeholder group on the BOM reconciliation tool (`AMAZON_INTERVIEW_PREPARATION.md` STORY 11 "Debug Endpoint for Manufacturing Transparency" + STORY 1 "The BOM Parser That Refused to Break"). The counterpart is a documented stakeholder/customer group, **not a peer or supervisor** — that mismatch is the story's real shape and I flag it up front rather than reframe it.

## Answer (core, ~430 words)

I'll be straight about the shape of this one. I built the PCB inspection suite solo — I was the only engineer on it — so there was no teammate on the other side of the table. The people whose opinions I had to work with were the customers: the manufacturing engineers. So this is not a peer-disagreement story and I won't dress it as one. What it actually shows is me taking pushback from a stakeholder group and the *commit* half of Disagree and Commit. The strength the rubric calls "disagrees with peers and supervisors using data" is one this record genuinely has no example of, and I say so at the end rather than borrow a colleague who wasn't there.

We shared one goal: boards verified correctly. We differed on two documented points. The first was a design call where I was simply wrong. I had decided variant references like `R1||R2` were invalid and my parser silently dropped them — each drop breaking the tool in production. A manufacturing engineer pointed out that one physical position can accept either part. He had the domain intent; I had a bad assumption. I didn't litigate it. I adopted his decision and rewrote the splitter to normalize `||` and `/`. That's the commit half — dropping my own design completely once the better call was clear — not backbone.

The second point was their distrust, not a disagreement I won. When the tool flagged a mismatch the engineers couldn't see the reasoning behind it, so they were hesitant to rely on it for critical decisions. The move actually in my hands wasn't an argument; it was making the decision path inspectable. I shipped a `?debug=true` view exposing the raw BOM text, the parsed reference after each preprocessing step, the matched layout entry, the comparison result, and the rule that fired, plus a per-component decision tree and an aggregate dashboard. If they thought a call was wrong, the view showed them where. I made two deliberate delivery decisions: a URL parameter rather than a UI toggle, because debug is for technical users and shouldn't clutter the production view, and localhost-only exposure, because the view dumps the engineer's own data back to them and nothing more.

That shifted their stance. In the first week they used the view to find two edge cases with unusual naming conventions and I fixed both — a behavior change, not agreement in a meeting. Once they could verify the decisions themselves, they were **more willing** to rely on the results for production verification. Two things I won't overstate: there's no headcount or verification-time number in this telling — those scale figures live in my stakeholder story, kept unblended — and nobody argued *against* the debug view, so "influence" here meant shipping what they'd asked for, not holding a line against a peer.

I'd do it the same way with one change: build the debug view from the start, because the trust gap was predictable and I treated transparency as an afterthought.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: The question asks about a peer — was there really no teammate?**
**A:** No. Solo project; I was the only engineer assigned. The only internal person in the record is an engineering lead who *received a demo*, and no opposing position from him is documented. The closest real counterpart was the manufacturing engineer, so I answered with the actual influence dynamic rather than inventing a peer. If you want the best-evidenced LP here, it's Earn Trust, not backbone against a colleague.

**Q: Did anyone disagree with you — about the debug view, or the concession?**
**A:** The only disagreement in the record is the one where they were right and I committed (the variant semantics). Nothing shows anyone resisting the debug view or preferring to keep their skepticism — I shipped what the engineers had effectively asked for. That absence is why I flag "disagree with a peer with data" as an open gap rather than claim it.

**Q: How did you influence people you had no authority over?**
**A:** With evidence they could inspect, not appeals. Their objection was that they couldn't verify the tool's decisions, so I removed the objection by exposing the decision path. Adoption followed from being able to check, not from being asked to trust.

**Q: Why a debug view, and why delivered as a query parameter on localhost?**
**A:** Accuracy wasn't the complaint — verifiability was, so a more accurate but still opaque tool would have stayed distrusted. I chose a URL parameter over a UI toggle because debug is for technical users and shouldn't clutter the production view, and kept it localhost/local-network only because it raw-dumps the engineer's own BOM and layout data, nothing they don't already hold. Those were the real delivery and security decisions in this act.

**Q: How did you know the adoption was real and not just politeness?**
**A:** They used the debug view to find and report two real edge cases in the first week, and became more willing to rely on the output for production verification. That's a behavior change, not a meeting nod. I'd caveat the n: it's one team, one week, and I'm not attaching a headcount to it in this telling.

**Q: What were the costs or trade-offs?**
**A:** Honest answer: the debug view was low-cost to ship and I have no development-time figure to quote — so I won't invent one. The trade-off I did feel was timing: I treated transparency as a follow-up, and the two naming edge cases the engineers found were exactly the kind the view would have caught sooner had I built it from day one.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Build the debug view from day one rather than after the skepticism. The trust gap was predictable, and I treated transparency as a follow-up instead of part of the product.

**Q: What happens at larger scale — a team that still won't trust you after you ship the view?**
**A:** This is one tool, one team, one week; I have no documented larger-scale win and won't claim one. The transferable mechanism I'd carry is: when a group won't trust output, make the decision path inspectable and let them find the counterexamples themselves.
