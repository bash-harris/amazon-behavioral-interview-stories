## Answer (core, ~500 words)

I'm proudest of the PCB inspection suite I built at Bosch, because I owned it end to end — and because it stopped being my project and became the way the manufacturing team verified boards.

The process had three separate verification steps, and engineers were doing all three by hand with different software. Schematic vs EBOM: does the bill of materials match the schematic? Document-to-document. PCB vs Layout: does the physical board match its design? Image-to-document. Good Board vs New Board: does a new board match a known-good reference? Image-to-image. Different alignment needs, different failure modes — and the BOM step alone was 20–30 minutes of cross-referencing two documents per board. I was the only engineer assigned to the suite.

My task was to build all three tools as one suite: reliable per tool, usable by non-technical engineers, maintainable by a single developer, and deployable standalone on locked-down floor machines.

The design that made that possible was to share the infrastructure and specialize the logic. All three needed the same skeleton — file upload and validation, image preprocessing, auto-save, report generation, UI components — so I built that once. The comparison engines stayed separate: entity matching for the BOM, homography-based alignment for the board-vs-layout images, a multi-signal pipeline for the board-vs-board compare. I considered one universal comparison engine and rejected it: the input formats and alignment algorithms are fundamentally different, and the abstraction layer would have added complexity without adding value. The cost I accepted was deliberate duplication in exchange for clear separation of concerns. The UX was identical across all three — same drag-and-drop, green for match, red for mismatch, blue for unknown — so learning one tool meant learning all three, and when I improved the auto-save, all three improved together.

I prioritized the build by impact: the BOM tool first, because it was the biggest time sink, then the two image tools, each standing on the infrastructure the previous one had paid for. I learned OpenCV.js while building the alignment-heavy PCB-vs-Layout tool — the steepest learning curve of the three — and the board-vs-board tool ran its in-browser inference on TensorFlow.js. All three were delivered inside the eight-week window I worked to.

The part I'd point to as ownership is what happened after delivery. When the engineers didn't trust the BOM verdicts, I didn't argue with them — I built the debug view so they could audit every decision: the raw BOM text, each preprocessing step, the layout reference found, the result, and the rule that produced it, plus a decision tree and an aggregate dashboard. In its first week, the engineers used it to find two heuristics I had gotten wrong. That auditability is what made them willing to rely on the tool for production verification — the shift from delivering a tool to owning the outcome.

I want to be precise about the outcome, because parts of it I measured and parts I didn't. Measured: a 20–30-minute manual reconciliation became a report in seconds, and the hardened tool caught three real mismatches in early production use — after the edge cases had broken and been fixed — that manual review had missed. Not measured: adoption and aggregate hours. The suite ran on both sites' lines for production verification; I remember roughly fifteen daily users across the two sites, and the engineers' own estimate of the give-back was around twenty hours a week across the floor. That's recollection and their arithmetic on the per-board baselines — I never instrumented it, so I won't dress it up as a tracked stat. The thing I'm proudest of doesn't depend on those numbers: it's that users could falsify my tool's reasoning, and did, and it got better.

If I did it again, I'd build the shared infrastructure before the first tool instead of retrofitting it, and I'd collect sample BOMs from the floor before writing the parser. Same lesson both times: get the real artifacts and the real users in front of me earlier — and measure what you intend to claim.

## Deep follow-ups (likely bar-raiser probes)

**Q: What was the hardest part of owning all three tools?**
**A:** Balancing shared infrastructure against specialized logic. Getting the skeleton right kept the tools consistent and maintainable by one developer; the risk was over-abstracting, so I deliberately kept the comparison engines separate. A universal engine would have needed an abstraction layer over fundamentally different inputs and alignment algorithms — complexity without value. I accepted the duplication cost knowingly.

**Q: How did you deliver three tools in eight weeks on your own?**
**A:** I prioritized by impact: the BOM tool first, because manual BOM checks were the most time-consuming process on the floor, then the two image tools. Each one stood on the infrastructure the previous had paid for, and I learned OpenCV.js on the alignment-heavy second tool rather than waiting to know it.

**Q: What's the evidence it actually mattered?**
**A:** The parts I can stand behind: engineers on both sites used it for production verification; the BOM step went from 20–30 minutes of manual cross-referencing to a seconds-long report; the hardened parser caught three real mismatches that manual review had missed; and the debug view's first week produced two wrong heuristics the engineers themselves found — users auditing me is evidence the tool was in real use. The parts I can't stand behind as measurements: the headcount and the ~20 hours a week. Those are my recollection and the team's estimate, not instrumented numbers, and I'd rather say that than defend a number I can't produce evidence for.

**Q: How did you make it usable by non-developers?**
**A:** Identical UX across all three tools — same drag-and-drop, same color-coded output — so they learned the workflow once. And browser-based by requirement: the suite had to be deployable standalone on machines where installing software wasn't an option, so no install and no IT ticket stood between an engineer and a comparison.

**Q: Which of the three was hardest, and why?**
**A:** PCB vs Layout — learning OpenCV.js and implementing the CAD alignment and parsing side gave it the steepest learning curve. The Good Board tool was the most algorithmically complex, but by then it stood on infrastructure the first two had paid for.

**Q: What went wrong along the way?**
**A:** Twice, publicly. Early after the BOM tool deployed, engineers reported misses on a meaningful fraction of boards — I chased the root causes systematically rather than patching the boards I'd happened to look at, and each fix went into a regression suite of representative production BOMs. And the results initially weren't trusted at all, which is what forced the debug view — that one turned out to be the feature the tool needed most.

**Q: What did the architecture cost you?**
**A:** Real costs: deliberate duplication between tools for clearer separation of concerns; the three tools ended up with slightly different error-handling patterns because they were built at different times; and the parser is tightly coupled to SAP export formats, which I accepted because the constraint was real and immediate. Those are the debts I'd name unprompted.

**Q: What would you do differently?**
**A:** Build the shared infrastructure before the first tool instead of retrofitting it, and collect representative BOMs from the floor before writing the parser. Both are the same lesson: get the real artifacts and real users in front of me before I build. The third lesson is newer: if you're going to claim an outcome, measure it — or say it's an estimate, like I just did.
